# Chapter 31. Quantum Sensing, Metrology, and Tomography

> **Status:** prereviewed · **Phase:** 5 · **Sections drafted:** 6 / 6

[← Previous: Chapter 30](30-quantum-machine-learning.md) · [Table of Contents](../../README.md) · [Next: Chapter 32 →](../part-12-adjacent-models/32-adjacent-computational-models.md)

Quantum *computation* is still working its way through the noisy-intermediate-scale era; quantum *sensing* and *metrology* are already in production. Optical lattice clocks measure time at a fractional uncertainty around $10^{-19}$, about three orders of magnitude better than the caesium standard that defines the SI second. Nitrogen-vacancy magnetometers do nanoscale NMR of single biomolecules and image currents inside integrated circuits on a benchtop. SQUID (superconducting quantum interference device — §31.2) arrays read out brain activity in clinical magnetoencephalography rooms. LIGO uses squeezed vacuum — light whose quantum noise has been redistributed so one measured quadrature fluctuates less than vacuum, at the expense of its partner — to extend its gravitational-wave sensitivity past the shot-noise floor at frequencies where it matters. None of these systems waits for a fault-tolerant logical qubit. They run today, in commercial and scientific deployments, on the strength of three ideas: that quantum states encode small parameters more efficiently than classical ones (§31.1–31.2), that entanglement can beat the $1/\sqrt{N}$ averaging law (§31.2), and that the same measurement-theory machinery of Chapter 11 doubles as the characterisation toolkit for everything the rest of this book has built (§31.4–31.5).

> **How to read this chapter.** §31.1 lays the metrology fundamentals: the Cramér–Rao bound, the quantum Fisher information, the standard quantum limit, and the Heisenberg limit. §31.2 surveys the four production sensing platforms — atomic clocks, magnetometers, gravimeters, and gravitational-wave detectors — and is the densest with concrete numbers. §31.3 covers the imaging side (ghost imaging, sub-shot-noise, quantum illumination). §31.4 lifts the state and process tomography of §§11.4–11.5 into the device-characterisation setting. §31.5 turns the same machinery outward into certification and verification. §31.6 is the bridge to adjacent computational models and the chapter's sanity checks. Readers who only want the deployed-today story can read §§31.1–31.2 and skim §31.6; readers building tomography pipelines should focus on §§31.4–31.5.

## 31.1 Quantum Metrology Fundamentals

A **metrological** task asks: given access to a quantum probe whose state depends on an unknown classical parameter $\varphi$ — a phase, a magnetic field, a frequency offset, a displacement — how precisely can $\varphi$ be estimated from $N$ uses of the probe? The classical answer is the **Cramér–Rao bound**: for any unbiased estimator $\hat\varphi$ built from $N$ samples of a probability distribution $p(x \mid \varphi)$, the variance obeys

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\hat\varphi) \ge \frac{1}{N\\, F(\varphi)}, \qquad F(\varphi) = \sum_x p(x \mid \varphi)\\, \bigl(\partial_\varphi \log p(x \mid \varphi)\bigr)^2,
$$

where $F(\varphi)$ is the **classical Fisher information** of the distribution. The bound is tight in the large-$N$ limit, saturated by the maximum-likelihood estimator.

The quantum extension replaces the classical Fisher information with its largest possible value over all valid POVMs (§11.2). For a parameterised family of states $\rho(\varphi)$, the **quantum Fisher information** $F_Q(\varphi)$ is defined via the symmetric logarithmic derivative $L$ that solves $\partial_\varphi \rho = \tfrac{1}{2}(L \rho + \rho L)$, giving $F_Q = \mathrm{tr}(\rho\\, L^2)$. The **quantum Cramér–Rao bound** is

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\hat\varphi) \ge \frac{1}{N\\, F_Q(\varphi)},
$$

and is saturated by the projective measurement onto the eigenbasis of $L$. The quantum Fisher information is *the* figure of merit in metrology: a sensing protocol is optimised by maximising $F_Q$ over input states, evolutions, and measurements. For a pure-state probe $|\psi(\varphi)\rangle = e^{-i \varphi H} |\psi\rangle$ generated by a Hamiltonian $H$, the quantum Fisher information equals $4\\, \mathrm{Var}_\psi(H)$ — four times the variance of the generator on the input state. The recipe is to prepare a state with large variance under the generator coupling to $\varphi$.

**Standard quantum limit.** Take $N$ independent probes, each a single qubit in the equal superposition $|+\rangle = (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$, each evolving under $H = Z/2$. Each probe has $\mathrm{Var}(H) = 1/4$, so each contributes $F_Q = 1$, and $N$ independent probes contribute $N$. The Cramér–Rao bound gives $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\varphi) \ge 1/N$, or a precision scaling

$$
\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{SQL}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}.
$$

This is the **standard quantum limit (SQL)** or shot-noise limit: the canonical $1/\sqrt{N}$ scaling that every classical averaging procedure also obeys. It is the floor you hit without entanglement.

**Heisenberg limit.** Replace the $N$ independent $|+\rangle$ probes with the **GHZ state** $|\mathrm{GHZ}_N\rangle = (|0\rangle^{\otimes N} + |1\rangle^{\otimes N})/\sqrt{2}$, and let it evolve under the collective generator $H = \sum_i Z_i / 2$. The variance of $H$ on $|\mathrm{GHZ}_N\rangle$ is $N^2 / 4$, so $F_Q = N^2$, and

$$
\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{HL}} = \frac{1}{N}.
$$

This is the **Heisenberg limit**: a quadratic improvement over the SQL, achieved by encoding $\varphi$ coherently into all $N$ probes through entanglement. The optical analogue is the **NOON state** $(|N, 0\rangle + |0, N\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$ — $N$ photons in either of two interferometer arms — which sees the relative phase $N$ times per photon and achieves the same $1/N$ scaling. The price of Heisenberg scaling is fragility: GHZ and NOON states decohere $N$-fold faster than product states, and in the presence of generic Markovian noise the asymptotic advantage often collapses back to a constant-factor improvement on top of the SQL. Designing noise-robust entangled probes (squeezed states, spin-squeezed atomic ensembles, error-corrected sensing) is the active frontier.

The metrological framework explains why sensing has been the first deployed quantum application. Unlike Shor's algorithm, which needs error-corrected logical qubits, an atomic-clock or magnetometer protocol needs a *single* coherent quantum probe and a calibrated readout. The precision advantage shows up immediately, on physical qubits, in laboratory hardware that already exists. The next section walks through the four platforms where this advantage has reached production.

## 31.2 Deployed Sensing Platforms

**Atomic clocks.** A clock is a frequency reference. The state of the art is the **optical lattice clock**: $10^3$–$10^5$ neutral strontium-87 or ytterbium-171 atoms trapped in a magic-wavelength optical lattice and interrogated on a narrow optical transition near $429$ THz (Sr) or $518$ THz (Yb). The Ramsey-fringe protocol on $N$ uncorrelated atoms gives an Allan deviation scaling as $(N\\, T)^{-1/2}$ where $T$ is the interrogation time, putting the SQL well into the $10^{-18}$ fractional-frequency regime. Current laboratory deployments at NIST, JILA, RIKEN, PTB, and SYRTE report fractional uncertainties at or below $10^{-18}$, with the leading systems pushing into the $10^{-19}$ decade — three orders of magnitude beyond the caesium primary standard that defines the SI second. **Quantum logic clocks** use a single trapped ion (Al$^+$, Yb$^+$, Lu$^+$) sympathetically cooled and read out via a coupled logic ion; the Al$^+$ system at NIST has reported systematic uncertainties at $9.4 \times 10^{-19}$. **Entanglement-enhanced clocks** spin-squeeze the atomic ensemble through QND measurements or cavity-mediated interactions, beating the SQL by a few decibels in the squeezed direction — a present-day Heisenberg-scaling demonstration is around $N = 100$ atoms in published work, with the squeezing advantage modest but real and growing.

**Magnetometers.** Three platforms own the field, each tuned to a different regime.

The **nitrogen-vacancy (NV) centre** in diamond is a point defect with a spin-triplet ground state ($S = 1$) whose Zeeman splitting is read out optically through the spin-dependent fluorescence of the $^3$E excited state. NV magnetometry runs at room temperature, with nanometre-scale spatial resolution (the sensor *is* the atomic defect), and sensitivities reaching $\sim \mathrm{pT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ for ensembles and $\sim \mathrm{nT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ for single NV scanning probes. Production applications: nanoscale NMR of single biomolecules, mapping of magnetic textures in condensed-matter samples, current imaging in integrated circuits.

The **SQUID** (superconducting quantum interference device) is an interferometer of two Josephson junctions in a superconducting loop. Flux through the loop modulates the critical current with a period of one flux quantum $\Phi_0 = h/(2e) \approx 2.07 \times 10^{-15}\\, \mathrm{Wb}$, and modern devices reach $\sim \mathrm{fT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ flux noise. The price is cryogenics — low-$T_c$ SQUIDs run at liquid-helium temperature (around $4\\,\mathrm{K}$), supplied by liquid helium or a closed-cycle cryocooler, not the millikelvin dilution refrigeration that quantum processors require. Production applications: **magnetoencephalography (MEG)** arrays with 200–300 SQUID channels measuring the femtotesla magnetic signature of neural currents through the skull; geomagnetic survey; non-destructive evaluation of metals.

**Atomic vapour cell magnetometers** (spin-exchange-relaxation-free, or **SERF**) use a hot alkali vapour (Rb, K, Cs) in which optically-pumped spins precess in the field to be measured. Sensitivities reach below $\mathrm{fT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ without cryogenics, and the cells can be made small enough to wear on a head — **optically-pumped magnetometer (OPM) MEG** systems from Cerca, FieldLine, and QuSpin are replacing SQUID arrays for paediatric and motion-tolerant clinical neuroimaging.

**Gravimeters and accelerometers.** Cold-atom **matter-wave interferometers** drop a cloud of ultracold rubidium or caesium atoms, split the wavepacket via stimulated Raman transitions ($\pi/2$–$\pi$–$\pi/2$ pulse sequence), let the two arms accumulate a phase difference under gravity, and recombine. The phase difference is $k_{\mathrm{eff}} g T^2$ for a single-loop interferometer with wavevector $k_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and free-fall time $T$; readout is by population imaging. Commercial absolute gravimeters (Muquans/iXBlue, AOSense) reach $\sim 1$ $\mu$Gal sensitivity ($\approx 10^{-9}\\,g$) in field deployments. Applications: geodesy, volcano monitoring, mineral and water-table surveys, fundamental tests of the equivalence principle, and inertial navigation in environments where GPS is unavailable or denied. Multi-axis cold-atom accelerometers under development for submarine and aircraft navigation aim at long-duration drift below conventional ring-laser-gyro stacks.

**Gravitational-wave detection.** LIGO and Virgo are kilometre-scale Michelson interferometers whose mirrors are kept in free-fall in the relevant frequency band. The shot-noise limit on the optical readout sets the high-frequency sensitivity floor. Both observatories now inject **frequency-dependent squeezed vacuum** into the dark port: a squeezed state from a non-linear optical parametric oscillator passes through a 300-metre filter cavity that rotates the squeezing angle so the noise is reduced in the *phase* quadrature at high frequencies (helping shot-noise-limited sensitivity) and in the *amplitude* quadrature at low frequencies (helping radiation-pressure-limited sensitivity). The deployed squeezing level is around $3$–$6$ dB, equivalent to a $25$–$50\%$ increase in detected-event rate without any change to the rest of the instrument. The Heisenberg-limit interpretation is operational: the dual-recycled interferometer's effective photon number $N$ is enormous, and the squeezed vacuum redistributes the metrological resource to where it is needed.

## 31.3 Quantum Imaging

Quantum sensing extends beyond single-parameter estimation to spatially-resolved imaging, where photon entanglement and squeezed light can break classical floors on signal-to-noise, depth-of-field, and detection probability.

**Ghost imaging** illuminates an object with one half of an entangled photon pair while detecting the *correlated* partner photon on a bucket detector and the *unilluminated* partner on a pixel array; the image is reconstructed from the coincidence statistics. The quantum version (using spontaneous parametric down-conversion sources) has been replicated with thermal-light correlations and even classical computational variants, leaving the strict quantum advantage debatable in most laboratory regimes but the technique itself has matured into a real tool for imaging in low-light and through-scattering-medium settings.

**Sub-shot-noise imaging** uses spatially-multimode squeezed light to reduce the per-pixel photon-counting variance below $\sqrt{N}$ across a CCD or sCMOS sensor. Demonstrations have reached a few decibels of squeezing across megapixel images, enabling biological imaging at illumination levels where photodamage would otherwise dominate.

**Quantum illumination** (Lloyd, Tan et al.) addresses target detection in a noisy background: split an entangled photon pair, send the *signal* photon toward a putative target, retain the *idler*, and perform a joint measurement on the returned signal and the retained idler. Even when the entanglement is completely destroyed by environmental noise — both arms see thermal photons swamping the returned signal — the joint measurement still extracts a $6$ dB advantage in the error exponent compared to the best classical illumination strategy of equal mean photon number. The catch is the joint measurement, which requires a quantum memory for the idler and an entangling readout; microwave-domain demonstrations relevant to radar are an active research direction at MIT Lincoln Lab and elsewhere.

These imaging modalities are the least deployed of the sensing applications — none has a commercial product line analogous to commercial atomic clocks or NV magnetometers — but they are the most active research front for *photonic* quantum advantage in measurement, and they share an algorithmic core with the photonic-computing models of Chapter 32.

## 31.4 State and Process Tomography in Practice

Chapter 11 introduced state and process tomography as the operational primitive that turns an unknown $\rho$ or $\Lambda$ into a fully-characterised mathematical object. This section lifts those primitives into the platform-characterisation setting: how do experimenters use tomography to certify devices, calibrate gates, and diagnose error sources?

**Full state tomography** is the brute-force baseline (§11.4): measure each of the $4^n - 1$ non-trivial Pauli expectation values $\mathrm{tr}(P\\, \rho)$ from independent shot batches, then reconstruct $\rho$ by linear inversion or by maximum-likelihood projection onto the positive semidefinite cone. The $4^n$ shot scaling restricts full tomography to $n \le 6$ in routine use and to $n \le 10$ with aggressive use of structure.

**Classical shadows** (§11.5) lift the qubit ceiling. The Huang–Kueng–Preskill protocol gives sample-efficient estimators for $M$ pre-declared observables with cost $O(\log M / \varepsilon^2)$ times the relevant shadow-norm factor — independent of $n$ for $k$-local observables. In a device-characterisation pipeline, shadows give per-circuit fidelity, single- and two-qubit reduced density matrices, and entanglement witnesses from a single shot batch that no longer scales exponentially with the number of qubits.

**Compressed-sensing tomography** exploits low rank. If the state of interest is approximately pure ($\rho \approx |\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$, rank $1$), or low-rank for any structural reason, the number of Pauli expectation values needed to reconstruct $\rho$ up to error $\varepsilon$ in trace distance is $O(r\\, d \log^2 d)$ where $d = 2^n$ and $r$ is the rank — a polynomial-in-$d$ rather than $d^2$-in-$d$ scaling. The reconstruction is by nuclear-norm minimisation (the matrix analogue of $\ell_1$ minimisation in compressed sensing). For NISQ devices whose target states are *intended* to be low-rank, compressed-sensing tomography is the right tool.

**Process tomography for gate characterisation.** A quantum channel $\Lambda$ on $n$ qubits is described by $4^{2n}$ complex parameters (the Pauli transfer matrix). Standard process tomography prepares each of $4^n$ basis states, sends each through $\Lambda$, performs state tomography on each output, and assembles the result — $16^n$ total shot budget. Two refinements dominate in practice. **Gate set tomography** characterises an entire set of gates plus the state preparation and measurement (SPAM) jointly, treating SPAM as another unknown to be solved for rather than a known calibration; this avoids the circular dependency where naive process tomography blames every error on the gate under test even when the readout is the dominant noise source. **Cycle benchmarking** and **randomised benchmarking** drop process-tomography ambitions entirely and extract a single average gate fidelity per Clifford gate at constant shot cost — the workhorse of device-qualification dashboards on every superconducting and ion-trap platform.

**Direct fidelity estimation** sits between full tomography and benchmarking. To estimate $\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle$ for a target pure state $|\psi\rangle$, sample Pauli operators $P$ from the importance distribution $\Pr(P) \propto \langle\psi|P|\psi\rangle^2$ and estimate $\mathrm{tr}(P\\, \rho)$ for each sampled Pauli. The total shot cost is $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ independent of $n$, at the cost of being target-state-specific.

In production characterisation, the picture is layered: randomised benchmarking gives a per-gate health number that runs every shift; direct fidelity estimation certifies a specific algorithmic state; classical shadows back out a moderate panel of expectation values; full or compressed-sensing tomography is reserved for the deep dives when something has visibly gone wrong.

## 31.5 Certification and Verification

The same tomography toolkit that characterises hardware also underwrites the *verification of quantum computations*. A purely classical verifier — a user who does not trust the quantum hardware — can extract a high-confidence certificate that a quantum computer ran the claimed circuit, using only classical communication with the device. (The term *device-independent* is reserved for the unconditional, Bell-test–based variant below; the single-prover protocol that follows is instead **cryptographically** sound, resting on a computational assumption.)

**Interactive proofs and Mahadev's protocol.** Mahadev (2018) showed that a classical verifier with access to a single quantum prover can verify any BQP computation, assuming the prover cannot break a post-quantum cryptographic primitive (specifically, the learning-with-errors hardness assumption). The verifier holds a trapdoor for an LWE-based commitment scheme; the prover is compelled to perform measurements in bases chosen by the verifier without learning which basis was chosen; the verifier extracts the outcome and verifies a polynomial-time-checkable certificate. This is a *cryptographic* analogue of state tomography — the verifier reconstructs enough of the quantum computation to certify its correctness, without ever holding a quantum state itself.

**Self-testing.** A weaker but unconditional verification primitive: a classical verifier interacting with two non-communicating quantum provers can certify the prover's state and measurements up to local isometries, using only the statistics of measurement outcomes on Bell-inequality games. The CHSH game is the canonical example: a maximal-violation strategy uniquely identifies the shared state as the Bell state $|\Phi^+\rangle$ and the measurements as $X$, $Z$ rotations to $45^\circ$, modulo local unitaries. Self-testing extends to multi-qubit states (graph states, GHZ states) and grounds device-independent quantum key distribution.

**Cross-platform verification.** Two physical devices claimed to implement the same algorithm should produce statistically indistinguishable output distributions. Cross-platform verification compares output samples between two devices (or between a device and a classical simulator within reach) using statistical distance estimators — total variation distance, classical fidelity, or specific moment tests — that can be evaluated from polynomially-many samples for restricted state families. The protocol is now standard in benchmarking superconducting and ion-trap platforms against each other and against tensor-network simulators.

**Industrial applications and outlook.** Beyond the laboratory, sensing and metrology power several near-term industrial deployments worth listing explicitly. Precision navigation by cold-atom inertial measurement in GPS-denied environments — submarines, aircraft over polar regions, missile guidance — is a multi-decade goal that has crossed into prototype hardware. Hyperpolarised-nucleus MRI uses dynamic nuclear polarisation to push the room-temperature nuclear spin polarisation from $\sim 10^{-5}$ to near unity, yielding $10^4$-fold signal-to-noise gains for $^{13}$C, $^{129}$Xe, and $^{15}$N tracers; this is a deployed clinical-research technique at a growing number of medical centres. MEG and EEG benefit from both SQUID and OPM magnetometer arrays in clinical neurology — epilepsy surgery planning, presurgical motor cortex mapping, dementia and Alzheimer's research. Each of these turns a metrology advance into a workflow change at the bench or bedside, on a much shorter horizon than universal quantum computation.

## 31.6 Bridge to Chapter 32

This chapter closed the *applications* part of the book by surveying the one quantum technology whose value has already left the laboratory. Quantum sensing and metrology lean on the same Hilbert-space formalism, the same measurement theory (Chapter 11), and the same noise channels (Chapter 18) as quantum computation — but they require neither error-correcting codes nor logical qubits, and they pay off at the single-coherent-probe level on hardware that already exists. The Cramér–Rao framework gives the precision floor; entanglement (GHZ, NOON, spin-squeezed states) gives a route past the SQL toward the Heisenberg limit; squeezed vacuum, NV centres, SQUIDs, atomic vapour cells, cold-atom interferometers, and quantum-light imaging give the concrete platforms. Tomography and certification close the loop: the operational primitives that characterise sensing devices are the same primitives that will eventually verify computational ones.

Chapter 32 turns to **adjacent computational models** that sit beside the gate-circuit picture this book has taken as default: measurement-based quantum computation, adiabatic and quantum-annealing models, continuous-variable photonic computation, and topological models. Each of these recasts the same Hilbert-space mechanics through a different lens, and each connects back to one or more of the sensing platforms in this chapter — photonic CV computation shares its primitive with squeezed-light interferometry, adiabatic models share their evolutionary picture with atom-interferometer protocols, and measurement-based models lean directly on the tomography and shadow machinery of §§31.4–31.5.
