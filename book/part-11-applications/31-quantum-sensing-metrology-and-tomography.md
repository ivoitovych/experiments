# Chapter 31. Quantum Sensing, Metrology, and Tomography

> **Status:** prereviewed · **Phase:** 5 · **Sections drafted:** 6 / 6

[← Previous: Chapter 30](30-quantum-machine-learning.md) · [Table of Contents](../../README.md) · [Next: Chapter 32 →](../part-12-adjacent-models/32-adjacent-computational-models.md)

Quantum *computation* is still working its way through the noisy-intermediate-scale era; quantum *sensing* and *metrology* are already in production. Optical lattice clocks realize frequency at fractional uncertainties reaching the $10^{-18}$–$10^{-19}$ decade, roughly three orders of magnitude beyond the caesium standard that defines the SI second. Nitrogen-vacancy magnetometers do nanoscale NMR down to single-biomolecule scales in demonstration experiments and image currents inside integrated circuits on a benchtop. SQUID (superconducting quantum interference device — §31.2) arrays read out brain activity in clinical magnetoencephalography rooms. LIGO uses squeezed vacuum — light whose quantum noise has been redistributed so one measured quadrature fluctuates less than vacuum, at the expense of its partner — to extend its gravitational-wave sensitivity past the shot-noise floor at frequencies where it matters. None of these systems waits for a fault-tolerant logical qubit. They run today, in commercial and scientific deployments, on the strength of three ideas: that quantum states encode small parameters more efficiently than classical ones (§31.1–31.2), that entanglement can beat the $1/\sqrt{N}$ averaging law (§31.2), and that the same measurement-theory machinery of Chapter 11 doubles as the characterization toolkit for everything the rest of this book has built (§31.4–31.5).

> **How to read this chapter.** §31.1 lays the metrology fundamentals: the Cramér–Rao bound, the quantum Fisher information, the standard quantum limit, and the Heisenberg limit. §31.2 surveys the four production sensing platforms — atomic clocks, magnetometers, gravimeters, and gravitational-wave detectors — and is the densest with concrete numbers. §31.3 covers the imaging side (ghost imaging, sub-shot-noise, quantum illumination). §31.4 lifts the state and process tomography of §§11.4–11.5 into the device-characterization setting. §31.5 turns the same machinery outward into certification and verification. §31.6 is the bridge to adjacent computational models and the chapter's sanity checks. Readers who only want the deployed-today story can read §§31.1–31.2 and skim §31.6; readers building tomography pipelines should focus on §§31.4–31.5.

## 31.1 Quantum Metrology Fundamentals

A **metrological** task asks: given access to a quantum probe whose state depends on an unknown classical parameter $\varphi$ — a phase, a magnetic field, a frequency offset, a displacement — how precisely can $\varphi$ be estimated from $N$ uses of the probe? The classical answer is the **Cramér–Rao bound**: for any unbiased estimator $\hat\varphi$ built from $N$ samples of a probability distribution $p(x \mid \varphi)$, the variance obeys

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\hat\varphi) \ge \frac{1}{N\\, F(\varphi)}, \qquad F(\varphi) = \sum_x p(x \mid \varphi)\\, \bigl(\partial_\varphi \log p(x \mid \varphi)\bigr)^2,
$$

where $F(\varphi)$ is the **classical Fisher information** of the distribution. The bound is tight in the large-$N$ limit, saturated by the maximum-likelihood estimator.

The quantum extension replaces the classical Fisher information with its largest possible value over all valid POVMs (§11.2). For a parameterized family of states $\rho(\varphi)$, the **quantum Fisher information** $F_Q(\varphi)$ is defined via the symmetric logarithmic derivative $L$ that solves $\partial_\varphi \rho = \tfrac{1}{2}(L \rho + \rho L)$, giving $F_Q = \mathrm{tr}(\rho\\, L^2)$. The **quantum Cramér–Rao bound** is

$$
\mathrm{Var}(\hat\varphi) \ge \frac{1}{N\\, F_Q(\varphi)},
$$

and is saturated by the projective measurement onto the eigenbasis of $L$. The quantum Fisher information is the central figure of merit for local single-parameter estimation (Bayesian, global, and multiparameter settings carry their own machinery): a sensing protocol is optimized by maximizing $F_Q$ over input states and evolutions — the optimization over measurements is already inside the QFI's definition. For a pure-state probe $|\psi(\varphi)\rangle = e^{-i \varphi H} |\psi\rangle$ generated by a Hamiltonian $H$, the quantum Fisher information equals $4\\, \mathrm{Var}_\psi(H)$ — four times the variance of the generator on the input state. The recipe is to prepare a state with large variance under the generator coupling to $\varphi$.

**Standard quantum limit.** Take $N$ independent probes, each a single qubit in the equal superposition $|+\rangle = (|0\rangle + |1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$, each evolving under $H = Z/2$. Each probe has $\mathrm{Var}(H) = 1/4$, so each contributes $F_Q = 1$, and $N$ independent probes contribute $N$. The Cramér–Rao bound gives $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\varphi) \ge 1/N$, or a precision scaling

$$
\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{SQL}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}.
$$

This is the **standard quantum limit (SQL)** or shot-noise limit: the canonical $1/\sqrt{N}$ scaling that every classical averaging procedure also obeys. It is the floor you hit without entanglement.

**Heisenberg limit.** Replace the $N$ independent $|+\rangle$ probes with the **GHZ state** $|\mathrm{GHZ}_N\rangle = (|0\rangle^{\otimes N} + |1\rangle^{\otimes N})/\sqrt{2}$, and let it evolve under the collective generator $H = \sum_i Z_i / 2$. The variance of $H$ on $|\mathrm{GHZ}_N\rangle$ is $N^2 / 4$, so $F_Q = N^2$ — and here the single GHZ state already contains all $N$ probes, so the bound per preparation is $\mathrm{Var}(\hat\varphi) \ge 1/F_Q = 1/N^2$ (do not also divide by $N$ as in the independent-probe formula), giving

$$
\Delta\varphi_{\mathrm{HL}} = \frac{1}{N}.
$$

This is the **Heisenberg limit**: a quadratic improvement in *variance* over the SQL ($1/N^2$ versus $1/N$; the standard deviation improves by $\sqrt{N}$), achieved by encoding $\varphi$ coherently into all $N$ probes through entanglement. The optical analog is the **NOON state** $(|N, 0\rangle + |0, N\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$ — $N$ photons in either of two interferometer arms — which sees the relative phase $N$ times per photon and achieves the same $1/N$ scaling. The price of Heisenberg scaling is fragility: GHZ and NOON states decohere $N$-fold faster than product states, and in the presence of generic Markovian noise the asymptotic advantage often collapses back to a constant-factor improvement on top of the SQL. Designing noise-robust entangled probes (squeezed states, spin-squeezed atomic ensembles, error-corrected sensing) is the active frontier.

The metrological framework explains why sensing has been the first deployed quantum application. Unlike Shor's algorithm, which needs error-corrected logical qubits, an atomic-clock or magnetometer protocol needs a *single* coherent quantum probe and a calibrated readout. The precision advantage shows up immediately, on physical qubits, in laboratory hardware that already exists. The next section walks through the four platforms where this advantage has reached production.

## 31.2 Deployed Sensing Platforms

**Atomic clocks.** A clock is a frequency reference. The state of the art is the **optical lattice clock**: $10^3$–$10^5$ neutral strontium-87 or ytterbium-171 atoms trapped in a magic-wavelength optical lattice and interrogated on a narrow optical transition near $429$ THz (Sr) or $518$ THz (Yb). The Ramsey-fringe protocol on $N$ uncorrelated atoms gives quantum-projection-noise-limited instability improving as $1/\sqrt{N}$ in atom number and $1/\sqrt{\tau}$ in averaging time (the full Allan-deviation expression also carries the transition frequency, Ramsey time, contrast, and dead time), with projection-noise floors in the $10^{-18}$ regime for realistic parameters — total clock performance also hinges on the local oscillator and systematics. Laboratory systems at NIST, JILA, RIKEN, PTB, and SYRTE report systematic fractional uncertainties at or below $10^{-18}$, with leading evaluations in the $10^{-19}$ decade — noting that systematic uncertainty, instability at a given averaging time, and demonstrated clock-comparison accuracy are three distinct figures. **Quantum logic clocks** use a single trapped ion (Al$^+$, Yb$^+$, Lu$^+$) sympathetically cooled and read out via a coupled logic ion; the Al$^+$ system at NIST has reported systematic uncertainties at $9.4 \times 10^{-19}$. **Entanglement-enhanced clocks** spin-squeeze the atomic ensemble through QND measurements or cavity-mediated interactions, beating the SQL by a few decibels in the squeezed direction — below-SQL demonstrations rather than full Heisenberg scaling, with the advantage modest but real and growing.

**Magnetometers.** Three platforms own the field, each tuned to a different regime.

The **nitrogen-vacancy (NV) center** in diamond is a point defect with a spin-triplet ground state ($S = 1$) whose Zeeman splitting is read out optically through the spin-dependent fluorescence of the $^3$E excited state. NV magnetometry runs at room temperature, with nanometer-scale spatial resolution (the sensor *is* the atomic defect), and sensitivities reaching $\sim \mathrm{pT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ for ensembles and $\sim \mathrm{nT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ for single NV scanning probes. Production applications: nanoscale NMR of single biomolecules, mapping of magnetic textures in condensed-matter samples, current imaging in integrated circuits.

The **SQUID** (superconducting quantum interference device) is, in its standard DC form, a superconducting loop interrupted by two Josephson junctions. Flux through the loop modulates the critical current with a period of one flux quantum $\Phi_0 = h/(2e) \approx 2.07 \times 10^{-15}\\, \mathrm{Wb}$; modern devices reach flux noise at the $\mu\Phi_0/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ scale, which the pickup-coil geometry converts into magnetic-field sensitivities of order $\mathrm{fT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$. The price is cryogenics — low-$T_c$ SQUIDs run at liquid-helium temperature (around $4\\,\mathrm{K}$), supplied by liquid helium or a closed-cycle cryocooler, not the millikelvin dilution refrigeration that quantum processors require. Production applications: **magnetoencephalography (MEG)** arrays with 200–300 SQUID channels measuring the femtotesla magnetic signature of neural currents through the skull; geomagnetic survey; non-destructive evaluation of metals.

**Atomic vapour cell magnetometers** (spin-exchange-relaxation-free, or **SERF**) use a hot alkali vapour (Rb, K, Cs) in which optically-pumped spins precess in the field to be measured. Sensitivities reach below $\mathrm{fT}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ without cryogenics, and the cells can be made small enough to wear on a head — **optically-pumped magnetometer (OPM) MEG** systems from Cerca, FieldLine, and QuSpin are replacing SQUID arrays for paediatric and motion-tolerant clinical neuroimaging.

**Gravimeters and accelerometers.** Cold-atom **matter-wave interferometers** drop a cloud of ultracold rubidium or caesium atoms, split the wavepacket via stimulated Raman transitions ($\pi/2$–$\pi$–$\pi/2$ pulse sequence), let the two arms accumulate a phase difference under gravity, and recombine. The phase difference is $k_{\mathrm{eff}} g T^2$ for a single-loop interferometer with wavevector $k_{\mathrm{eff}}$ and free-fall time $T$; readout is by population imaging. Commercial absolute gravimeters (Muquans/iXBlue, AOSense) reach $\sim 1$ $\mu$Gal sensitivity ($\approx 10^{-9}\\,g$) in field deployments. Applications: geodesy, volcano monitoring, mineral and water-table surveys, fundamental tests of the equivalence principle, and inertial navigation in environments where GPS is unavailable or denied. Multi-axis cold-atom accelerometers under development for submarine and aircraft navigation aim at long-duration drift below conventional ring-laser-gyro stacks.

**Gravitational-wave detection.** LIGO and Virgo are kilometer-scale Michelson interferometers whose mirrors are kept in free-fall in the relevant frequency band. The shot-noise limit on the optical readout sets the high-frequency sensitivity floor. Both observatories now inject **frequency-dependent squeezed vacuum** into the dark port: a squeezed state from a non-linear optical parametric oscillator passes through a 300-meter filter cavity that rotates the squeezing angle so the noise is reduced in the *phase* quadrature at high frequencies (helping shot-noise-limited sensitivity) and in the *amplitude* quadrature at low frequencies (helping radiation-pressure-limited sensitivity). The deployed squeezing level is around $3$–$6$ dB in the squeezed band; because squeezing improves only part of the sensitivity band, the observatories report event-rate gains of tens of percent — far less than a naive broadband SNR-cubed conversion would suggest (sanity check 5 explores the gap) — without any change to the rest of the instrument. The Heisenberg-limit interpretation is operational: the dual-recycled interferometer's effective photon number $N$ is enormous, and the squeezed vacuum redistributes the metrological resource to where it is needed.

## 31.3 Quantum Imaging

Quantum sensing extends beyond single-parameter estimation to spatially-resolved imaging, where photon entanglement and squeezed light can break classical floors on signal-to-noise, depth-of-field, and detection probability.

**Ghost imaging** illuminates an object with one half of an entangled photon pair while detecting the *correlated* partner photon on a bucket detector and the *unilluminated* partner on a pixel array; the image is reconstructed from the coincidence statistics. The quantum version (using spontaneous parametric down-conversion sources) has been replicated with thermal-light correlations and even classical computational variants, leaving the strict quantum advantage debatable in most laboratory regimes but the technique itself has matured into a real tool for imaging in low-light and through-scattering-medium settings.

**Sub-shot-noise imaging** uses spatially-multimode squeezed light to reduce the per-pixel photon-counting variance below $\sqrt{N}$ across a CCD or sCMOS sensor. Demonstrations have reached a few decibels of squeezing across megapixel images, enabling biological imaging at illumination levels where photodamage would otherwise dominate.

**Quantum illumination** (Lloyd, Tan et al.) addresses target detection in a noisy background: split an entangled photon pair, send the *signal* photon toward a putative target, retain the *idler*, and perform a joint measurement on the returned signal and the retained idler. Even when the entanglement is completely destroyed by environmental noise — the *returned signal* is swamped by thermal photons while the retained idler stays clean — the joint measurement still extracts up to a $6$ dB advantage in the error exponent over the best classical strategy of equal mean photon number (the ideal quantum-Chernoff-bound result, under the theorem's assumptions; residual phase-sensitive cross-correlations survive the entanglement's death). The catch is the joint measurement, which requires a quantum memory for the idler and an entangling readout; microwave-domain demonstrations relevant to radar are an active research direction at MIT Lincoln Lab and elsewhere.

These imaging modalities are the least deployed of the sensing applications — none has a commercial product line analogous to commercial atomic clocks or NV magnetometers — but they are the most active research front for *photonic* quantum advantage in measurement, and they share an algorithmic core with the photonic-computing models of Chapter 32.

## 31.4 State and Process Tomography in Practice

Chapter 11 introduced state and process tomography as the operational primitive that turns an unknown $\rho$ or $\Lambda$ into a fully-characterized mathematical object. This section lifts those primitives into the platform-characterization setting: how do experimenters use tomography to certify devices, calibrate gates, and diagnose error sources?

**Full state tomography** is the brute-force baseline (§11.4): measure each of the $4^n - 1$ non-trivial Pauli expectation values $\mathrm{tr}(P\\, \rho)$ from independent shot batches, then reconstruct $\rho$ by linear inversion or by maximum-likelihood projection onto the positive semidefinite cone. The $4^n$ shot scaling restricts full tomography to $n \le 6$ in routine use and to $n \le 10$ with aggressive use of structure.

**Classical shadows** (§11.5) lift the qubit ceiling. The Huang–Kueng–Preskill protocol gives sample-efficient estimators for $M$ pre-declared observables with cost $O(\log M / \varepsilon^2)$ times the relevant shadow-norm factor — independent of $n$ for $k$-local observables. In a device-characterization pipeline, shadows give per-circuit fidelity, single- and two-qubit reduced density matrices, and entanglement witnesses from a single shot batch that no longer scales exponentially with the number of qubits.

**Compressed-sensing tomography** exploits low rank. If the state of interest is approximately pure ($\rho \approx |\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$, rank $1$), or low-rank for any structural reason, the number of measurement *settings* needed is $O(r\\, d \log^2 d)$ where $d = 2^n$ and $r$ is the rank — near-linear rather than quadratic in the Hilbert dimension, though still exponential in qubit count, and the shot cost for target error $\varepsilon$ multiplies on top. The reconstruction fits the incomplete noisy data with trace/nuclear-norm-regularized estimators (the matrix analog of $\ell_1$ minimization). For NISQ devices whose target states are *intended* to be low-rank, compressed sensing is one candidate tool — with the caveat that the prepared state need not be low-rank precisely when something has gone wrong, which is when you reach for tomography.

**Process tomography for gate characterization.** A quantum channel $\Lambda$ on $n$ qubits is described by a $4^n \times 4^n$ Pauli transfer matrix — $16^n$ real entries before trace-preservation and complete-positivity constraints cut them down. Standard process tomography prepares an informationally complete set of $\sim 4^n$ input states, sends each through $\Lambda$, and performs state tomography on each output — $O(16^n)$ measurement *configurations*, with the shot count for target precision multiplying on top. Two refinements dominate in practice. **Gate set tomography** characterizes an entire set of gates plus the state preparation and measurement (SPAM) jointly, treating SPAM as another unknown to be solved for rather than a known calibration; this avoids the circular dependency where naive process tomography blames every error on the gate under test even when the readout is the dominant noise source. **Cycle benchmarking** and **randomized benchmarking** drop process-tomography ambitions entirely and extract an average error rate over the Clifford set (interleaved variants target one gate) from a handful of sequence lengths, randomizations, and shots — a cost that is modest and roughly independent of the channel's $16^n$ dimension count, though not literally constant. This is the workhorse of device-qualification dashboards across superconducting and ion-trap platforms.

**Direct fidelity estimation** sits between full tomography and benchmarking. To estimate $\langle\psi|\rho|\psi\rangle$ for a target pure state $|\psi\rangle$, sample Pauli operators $P$ from the importance distribution $\Pr(P) \propto \langle\psi|P|\psi\rangle^2$ and estimate $\mathrm{tr}(P\\, \rho)$ for each sampled Pauli. The total shot cost is $O(1/\varepsilon^2)$ independent of $n$, at the cost of being target-state-specific.

In production characterization, the picture is layered: randomized benchmarking gives a per-gate health number that runs every shift; direct fidelity estimation certifies a specific algorithmic state; classical shadows back out a moderate panel of expectation values; full or compressed-sensing tomography is reserved for the deep dives when something has visibly gone wrong.

## 31.5 Certification and Verification

Verification of quantum computations is a related but distinct discipline: where tomography measures, verification must *convince* — with interactive proofs, cryptography, and nonlocality doing work the tomography toolkit does not. A purely classical verifier — a user who does not trust the quantum hardware — can extract a high-confidence certificate that a quantum computer ran the claimed circuit, using only classical communication with the device. (The term *device-independent* is reserved for the unconditional, Bell-test–based variant below; the single-prover protocol that follows is instead **cryptographically** sound, resting on a computational assumption.)

**Interactive proofs and Mahadev's protocol.** Mahadev (2018) showed that a classical verifier with access to a single quantum prover can verify any BQP computation, assuming the prover cannot break a post-quantum cryptographic primitive (specifically, the learning-with-errors hardness assumption). The verifier holds a trapdoor for an LWE-based commitment scheme; the prover is compelled to perform measurements in bases chosen by the verifier without learning which basis was chosen; the verifier extracts the outcome and verifies a polynomial-time-checkable certificate. The verifier never reconstructs the state: the cryptography forces the prover into committed measurements whose statistics certify the computation, without the verifier ever holding a quantum state itself.

**Self-testing.** A weaker but unconditional verification primitive: a classical verifier interacting with two non-communicating quantum provers can certify the prover's state and measurements up to local isometries, using only the statistics of measurement outcomes on Bell-inequality games. The CHSH game is the canonical example: a maximal-violation strategy uniquely identifies the shared state as the Bell state $|\Phi^+\rangle$ and the measurements as $X$, $Z$ rotations to $45^\circ$, modulo local unitaries. Self-testing extends to multi-qubit states (graph states, GHZ states) and grounds device-independent quantum key distribution.

**Cross-platform verification.** Two physical devices claimed to implement the same algorithm should produce statistically indistinguishable output distributions. Cross-platform verification compares output samples between two devices (or between a device and a classical simulator within reach) using statistical distance estimators — total variation distance, classical fidelity, or specific moment tests — that can be evaluated from polynomially-many samples for restricted state families. The protocol is now standard in benchmarking superconducting and ion-trap platforms against each other and against tensor-network simulators.

**Industrial applications and outlook.** Beyond the laboratory, sensing and metrology power several near-term industrial deployments worth listing explicitly. Precision navigation by cold-atom inertial measurement in GPS-denied environments — submarines, aircraft over polar regions, missile guidance — is a multi-decade goal that has crossed into prototype hardware. Hyperpolarized-nucleus MRI uses dynamic nuclear polarization to push the room-temperature nuclear spin polarization from $\sim 10^{-5}$ to near unity, yielding $10^4$-fold signal-to-noise gains for $^{13}$C, $^{129}$Xe, and $^{15}$N tracers; this is a deployed clinical-research technique at a growing number of medical centers. MEG benefits from both SQUID and OPM magnetometer arrays in clinical neurology — epilepsy surgery planning, presurgical motor cortex mapping, dementia and Alzheimer's research (EEG, its electrical sibling, needs no magnetometers). Each of these turns a metrology advance into a workflow change at the bench or bedside, on a much shorter horizon than universal quantum computation.

## 31.6 Bridge to Chapter 32

This chapter closed the *applications* part of the book by surveying the one quantum technology whose value has already left the laboratory. Quantum sensing and metrology lean on the same Hilbert-space formalism, the same measurement theory (Chapter 11), and the same noise channels (Chapter 18) as quantum computation — but they require neither error-correcting codes nor logical qubits, and they pay off at the single-coherent-probe level on hardware that already exists. The Cramér–Rao framework gives the precision floor; entanglement (GHZ, NOON, spin-squeezed states) gives a route past the SQL toward the Heisenberg limit; squeezed vacuum, NV centers, SQUIDs, atomic vapour cells, cold-atom interferometers, and quantum-light imaging give the concrete platforms. Tomography and certification close the loop: the operational primitives that characterize sensing devices are the same primitives that will eventually verify computational ones.

Chapter 32 turns to **adjacent computational models** that sit beside the gate-circuit picture this book has taken as default: measurement-based quantum computation, adiabatic and quantum-annealing models, continuous-variable photonic computation, and topological models. Each of these recasts the same Hilbert-space mechanics through a different lens, and each connects back to one or more of the sensing platforms in this chapter — photonic CV computation shares its primitive with squeezed-light interferometry, adiabatic models share their evolutionary picture with atom-interferometer protocols, and measurement-based models lean directly on the tomography and shadow machinery of §§31.4–31.5.

**Sanity checks before moving on.**

1. For a single-qubit probe $|+\rangle$ evolving under $H = Z/2$, verify that the quantum Fisher information equals $1$ and that $N$ independent copies give $F_Q = N$, recovering the standard quantum limit $\Delta\varphi = 1/\sqrt{N}$.
2. For the GHZ state $|\mathrm{GHZ}_N\rangle = (|0\rangle^{\otimes N} + |1\rangle^{\otimes N})/\sqrt{2}$ evolving under the collective generator $H = \sum_i Z_i / 2$, compute $\mathrm{Var}(H)$ and verify $F_Q = N^2$, giving the Heisenberg-limit scaling $\Delta\varphi = 1/N$.
3. A fractional-frequency uncertainty of $10^{-18}$ on an optical transition at $429$ THz corresponds to what absolute frequency uncertainty in hertz? At what averaging time does an Allan deviation of $10^{-16}/\sqrt{\tau}$ (typical for a good optical clock) reach the $10^{-18}$ floor?
4. Standard process tomography on $n$ qubits requires $O(16^n)$ measurement *configurations* under the naive protocol of §31.4, with shots for precision multiplying on top. Randomized benchmarking instead uses sequence lengths × randomizations × shots, roughly independent of that dimension count. For $n = 5$ ($16^5 \approx 10^6$ configurations), specify a representative RB budget (say, 10 lengths × 30 sequences × 500 shots) and compare the two totals — then note what each protocol actually delivers: a full channel description versus one averaged error rate.
5. The squeezed-vacuum injection in LIGO produces approximately $3$–$6$ dB of noise reduction in the shot-noise-limited band. Compute the *idealized* event-rate improvement if that were a broadband power-SNR gain, with detection rate scaling as range$^3$ (mind whether your dB is power or amplitude: 3 dB power ≈ range ×1.41 ≈ rate ×2.8). Compare with the tens-of-percent gains observatories actually report, and explain the gap — squeezing covers only part of the band, and other noise sources and thresholds intervene.
