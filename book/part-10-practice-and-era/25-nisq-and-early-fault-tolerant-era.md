# Chapter 25. NISQ and the Early Fault-Tolerant Era

> **Status:** prereviewed · **Phase:** 5 · **Sections drafted:** 6 / 6

[← Previous: Chapter 24](../part-09-hardware-and-software/24-classical-simulation-of-quantum-systems.md) · [Table of Contents](../../README.md) · [Next: Chapter 26 →](26-practical-access-and-hands-on-work.md)

Parts 5–9 built up the algorithm, error-correction, and hardware machinery roughly as if all of it were available at once. The reality in 2026 is more sober: the algorithms exist on paper, the codes have theory and small hardware demonstrations behind them but no machine-scale fault tolerance, the hardware exists in modest sizes, and the intersection of "runs on real hardware today" and "outperforms the best classical alternative on a problem anyone outside the lab cares about" is still nearly empty. This chapter is the reality check. It introduces the **NISQ** (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum) regime that has defined the field since 2018, calibrates what current devices can and cannot do, walks through the contested history of quantum-advantage claims, and sketches the transition into the **early fault-tolerant** regime that is starting to peek over the horizon. Nothing here is new physics; everything here is engineering, economics, and expectation-setting — and the difference between a useful pilot project and a press-release failure mostly comes down to getting these calibrations right.

> **How to read this chapter.** §25.1 and §25.2 are the load-bearing definitions: every later section refers to circuit-depth budgets, fidelity numbers, and the absence of error correction set here. §25.3 (variational algorithms in practice, including error mitigation) is essential before reading §15.8 (VQE) with any realistic expectations. §25.4 (the advantage saga) is historical context that can be skimmed if you already lived through it. §25.5 (benchmarking) and §25.6 (the early-FT transition) are forward-looking and matter most for capacity-planning and post-quantum-cryptography decisions; §25.6 in particular sets up Chapter 26 (practical access) and Chapter 27 (cryptography migration).

## 25.1 Definition of NISQ

The term **NISQ** was coined by John Preskill in his December 2017 Q2B keynote, published in 2018 as the *Quantum* article "Quantum Computing in the NISQ era and beyond." The definition has three parts, all of which still apply in 2026:

- **Noisy.** Gate operations have non-trivial error rates — typically $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-2}$ per two-qubit gate — and whatever circuit fidelity results is what the physical gates deliver, compounding with depth (multiplying like independent survival probabilities only in the crude stochastic model; coherent and correlated errors compose differently).
- **Intermediate-scale.** Qubit counts are in the range that classical brute-force statevector simulation eventually fails (tens to hundreds of qubits) but that is much smaller than the millions of physical qubits that common surface-code architecture forecasts assign to a fully fault-tolerant machine (other codes and architectures shift that number). Preskill's original sketch was 50–100 qubits; the 2026 ceiling is closer to a few thousand for some platforms.
- **No QEC.** This is the operative constraint. Without error correction, error accumulates with every gate — linearly in the first-order independent model, faster when coherent errors add amplitude-wise — so usable depth is bounded on the order of the inverse gate-error rate, modulated by width, gate density, idles, and the observable required. Surface-code logical qubits exist in labs (§25.6) but the bulk of devices that researchers and customers actually run circuits on are still bare physical qubits.

The combination is what makes NISQ awkward: the qubit counts are large enough that simulation is hard, but the depth budgets are too small to run any algorithm that would have given an asymptotic speedup. The intuition that "more qubits is automatically better" fails in this regime — adding qubits without lengthening the usable circuit only enlarges the system you are not yet able to compute with.

The regime is bracketed by two earlier and later eras: the pre-NISQ "few-qubit demos" era (roughly 1998–2015, when most gate-model demonstrations ran on a handful of qubits — annealers and some special-purpose systems were larger, and counting conventions differ) and the post-NISQ early-FT era (§25.6) where logical qubits, even in small numbers, start to be available. NISQ is the awkward middle.

## 25.2 Constraints of NISQ Machines

A useful number to keep in mind is the **effective depth budget**: how many two-qubit gates one can apply in sequence before the cumulative error swamps the signal. A deliberately crude toy model — independent stochastic errors, every layer packed with $n/2$ two-qubit gates covering all $n$ qubits — estimates the surviving global fidelity of a depth-$d$ circuit at per-gate error $\epsilon$ as

$$
F \;\approx\; (1 - \epsilon)^{n d / 2} \;\approx\; e^{-n d \epsilon / 2}.
$$

The *global* fidelity falls to $e^{-1}$ when $n d \epsilon / 2 \gtrsim 1$ — which is not the same as the output turning random or useless: many workloads need only observables or mitigated statistics that survive longer. With a good 2026 two-qubit error $\epsilon \sim 5 \times 10^{-3}$ (a representative working value; the best pairs do better, §22.3) and $n \sim 100$, this gives a budget of $d \sim 4$ — a handful of full layers of two-qubit gates across the device before global fidelity decays to $e^{-1}$. Per-qubit, the budget is $\sim 1/\epsilon \approx 200$ two-qubit gates in series for a single line of the circuit; the difference between the two numbers is the difference between "any one qubit's worldline can survive a few hundred gates" and "the whole device's joint state can survive only a handful of layers."

Concrete typical parameters across the leading platforms in 2026:

- **Superconducting transmons** (IBM, Google, Rigetti). $\sim 100$ to $\sim 1{,}000$ physical qubits per chip. Single-qubit fidelity around 99.9%, two-qubit fidelity around 99.5%. Gate times of tens to hundreds of nanoseconds. Coherence times ($T_1$, $T_2$) in the $100$–$300\\,\mu s$ range. Nearest-neighbor connectivity on a heavy-hex or grid topology, requiring SWAP routing for non-local interactions.
- **Trapped ions** (IonQ, Quantinuum, Oxford Ionics). $\sim 30$ to $\sim 100$ physical qubits per trap. Single-qubit fidelity around 99.99%, two-qubit fidelity around 99.7–99.9%. Gate times of microseconds to milliseconds — roughly one to four orders of magnitude slower than superconducting, depending on which gates are compared. All-to-all connectivity within a trap; mid-circuit measurement and qubit reuse are mature.
- **Neutral atoms** (QuEra, Pasqal, Atom Computing). $\sim 100$ to $\sim 1{,}000$ atoms in optical tweezers. Two-qubit fidelity reaching 99.5% on best devices. Reconfigurable connectivity by physically rearranging atoms between gate stages — a distinctive capability that trades SWAP chains for motion time, with loss, heating, and geometry constraints as the price rather than a free flattening of routing cost.
- **Photonic** (PsiQuantum, Xanadu). Measurement-based and continuous-variable approaches with different metric profiles; scale is harder to compare directly because the natural unit is the photon mode rather than a stationary qubit.

The fidelity numbers above are meant as representative working-device figures — a mix of medians and best-reported values as flagged ("reaching" marks a record, not a median) — rather than single-best-pair heroics. They also describe **isolated** gates; running gates in parallel across the device typically degrades each somewhat through crosstalk. Quantum Volume folds some of those parallel, contextual degradations in; CLOPS is primarily a throughput figure (see Chapter 22 for the metric definitions).

Two constraints that are easy to forget when reading vendor announcements:

- **Connectivity matters more than qubit count.** A 1{,}000-qubit chip with nearest-neighbor 2D connectivity, on which a hypothetical algorithm needs an all-to-all interaction graph, costs $O(\sqrt{n})$ SWAPs per worst-case non-local gate (a 1D chain would cost $O(n)$). The SWAPs themselves consume the depth budget. Trapped-ion all-to-all and neutral-atom rearrangement pay in different currencies — mode crowding and parallelism limits for ions, motion time and loss for atoms — rather than dodging the cost; superconducting devices pay it in SWAPs.
- **Classical control bandwidth bounds shot rate.** Each circuit execution is a shot; getting statistically useful expectation values needs $10^3$ to $10^6$ shots. With effective per-shot times ranging from tens of microseconds (fast, batched superconducting execution) to milliseconds and beyond once resets, control latencies, and queueing enter, a single VQE optimization can run for hours of wall-clock time on real hardware, often dominated by data movement and orchestration rather than by the quantum part.

The takeaway: NISQ machines are wide but shallow. Algorithms designed for the regime have to be shallow by construction.

## 25.3 Variational Algorithms in Practice

Variational algorithms — VQE, QAOA, and the broader **variational** family (§§15.7–15.9) — were the field's bet that shallow circuits, looped through a classical optimizer, could extract useful information from NISQ devices. The bet has not paid off as cleanly as hoped, but the techniques developed in pursuit of it are part of how anything runs on noisy hardware today.

The basic structure is: prepare a parameterized state $|\psi(\vec\theta)\rangle$ with a shallow circuit, measure an expectation value $\langle \psi(\vec\theta) | H | \psi(\vec\theta) \rangle$ for some problem Hamiltonian $H$, feed the value to a classical optimizer, and iterate. The hope was that *expressibility* of the ansatz and *trainability* of the optimizer would suffice to find useful ground states or combinatorial optima, even with imperfect gates, because the variational principle is in some sense robust: any state the circuit prepares gives a valid upper bound on the ground-state energy.

Three obstacles emerged with experience:

- **Barren plateaus.** For sufficiently expressive, near-random ansätze with global cost functions on $n$ qubits, the landscape goes exponentially flat: the gradient variance scales as $\mathrm{Var}(\partial_\theta) \sim 2^{-n}$. This is a property of specific ansatz/cost/initialization families, not of variational circuits universally — local costs, shallow structured circuits, symmetries, and careful initialization change the scaling. The practical workarounds carry their own caveats: hardware-efficient ansätze can still hit plateaus, UCC-style circuits can be deep, and initializing near a known classical solution can undercut the case for the quantum step. The theoretical guarantees remain weak.
- **Optimizer cost.** Each gradient evaluation costs many circuit executions (parameter-shift rule, §8.13): two shifted expectation values per parameter, each spanning the Hamiltonian's measurement groups. For $p$ parameters, $G$ measurement groups, and shot budget $S$ per expectation value, one gradient step costs on the order of $2 p G S$ shots. With $p \sim 100$, $S \sim 10^4$, and even a handful of groups, a single step runs into the multi-million-shot range; hundreds of steps to converge lands around $10^8$–$10^9$ shots. At kilohertz shot rates that is a day to weeks of wall-clock time per problem instance (adaptive shot allocation and gradient-free optimizers shift the arithmetic).
- **Noise interaction.** Noise biases the estimated expectation values in ways that vary with the circuit, so the optimizer is chasing a moving target. The cost surface seen by the optimizer is not the noiseless cost surface.

The dominant practical mitigation is the family of **error-mitigation** techniques. Unlike error *correction* (Chapters 18–19), which encodes, extracts syndromes, and decodes to suppress logical error at runtime, error mitigation turns a *biased* estimator into a less-biased one — partly by pre-execution circuit modification, partly by post-processing — at the cost of higher variance, and therefore more shots. Chapter 18 (§18.18) gives the full treatment, including symmetry verification and the bias-variance analysis; four techniques prominent in variational practice, in brief (readout mitigation, symmetry verification, and randomized compiling also belong in the toolbox):

- **Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE).** Run the circuit at the device's natural noise level $\lambda$, then at amplified noise $2\lambda$, $3\lambda$ (by inserting identity-equivalent gate pairs or by pulse stretching), and fit a polynomial or exponential to extrapolate to $\lambda = 0$. The Richardson extrapolation form gives a linear-combination estimator; the exponential form fits naturally when the noise is well-modeled as global depolarizing — neither form is generically more robust; both are model-sensitive. Cost: several circuit variants *times* the shots allocated to each, with variance amplified by the extrapolation — often far more than a handful of extra runs in total.
- **Probabilistic error cancellation (PEC).** With a characterized noise model, express the inverse channel as a quasi-probability distribution over implementable operations, sample from it, and combine with signed weights. PEC is unbiased in principle — for an exact, stable noise model — but the sampling overhead grows with the square of the quasi-probability norm, schematically exponential in the total entangling-gate error budget, on top of a substantial characterization cost that drifts with the device. Useful at small total error; where it becomes infeasible depends on per-gate noise and target precision, not on a universal gate count.
- **Clifford data regression (CDR).** Near-Clifford circuits are classically simulable; replace the target circuit with a Clifford-rich variant whose noisy and noiseless expectations can both be computed, fit a calibration $f$ mapping noisy to noiseless, then apply $f$ to the original circuit's noisy estimate. Cheap, model-light, but assumes the calibration generalizes across the small non-Clifford perturbation.
- **Virtual distillation** (Huggins et al.). Run $M$ copies of the same state preparation and use collective measurements to estimate purified quantities like $\mathrm{Tr}(O \rho^M)/\mathrm{Tr}(\rho^M)$, suppressing non-dominant noise contributions — without a noise model. Cost: parallel implementations need $M\times$ the qubits (sequential variants trade time instead) plus the collective-measurement circuitry, which brings noise of its own; coherent errors that rotate the dominant eigenvector are not removed. Useful in regimes where extrapolation-based methods struggle, though there is no universal ordering of the techniques.

All four sit on the same **bias-variance** trade-off: the noisy expectation is a biased estimator of the noiseless one; mitigation reduces the bias at the cost of variance, and variance is what shots buy down. The hidden cost of mitigation is therefore shots — sometimes orders of magnitude more shots than the un-mitigated run. At cloud prices — billed per task, per second, or per shot depending on the provider — this is a real budget line in a research grant.

Where variational algorithms have demonstrably run end-to-end on hardware in 2026: small-molecule chemistry up to roughly 50 spin-orbitals (one-particle basis states, roughly one qubit each under the common encodings — Chapter 28) with active-space reduction (restricting the problem to the chemically active orbitals); Trotterized time evolution on lattices of a few tens of sites; small QAOA instances on $\sim 100$-vertex graphs whose optimal solutions are also obtainable by classical heuristics in seconds. Where they have *not* delivered a clean win against classical baselines: at scale. The 2024–2025 literature includes large many-qubit variational demonstrations paired, in several cases, with classical reproductions of the same observables by tensor-network and related methods at modest cost — matched-task comparisons whose specifics belong in Appendix F in sourced form.

## 25.4 Quantum Supremacy and Quantum Advantage Experiments

The 2019–2025 sequence of quantum-advantage claims is worth knowing as both technical history and as a cautionary tale about how the field communicates results.

**Sycamore, Google, October 2019.** A 53-qubit superconducting processor sampled from the output distribution of a depth-20 random circuit in $\sim 200$ seconds; Google's team estimated the same task would take $\sim 10{,}000$ years on the leading supercomputer of the time. The claim was branded "quantum supremacy." Within weeks, IBM published a classical-simulation strategy using secondary storage that brought the estimate down to $\sim 2.5$ days; subsequent tensor-network and GPU work brought the believed classical cost of matched benchmarks down by orders of magnitude (§24.14) — with the caveat that the follow-up computations differ in samples, fidelities, and hardware, so "equivalent" needs care. The Sycamore experiment was real — a random-circuit sampler with cross-entropy benchmarking score above the classical-simulability frontier *as classical simulation stood at the time* — but the frontier moved.

**Jiuzhang, USTC, December 2020.** A photonic Gaussian Boson Sampling experiment with up to 76 detected photons claimed an advantage of $\sim 10^{14}$ over classical. Subsequent classical algorithms exploiting structure in the Gaussian-Boson-Sampling output distribution and approximate-sampling techniques narrowed the gap considerably; the surviving factor depends on the matched task, accuracy metric, and resources compared — not merely on whose benchmarks one trusts.

**IBM, 2023–2024 utility-scale experiments.** A 127-qubit Eagle processor was used to compute expectation values of an Ising-model time evolution at depths beyond the reach of exact classical statevector simulation. IBM framed the result as "utility" from the outset — the paper's title is "Evidence for the utility of quantum computing before fault tolerance" — while claiming the computation lay beyond brute-force classical methods; within months, multiple classical-simulation teams reproduced the published expectation values using tensor networks (MPS, isometric tensor networks, neural-network quantum states) on commodity GPUs, narrowing what "beyond classical methods" could mean. The paper's own claim was that the device gave accurate results at a scale where brute-force methods fail and then-available general methods struggled; the classical reproductions narrowed the domain of that claim rather than refuting the measurements. (Reading "utility" as consistency-plus-validation is this book's gloss — the authors stated their criterion in their own terms.)

**The pattern.** Each claim has followed a similar arc: a careful quantum experiment with a defensible benchmark; classical simulation specialists, often academic groups with no commercial stake, finding that the chosen benchmark is more classically tractable than the original authors estimated; the headline narrative softens. None of this means the quantum experiments were faked or that the hardware is not impressive — the hardware is impressive — but the *gap* between "this is the largest quantum computation ever run (by some stated metric)" and "this is a computation that *only* a quantum computer can run" has stayed open longer than the field anticipated in 2019.

A more careful framing separates **quantum computational advantage** — the literature's preferred neutral term for what was branded "supremacy": any task, even a useless one, performed faster than the best *known* classical alternative — from **practical (useful) quantum advantage**: a useful task at matched accuracy and accounted cost. The first has been plausibly demonstrated for narrow synthetic benchmarks against the classical algorithms known at the time; the second has, as of 2026, no widely accepted public demonstration on a production workload.

## 25.5 Benchmarking Progress Over Time

How does one measure progress in a field where the headline numbers are contested? Several complementary metrics have settled into common use:

- **Physical qubit count.** Useful but weak as a capability predictor on its own; a 1{,}000-qubit chip with poor connectivity and median two-qubit fidelity 98% is not 10× better than a 100-qubit chip with 99.5% fidelity.
- **Quantum Volume (QV).** IBM's combined measure of width and depth: $2^{d^{\ast}}$ for the largest $d^{\ast}$ at which random $d^{\ast} \times d^{\ast}$ circuits pass the heavy-output test (§22.7). Captures the depth-budget reality of §25.2 in a single number. The milestone record runs to $2^{25}$ (Quantinuum, 2025 — §22.13); most other vendors have stopped reporting QV, less because the metric "saturated" than because its verification cost grows exponentially and other metrics fit their architectures better.
- **Algorithmic Qubits (AQ).** IonQ's metric: the largest problem size $n$ at which a suite of algorithmically structured circuits (QED-C-derived — §22.9) passes its success threshold. More application-anchored than QV, less standardized across vendors.
- **CLOPS** (Circuit Layer Operations Per Second). Throughput metric capturing how fast the device-plus-control-stack actually executes layers, important for total time-to-solution on a variational workload.
- **Logical-qubit lifetime and logical error rate.** Once distance-$d$ codes are running (§25.6), the relevant metrics shift to logical error per syndrome round, per logical gate, and per algorithm — distinct quantities that must not be interchanged — with suppression improving roughly exponentially in $d$ below threshold (the rate is code-, noise-, and decoder-dependent).

Trend lines here are tempting but treacherous: aggregating across vendors and modalities mixes incomparable statistics, and this book maintains no curated dataset to fit. As rough shapes only: physical-qubit counts have grown by tens of percent per year; two-qubit error rates have fallen severalfold on the leading platforms over 2018–2026; QV climbed steeply where it is still reported and stopped being reported elsewhere. Extrapolation is scenario material, not derivation: *if* counts reach the $10^4$ scale with two-qubit error at the $10^{-3}$ level on one usable system around the turn of the decade, that is the regime where small distance-$d$ codes outperform their physical constituents — a milestone to watch for, not a date to bank on.

## 25.6 Transition to Early Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computing

The boundary between "NISQ" and "fault-tolerant" is not sharp. The early-fault-tolerant (early-FT) regime is the awkward middle: devices that can support a small number of logical qubits encoded at modest distance, with logical error rates better than the underlying physical rates but not yet competitive with the $10^{-15}$ logical errors a fully fault-tolerant resource estimate would assume.

**Below-threshold demonstrations.** The surface code's threshold theorem (Chapter 19) promises that when the physical error rate $p$ is below the threshold $p_{\mathrm{th}}$, increasing the code distance $d$ decreases the logical error rate as

$$
p_L \;\sim\; A (p/p_{\mathrm{th}})^{(d+1)/2}.
$$

Demonstrating this scaling experimentally requires running codes of multiple distances on the same hardware and confirming that $p_L$ falls as $d$ grows. The first claims of below-threshold operation came from Google's 2023 distance-3 to distance-5 surface-code experiments, followed in 2024 by distance-7 results showing $p_L$ at distance-7 below $p_L$ at distance-5. Quantinuum's 2024–2025 trapped-ion experiments reported error-suppression scaling on a different code family (code, metric, and postselection details differ, so the results are not directly comparable). As of 2026, below-threshold operation has been reported at small distances on more than one platform — each with its own definitions, which the factcheck layer must carry — and larger-distance demonstrations are anticipated; announced plans are not demonstrations.

**Logical-qubit overhead.** At distance $d$, a rotated surface-code logical qubit consumes $2 d^2 - 1$ physical qubits *including* its syndrome ancillas (Chapter 19). At $d = 7$ that is about 100 physical qubits per logical patch; at $d = 21$, about 900; at the $d \approx 29$ that a $10^{-15}$ logical-error target implies under favorable assumptions (sanity check 2), about 1{,}700. Cryptographically relevant Shor instances need a distance set by the full error budget and architecture, not one universal number (Chapter 27). Routing space, factories, workspace, and dead qubits push the conversion factor between "physical qubit count in vendor announcement" and "logical qubits usable for an algorithm" to roughly $10^2$–$10^3$ and beyond.

**Magic-state distillation.** Most logical operations on a surface code are Clifford, but Clifford alone is not universal (§8.10). Non-Clifford gates — $T$ gates, in the standard scheme — are implemented by consuming **magic states**, prepared by distillation protocols that themselves consume many noisy magic states to produce few high-fidelity ones. Distillation has been demonstrated at small scale in 2024–2025 (Quantinuum and others), and is on the critical path for any application that needs more than a few $T$ gates. Resource estimates for demanding fault-tolerant workloads run from roughly $10^9$ $T$ gates upward — varying by orders of magnitude with the algorithm, precision, and construction — each $T$ requiring a distilled magic state — the magic-state factory is a sizable fraction of the projected physical-qubit budget of any future fault-tolerant machine.

**Projections (treat with appropriate skepticism).** Combining current trend lines with code-distance and magic-state arithmetic:

- One scenario for $\sim 2030$: tens of logical qubits at modest distance — enough for algorithmic demonstrations beyond NISQ reach, though genuinely *useful* chemistry additionally requires QPE-era machinery, state preparation, and precision that beats strong classical methods.
- One scenario for $\sim 2035$–$2040$: thousands of logical qubits sustaining $\sim 10^9$ or more logical operations — the scale at which cryptographically relevant Shor on 2048-bit RSA becomes plausible under common architecture assumptions (Chapter 27 treats the threat model properly).

These are scenarios with wide error bars, not derivations from the trend shapes above.

Projections in this field have missed in both directions: early-2010s forecasts generally did not anticipate the shape of the NISQ decade, and several 2020-era fault-tolerance timelines have already slipped — though any such retrospective is itself selective without a collected forecast dataset. The 2025-vintage projections are the current best guess, no more.

**The honest industry view.** Quantum advantage for production workloads is not here in 2026. For a real industrial user running quantum hardware and getting an answer unavailable from a laptop, an HPC cluster, or a tensor-network simulation, there is no widely accepted public demonstration (proprietary claims are hard to assess). The cases where they get an answer they *might* not be able to get classically in five years, given the trend lines, are real, and there are sound reasons to build internal capability now:

- **Tooling lead time.** Building a team that can write VQE-style code, formulate problems as Hamiltonians, and interpret noisy results is typically a multi-year exercise — team size, background, and partnerships move the timeline. Starting after a hypothetical quantum advantage is announced is starting late.
- **Algorithm-side R&D.** Many of the algorithms that will run on early-FT machines have not been written yet, or have been written assuming idealized hardware. Iterating algorithms against real device behavior is most valuable now, when the gap is widest.
- **Cryptographic migration.** Post-quantum cryptographic migration (Chapter 27) is a 10–15-year exercise that has to start well before any cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists. The migration cost is real and current; the threat is future and uncertain. Acting on the threat now is the rational response to its asymmetry.
- **Workforce.** Quantum-aware developers are scarce and expensive; the supply takes years to grow. Hiring during the early-FT ramp will be more expensive than hiring through it now.

The recommendation pattern, then, for a typical industry team: simulators first (Chapter 24, Chapter 26) for algorithm development; small-scale hardware runs (Chapter 26) for benchmarking, calibration of expectations, and tooling; serious capital investment deferred until the early-FT regime delivers an algorithm with a defensible advantage on the team's actual problem. Plan for the long arc; do not bet the quarter on the short one.

**Sanity checks before moving on.**

1. With per-two-qubit-gate error $\epsilon = 5 \times 10^{-3}$ and $n = 100$ qubits, estimate the depth $d$ at which the toy-model surviving fidelity $e^{-n d \epsilon / 2}$ (about $n/2$ two-qubit gates per full layer) falls to $1/e$. Compare to the per-qubit budget $1/\epsilon$, explain the discrepancy — and say why global fidelity $1/e$ is not the same as useless output.
2. A distance-$d$ surface code with $p / p_{\mathrm{th}} = 0.1$ has logical error $p_L \approx A \\, (0.1)^{(d+1)/2}$. Taking $A = 1$, find the smallest odd $d$ for which $p_L \le 10^{-15}$, and read off the physical-qubit count per logical patch at that distance ($2 d^2 - 1$, syndrome ancillas included). (Watch the boundary: $d = 29$ gives exactly $10^{-15}$.)
3. A VQE problem has $p = 200$ parameters, shot budget $S = 10^4$ per expectation value, and converges in $K = 300$ optimizer steps. Counting two shifted expectation evaluations per parameter and treating the Hamiltonian as a single measurement group, estimate the total shot count and, at a per-shot time of $1\\,\mathrm{ms}$, the wall-clock time. Identify the bottleneck — and name two real-world factors (measurement grouping, batching, adaptive allocation) that move the estimate in either direction.
4. List two reasons the Sycamore (2019) and Jiuzhang (2020) claims of "quantum supremacy" do not directly establish quantum advantage in the §25.4 sense. Identify which of these reasons applies to the IBM utility-scale experiments and which does not.
5. A vendor announces a chip with $1{,}000$ physical qubits, two-qubit fidelity 99.5%, and nearest-neighbor connectivity. Using the rotated-code count of $2d^2 - 1$ physical qubits per patch (syndrome ancillas included) and ignoring routing and workspace overhead, estimate the largest distance-$d$ logical qubit it could support and the number of logical qubits the device could hold simultaneously. What is the dominant constraint — qubit count or fidelity?
