# Chapter 21. Quantum Control and Electronics

> **Status:** prereviewed · **Phase:** 4 · **Sections drafted:** 15 / 15

[← Previous: Chapter 20](20-quantum-hardware-platforms.md) · [Table of Contents](../../README.md) · [Next: Chapter 22 →](22-hardware-engineering-metrics.md)

The gate alphabet of Chapter 8 and the circuit model of Chapter 9 hide a substantial engineering stack. Every $X$, $\sqrt{X}$, CNOT, or ECR you write in OpenQASM is, at the hardware level, a sequence of nanosecond-scale analogue waveforms generated by room-temperature electronics, delivered through coaxial lines that pass through several cryogenic stages, and shaped to drive a specific transition of a specific qubit without exciting its neighbours or its higher levels. This chapter walks down that stack from the abstract unitary to the picojoule of microwave energy that implements it, then back up through calibration, drift, and the software interfaces that let a circuit author reach into the pulse layer when the gate library is not enough.

> **How to read this chapter.** §§21.1–21.3 are the pulse-level core: how a gate becomes a waveform, what shaping is for, and where the abstraction is exposed in user-facing software. §§21.4–21.7 cover the physical control plane — electronics, signal integrity, cryogenics, packaging — and are essential reading if you ever expect to debug a real device or read a vendor's white paper. §§21.8–21.10 cover the closed loops (feedback, mid-circuit conditioning, calibration) that turn an open-loop instrument into a reproducible computer. §§21.11–21.14 zoom out to the system-engineering view that Chapter 22 then quantifies. A reader who only cares about the programming-model interface can skim 21.4–21.7; a reader building a control system should treat the whole chapter as a map of the territory and follow the citations into the vendor documentation.

## 21.1 Pulse Shaping

At the bottom of the stack, every single-qubit gate is a **Rabi oscillation** driven by a resonant control field: operationally, the qubit's Bloch vector traces an arc whose speed is set by the drive amplitude and whose axis is set by the drive phase — the gate *is* that arc. The two-level Hamiltonian in the rotating frame of the drive is

$$
H_{\mathrm{drive}}(t) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\\,\Omega(t)\\,\big(\cos\phi(t)\\,X + \sin\phi(t)\\,Y\big) \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\\,\Delta\\,Z,
$$

where $\Omega(t)$ is the **Rabi frequency** (proportional to the drive amplitude), $\phi(t)$ is the drive phase, and $\Delta$ is the detuning between drive and qubit. A gate is a choice of envelope $\Omega(t)$ and phase $\phi(t)$ such that the time-ordered evolution $U = \mathcal{T}\exp\big(-i \int_0^{t_g} H_{\mathrm{drive}}(t)\\,dt\big)$ equals the target unitary up to a global phase. For a square pulse of constant amplitude $\Omega_0$ on resonance ($\Delta = 0$), the qubit traces a circle on the Bloch sphere at angular velocity $\Omega_0$; an $X_\pi$ rotation needs duration $t_g = \pi/\Omega_0$.

Square pulses are pedagogically clean and operationally bad. Their abrupt edges have broadband Fourier content that excites the next transition of the transmon ($|1\rangle \to |2\rangle$, detuned by the anharmonicity $\alpha \approx -200\\,\mathrm{MHz}$) and couples to spectator qubits. The standard remedy is a smooth envelope — Gaussian, raised-cosine, $\mathrm{sech}$ — truncated and renormalised so that $\int \Omega(t)\\,dt$ still equals the target rotation angle. A typical superconducting $X_\pi$ pulse today is a Gaussian of total duration $20$–$60\\,\mathrm{ns}$ with $\sigma$ around a quarter of the total length.

For trapped ions the story is similar in shape and very different in scale. A single-qubit gate is driven by a laser tuned to a narrow electronic or hyperfine transition; pulse durations range from microseconds (Raman gates on hyperfine clock states) to milliseconds (direct optical transitions). The Hamiltonian still reduces to a driven two-level system in the rotating frame, but the envelope and phase live in the laser's acousto-optic modulator (AOM) rather than in a microwave AWG.

## 21.2 Microwave and RF Control

Across superconducting platforms the qubit transition frequency $\omega_{01}/2\pi$ sits in the $3$–$8\\,\mathrm{GHz}$ band. Generating amplitude-and-phase-shaped pulses at that frequency directly from a DAC is possible but bandwidth-hungry; the conventional path is **IQ upconversion**. A baseband AWG produces an in-phase ($I$) and quadrature ($Q$) pair of sidebanded signals at an intermediate frequency (IF) of a few hundred megahertz; an analogue mixer multiplies them against a local oscillator (LO) at the carrier frequency; the upconverted RF tone arrives at the qubit's drive port as $\Omega(t)\cos(\omega_{LO}t + \phi(t))$. Single-sideband mixers and image-rejection calibration suppress the unwanted mirror image at $\omega_{LO}-\omega_{IF}$.

Microwave engineering quietly governs the rest. The drive line is a **transmission line** with characteristic impedance matched to $50\\,\Omega$ end-to-end; an impedance discontinuity reflects power back toward the source and shows up as a ripple in the calibrated pulse shape. **Isolators** and **circulators** — ferrite-based nonreciprocal three-port devices — protect the qubit from amplifier noise on the readout return path; because they are bulky and lossy at millikelvin temperatures, newer designs increasingly replace them with more compact **nonreciprocal on-chip elements** (Josephson/kinetic-inductance circulators and nonreciprocal parametric amplifiers), often combined with directional couplers for pump injection and tailored attenuation. (A directional coupler is itself reciprocal and does not provide isolation on its own.) The first readout amplifier is typically a **traveling-wave parametric amplifier** (TWPA) at the mixing chamber followed by a **HEMT** (high-electron-mobility transistor) amplifier at the $4\\,\mathrm{K}$ stage, with $40$–$60\\,\mathrm{dB}$ of total gain before the signal reaches room-temperature electronics.

A separate channel handles **flux control** when the device has tunable couplers or tunable qubits: a DC + fast-pulse current driven into an on-chip SQUID loop to modulate inductance. Flux lines are low-frequency (DC to a few hundred megahertz) and route through different cryogenic filtering than the drive lines.

## 21.3 Pulse-Level Programming

The user-facing programming model has caught up with the pulse layer in stages. **OpenQASM 3** added native syntax for `cal` (calibration) blocks, `defcal` (gate-to-pulse definitions), `delay` instructions in real time units, and `extern` declarations that bind into the control system. A `defcal x $0` block in OpenQASM 3 looks like a small DSL describing the waveform, the channel, the frame, and any pre/post phase corrections — the same information the compiler would otherwise generate from the device's calibration database.

In Qiskit, **Qiskit Pulse** exposed the same layer through Python `Schedule` objects: `Play(Gaussian(duration, amp, sigma), DriveChannel(0))` puts a Gaussian onto qubit 0's drive line; `ShiftPhase`, `SetFrequency`, and `Delay` complete the instruction set. (This API was deprecated in Qiskit 1.3 and removed in Qiskit 2.0 (2025) as IBM withdrew pulse-level control from its cloud QPUs; the same abstractions now live in OpenQASM 3 `defcal` and in vendor stacks — see §23.5.) Cirq exposes no public pulse API — Google's pulse-level access is internal — while IQM's **Cocos** stack plays the analogous role in its ecosystem. The common abstractions across these layers are *channels* (a logical waveform sink such as "drive for qubit 3" or "measurement readout for qubit 5"), *frames* (a per-channel phase reference that absorbs $Z$-rotations as software phase tracking — the "virtual $Z$" trick), and *waveforms* (typed samples or parametric envelopes).

The programming-model payoff is access to two capabilities the gate-level abstraction hides. First, **virtual $Z$**: a $Z(\theta)$ rotation can be implemented in zero physical time by advancing the frame phase by $-\theta$ on subsequent drives; almost every modern compiler emits virtual $Z$ for all $Z$-rotations and reserves physical pulses for $X$ and $Y$ family operations. Second, **custom gates**: when the gate library is wrong for the algorithm (a fractional-angle controlled phase, a bespoke parametric two-qubit gate, an echoed cross-resonance pulse with custom phase corrections), pulse-level programming is the only way to express it without paying a compilation overhead.

## 21.4 Control Electronics Architecture

A modern control rack is a tightly coupled hybrid of arbitrary waveform generators (AWGs), digitisers, local oscillators, and an FPGA-based real-time controller. The AWG produces I/Q waveform pairs at a sample rate of $1$–$10\\,\mathrm{GS/s}$ with $14$–$16$ bits of vertical resolution; the **digitiser** (ADC) samples readout return signals at comparable rates and feeds them into an FPGA for real-time demodulation and discrimination. The local oscillators sit alongside as fixed-frequency low-phase-noise sources; the mixers and the LO distribution network live in shielded enclosures to suppress LO leakage.

The orchestration model has shifted in the last decade from "AWG plays a pre-loaded sequence" to "real-time controller executes a small program with branches, loops, and feedback". **Quantum Machines OPX**, **Zurich Instruments SHFQC**, and several in-house systems (IBM's Qiskit Runtime stack, Google's in-house pulse system, Rigetti's Quil-T pulse-level layer in the QCS stack) expose a programming model in which the user writes a procedural sequence of pulses, measurements, classical computations, and conditional branches, and the controller compiles that program to firmware that runs deterministically on the FPGA. The latency from measurement to conditional gate is then a small constant ($100$–$500\\,\mathrm{ns}$) instead of a software round-trip.

The FPGA is doing a great deal of work behind that simple interface: real-time demodulation of the readout signal against a reference, threshold-based discrimination of the IQ point, lookup-table-based waveform generation, per-channel phase tracking, and arbitration between deterministic playback and feedback-driven branches. Custom HDL is rarely written by end users today; the vendor's compiler emits the appropriate gate-ware from the higher-level program, and the user only sees the bitstream when something exotic (a new readout discriminator, a custom feedback rule, a hardware-level dynamical decoupling sequence) is needed.

## 21.5 DAC/ADC Interfaces

Between the digital plane and the analogue plane sits a small zoo of converters whose limits become the qubit's limits. A **DAC** (digital-to-analogue converter) at $2\\,\mathrm{GS/s}$ with $14$-bit resolution has a Nyquist bandwidth of $1\\,\mathrm{GHz}$ and a usable dynamic range of about $84\\,\mathrm{dB}$ — enough headroom to place a $-30\\,\mathrm{dBm}$ drive tone alongside spurs that sit $50\\,\mathrm{dB}$ below. The DAC's intrinsic SFDR (spurious-free dynamic range) and harmonic distortion show up in the qubit as off-resonant drive tones; a high-purity DAC plus careful image-reject mixing keeps stray tones at least $-60\\,\mathrm{dBc}$ from the carrier.

On the readout side the ADC samples the demodulated IF tone returning from the chip after amplification. Typical configurations are $1$–$2\\,\mathrm{GS/s}$ at $12$–$14$ bits; the FPGA then digitally mixes against a reference IF, integrates over a window matched to the readout pulse, and thresholds the resulting IQ point against a per-qubit decision boundary calibrated from prior measurements. The integrated SNR of a single shot — the readout's signal-to-noise per dispersive readout pulse — is what sets the **single-shot readout fidelity** (Chapter 22 quantifies this metric explicitly).

A subtle DAC-side trap: **predistortion**. Drive lines have non-flat frequency response and finite settling time, so a Gaussian waveform commanded from the AWG is not what arrives at the qubit. Vendors apply a per-channel digital pre-distortion filter (an inverse-response FIR) so that the *delivered* waveform matches the requested one. Recalibrating this filter is part of the calibration cadence (§21.10).

## 21.6 Signal Integrity

Every microwave engineer's checklist applies at low temperature. **Impedance matching** at every interface — connector, PCB trace, on-chip launcher — to keep reflections below a few percent. **Cross-coupling** between adjacent lines kept below $-30\\,\mathrm{dB}$ by ground-plane integrity, careful via stitching, and (when needed) absorber-loaded enclosures. **Filtering**: copper-powder filters and reflective LPFs on every flux line to suppress thermal noise above the qubit frequency band, and infrared (IR) absorbers in line-of-sight paths to block stray blackbody photons from the warmer stages, which would otherwise both generate quasiparticles (degrading $T_1$) and cause photon-shot-noise dephasing (degrading $T_2$).

**Crosstalk** comes in two flavours. *Classical control crosstalk*: a drive applied to qubit $i$ also leaks onto qubit $j$'s drive line with some amplitude $c_{ij}$ and phase. The standard mitigation is **active cancellation**: when driving qubit $i$ by $\Omega_i(t)$, simultaneously play $-c_{ij}\\,\Omega_i(t)$ on every nearby drive line, the coefficients $c_{ij}$ calibrated from a crosstalk-tomography experiment that drives one qubit and measures induced rotations on its neighbours. *Quantum crosstalk* is the always-on **$ZZ$ interaction** between coupled qubits: even when both qubits are idle, the static Hamiltonian contains a $\zeta\\,Z_i Z_j$ term of size $10$–$100\\,\mathrm{kHz}$ that produces a slow conditional phase. Tunable couplers operated at the **$ZZ$-zero point**, echoed cross-resonance sequences, and **frame changes** that absorb the slow phase into virtual $Z$ rotations are all in current use; on devices with fixed couplers the $ZZ$ contribution sets a hard floor on the parallel-gate fidelity that simultaneous randomised benchmarking measures.

## 21.7 Cryogenic Electronics

A superconducting qubit lives at the bottom of a **dilution refrigerator**: a multi-stage cryostat that uses a $^3$He/$^4$He mixture below its phase-separation temperature to pump heat continuously and reach a base temperature of approximately $10\\,\mathrm{mK}$ at the **mixing chamber**. The intermediate plates — $50\\,\mathrm{K}$, $4\\,\mathrm{K}$, **still** ($\sim 700\\,\mathrm{mK}$), **cold plate** ($\sim 100\\,\mathrm{mK}$), and mixing chamber — each absorb heat from cables, attenuators, and amplifiers within a strict thermal budget. The colder the stage, the smaller the available cooling power: the mixing chamber provides only tens of microwatts of cooling power at base temperature (a few hundred microwatts at 100 mK), so every milliwatt of heat coming down a coaxial line through the attenuator chain matters.

The standard attenuation profile on a drive line places fixed attenuators at successive stages — for example, $20\\,\mathrm{dB}$ at $4\\,\mathrm{K}$, $10\\,\mathrm{dB}$ at the still, $20\\,\mathrm{dB}$ at the mixing chamber — both to thermalise the inner conductor's photon population at each stage and to attenuate the noise from the warmer stage's Johnson noise. The flip side is that achieving a useful drive amplitude at the qubit requires $60$–$70\\,\mathrm{dB}$ of total attenuation between the AWG output and the chip, which the room-temperature electronics has to overcome by sourcing a relatively high power.

**Wiring overhead** dominates the engineering of large devices. A typical superconducting qubit needs three to five physical lines: a drive line, a flux line (if tunable), a readout input, a readout output (often shared via a feedline among many qubits), and sometimes a separate fast-flux line for two-qubit gates. At a hundred physical qubits that is several hundred coaxial cables passing through the fridge, each contributing heat load and bulk; at a thousand qubits the cable count alone runs against the geometry of the cryostat. **Cryogenic CMOS** controllers at the $4\\,\mathrm{K}$ stage, on-chip multiplexed readout, and superconducting flexible ribbons are all under active development to break the line-count scaling. Trapped-ion systems face a different but related bottleneck: a single ion chain needs many independent laser beams or addressing AOM channels, and the optical wiring scales with the ion count.

## 21.8 Real-Time Feedback

A control system that only plays open-loop pulses is an instrument; one that closes a loop between measurement and the next pulse is a computer. **Active reset** is the simplest closed loop: measure the qubit, apply $X$ if and only if the outcome was $1$, and the qubit is left in $|0\rangle$ in a known time independent of $T_1$. The same primitive supports **mid-circuit measurement** (§9.8) and **classical feedforward** (§9.9): the FPGA discriminates the readout in real time, and a conditional branch in the pulse program selects the next gate.

The latency budget is tight. The feedforward loop must complete within the coherence time of the qubits that are *not* being measured; on superconducting devices that is $50$–$200\\,\mu\mathrm{s}$, and a typical end-to-end feedback latency (ADC sample $\to$ demodulation $\to$ discrimination $\to$ instruction dispatch $\to$ AWG playback) on current hardware is a few hundred nanoseconds to a microsecond. Anything that introduces software in the loop (a USB transfer, a host-side decision) blows the budget by orders of magnitude.

A short canonical sequence for **active qubit initialisation** with measurement-based reset:

```text
loop_initialise:
    measure  q0  -> c0
    if (c0 == 1):
        x  q0
    # repeat to drive heralded error below threshold
```

Iterating the loop $k$ times suppresses residual excitation as $p_e^k$ (assuming independent measurement errors), which is why two or three passes are standard.

## 21.9 Mid-Circuit Control Loops

Mid-circuit measurement, conditional gates, and parameterised loops compose to form the **adaptive circuit** primitive at the heart of error-corrected execution. A surface-code cycle (Chapter 19) is a tight inner loop of: round-trip syndrome measurements on the ancilla qubits, decoder execution against the accumulated syndromes, Pauli-frame update applied virtually or via physical correction, and continuation to the next cycle. Every layer of this loop sits on top of the feedforward latency budget of §21.8.

Three constraints govern what mid-circuit loops can do today. **Measurement disturbance**: a readout pulse on one qubit can scatter photons onto neighbours through the shared feedline, perturbing their coherence; the practical compromise is per-readout dead-time of a few hundred nanoseconds before the next gate. **Reset fidelity**: residual excited-state population after a *single* active-reset round (typically $1$–$3\%$ on current devices, dominated by readout error; repeated heralded rounds push it to the $10^{-3}$–$10^{-4}$ of §18.6) is the noise source for the next round of computation; deeper resets or measurement-based heralding squeeze it down at additional time cost. **Classical bandwidth**: the FPGA's branching instruction set is small, the decoder must be lightweight, and the syndrome buffer has finite depth. Real-time decoding of large surface-code patches is itself a research area; pre-decoders and lookup-table approximations bridge the gap until full decoders become FPGA-resident.

OpenQASM 3's `if`, `while`, and `for` constructs map directly onto FPGA branches when the target supports them; falling off the supported subset silently demotes a feedforward construct to a slow software loop. Reading the vendor's "real-time control" or "dynamic circuits" documentation is the only way to know which idioms run in the FPGA versus on the host.

## 21.10 Calibration and Drift

Every parameter the controller uses — drive frequency, drive amplitude, single-qubit gate length, two-qubit gate flux pulse, readout pulse frequency and shape, IQ discrimination boundary — is *measured* on the device, not derived from first principles. A daily or hourly **calibration routine** sweeps a hierarchy of experiments to refit them.

A typical superconducting cadence walks from coarse to fine:

1. **Spectroscopy**: drive the qubit at varying frequency and watch the readout to locate $\omega_{01}/2\pi$ to within a few hundred kilohertz. Repeat for the $|1\rangle \to |2\rangle$ transition to refit the anharmonicity $\alpha$.
2. **Rabi**: at the refit drive frequency, sweep amplitude and pulse duration; fit the Rabi oscillation to pin the amplitude that produces an $X_\pi$ in the nominal $30\\,\mathrm{ns}$.
3. **Ramsey**: measure $T_2^*$ and refine the drive detuning to within a kilohertz.
4. **DRAG calibration**: sweep the DRAG coefficient (§21.11) until leakage and phase error are minimised; typical figure of merit is the error in a repeated $X_{\pi/2}$ sequence.
5. **Single-qubit randomised benchmarking**: verify the Clifford error rate is below the spec ($10^{-3}$ to $10^{-4}$).
6. **Two-qubit gate tune-up**: sweep the cross-resonance amplitude, phase, and rotary-echo parameters; refit until interleaved RB on the entangling gate meets spec.
7. **Readout**: sweep readout pulse amplitude and duration; refit the IQ discrimination boundary from a calibration dataset of prepared $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ shots.

**Drift** is what makes this an ongoing exercise rather than a one-shot. Two-level-system (TLS) fluctuators in the substrate cause $\omega_{01}$ to wander by tens of kilohertz over hours; thermal cycling and slow flux drift shift two-qubit-gate parameters; readout drift comes from amplifier gain drift and from cryogenic cable temperature fluctuations. Production devices run **closed-loop calibration**: a low-overhead background experiment (single Ramsey, single repeated $X_\pi$) checks the most drift-prone parameters every few minutes, and a full re-tune is triggered whenever an error budget is breached. Re-tune cadence on a stable platform is every few hours; on an unstable platform it can be every few minutes.

## 21.11 Control-Plane Engineering

Optimal control turns calibration's hand-fit pulses into numerically optimised ones. Given a model Hamiltonian $H(t; \vec{c}) = H_0 + \sum_k c_k(t)\\,H_k$ with control envelopes $c_k(t)$ and a target unitary $U_{\mathrm{target}}$, the goal is to choose $c_k(t)$ such that the realised evolution achieves $U_{\mathrm{target}}$ with high fidelity in the shortest pulse time. Three algorithm families dominate:

- **GRAPE** (Gradient Ascent Pulse Engineering): discretise the pulse into $N$ piecewise-constant slices, compute the analytic gradient of the fidelity with respect to each slice's amplitude, and ascend. Standard for time-discretised problems with a known Hamiltonian.
- **Krotov**: a monotonic-improvement variant — think of GRAPE's gradient step with the step size chosen so that fidelity provably never decreases — implemented via an integral-equation update. Numerically robust on stiff problems.
- **CRAB** (Chopped Random Basis): expand the control into a small basis (Fourier modes, randomised orthogonal functions) with $O(10)$ parameters and run derivative-free optimisation on those. Tradeoff is expressive power for parameter count, which matters when the only feedback is closed-loop on the device itself.

In practice, **DRAG** (Derivative Removal by Adiabatic Gate) pulses are the workhorse for single-qubit gates: the in-phase quadrature is a Gaussian and the out-of-phase quadrature is its derivative scaled by $-1/\alpha$ (the inverse anharmonicity). The construction analytically suppresses leakage to $|2\rangle$ to first order in $1/\alpha$ and is calibrated by a one-parameter scan of the DRAG coefficient on the device. Two-qubit gates often use **echoed cross-resonance** with rotary-echo refocusing pulses to suppress slow drifts and residual $ZZ$. **Dynamical decoupling** sequences — **CPMG** (Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill) for $T_2$ extension, **XY8** for robustness against pulse errors and inhomogeneous dephasing — are inserted into idle stretches of a circuit to push the effective coherence time of an idling qubit toward $T_1$ rather than $T_2^*$.

## 21.12 FPGA/HDL in the Quantum Control Stack

The FPGA is the seam between the deterministic real-time world and the bursty software world. On the deterministic side, it owns waveform DMA, per-channel phase accumulators, IQ demodulators, threshold comparators, conditional dispatchers, and a small instruction set that the vendor's compiler targets. On the software side, it exposes a host interface (PCIe, Ethernet, USB) over which programs are uploaded, parameters are pushed, and result buffers are streamed back.

Vendor-specific instruction-set abstractions sit between user code and the gate-ware. **Quantum Machines QUA** is a Python-embedded DSL whose programs compile to an OPX bitstream; **Zurich Instruments LabOne Q** plays an analogous role; the Qiskit Pulse to IBM Quantum lowering compiles to a similar layer that is not directly user-visible. From a developer's perspective, the abstraction matters most when something is wrong: the wrong feedback latency, an unexpected double-pulse, a channel-pair phase glitch — all of which are debugged by reading the FPGA-level trace or by toggling a hardware probe rather than by debugging Python.

A non-trivial fraction of in-house control systems still write custom HDL. The reasons cluster around new readout discriminators (e.g., matched-filter banks for multi-state readout, machine-learned discrimination), low-latency decoders for error correction, and bespoke pulse-shaping engines that do not fit any vendor's pre-built primitives.

## 21.13 Packaging and Interconnects

The chip itself is mounted in a **package** — a printed-circuit board or microwave-engineered carrier — that routes the on-chip launchers to coaxial connectors at the cold plate. Package design is its own discipline: the goal is to deliver signals to every qubit with minimal reflection and minimal cross-coupling, while presenting a clean ground plane and a controlled enclosure that suppresses spurious resonant modes ("box modes") between the chip and the lid.

Common failure modes include **chip-package modes** that hybridise with the qubit and pull its frequency, **wire-bond inductance** that adds a frequency-dependent reflection to every line, and **ground-plane discontinuities** that allow common-mode currents to flow between qubits and produce difficult-to-debug crosstalk. Mitigations include **flip-chip** assembly (replacing wirebonds with controlled-impedance bumps to a separate interposer), **superconducting through-silicon vias** (TSVs) that provide low-loss vertical interconnect, and tighter-tolerance machining of the carrier itself.

At the next level up, the package mounts into a **fridge insert** — a copper rod with integrated coaxial routing, attenuator brackets, and amplifier mounts — that descends through the cryostat. The geometric density of cables on the insert is the practical bottleneck for scaling beyond a few hundred qubits; flexible superconducting ribbon cables and integrated cryogenic multiplexers are the active responses.

## 21.14 Gate Speed and Readout Fidelity

The end-to-end metric the gate-and-pulse stack delivers is the pair (gate time, gate fidelity), with **readout fidelity** as an independent companion that Chapter 22 treats as a first-class metric. Single-qubit gates on superconducting transmons land in the $20$–$60\\,\mathrm{ns}$ range with fidelities of $99.9\%$ to $99.99\%$. Two-qubit gates (cross-resonance, $\sqrt{\mathrm{iSWAP}}$, CZ via tunable coupler) run in $40$–$300\\,\mathrm{ns}$ with fidelities of $99.0\%$ to $99.7\%$ on leading devices. Trapped-ion two-qubit gates are slower — microseconds to milliseconds — but routinely achieve $99.9\%$ on small chains. Readout times of $200$–$1000\\,\mathrm{ns}$ at $98\%$ to $99.5\%$ single-shot fidelity are typical on superconducting; trapped-ion readout is slower (hundreds of microseconds) but reaches above $99.9\%$ through photon counting.

The trade-offs visible in those numbers are exactly the trade-offs §§21.1–21.13 controlled. Shorter pulse times reduce decoherence exposure but increase leakage (worse without DRAG), excite spectators (worse without crosstalk cancellation), and demand more bandwidth from the converters. Higher fidelity at fixed time means tighter calibration, more aggressive optimal-control polishing, and more careful microwave engineering. Higher readout fidelity at fixed time means more readout power and a louder return signal — which can disturb neighbouring qubits unless the package and the parametric amplifier chain are engineered to handle it. The whole chapter, viewed from one step back, is an instance of the standard physics trilemma: speed, fidelity, parallelism — pick any two and pay for the third.

## 21.15 Bridge to Chapter 22

The pulse layer this chapter described is what the gate-level numbers in vendor datasheets actually quantify. Chapter 22 takes those numbers — qubit count, connectivity, gate fidelity, $T_1$, $T_2$, gate speed, readout fidelity, **quantum volume**, **circuit layer operations per second** (CLOPS) — and turns them into the engineering metrics by which devices are compared, benchmarked, and ranked. The thread to follow: every metric in Chapter 22 is the integrated effect of one or more pulse-layer choices in Chapter 21; reading the two chapters back-to-back is what makes a vendor white paper interpretable rather than opaque.

**Sanity checks before moving on.**

1. A square $X_\pi$ pulse of length $30\\,\mathrm{ns}$ on a transmon with anharmonicity $\alpha/2\pi = -200\\,\mathrm{MHz}$ has Fourier weight at the $|1\rangle\to|2\rangle$ transition frequency that is far from zero. Estimate the leakage to $|2\rangle$ to lowest order and explain qualitatively how a Gaussian envelope plus DRAG suppresses it.
2. A drive line passes through $60\\,\mathrm{dB}$ of cumulative attenuation between room temperature and the mixing chamber. If the AWG outputs $0\\,\mathrm{dBm}$, what power reaches the chip? Why does the attenuation chain still help thermalisation despite throwing away $99.9999\%$ of the signal power?
3. Two qubits with a static $ZZ$ coupling $\zeta/2\pi = 50\\,\mathrm{kHz}$ idle for $1\\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ between operations. Compute the conditional phase accumulated. How does a virtual $Z$ rotation absorb it, and what does this miss that an active cancellation pulse would catch?
4. A measurement-based active reset loop measures with fidelity $98\%$ and applies a conditional $X$. Assuming independent rounds, how many iterations are needed to drive the residual excited-state population below $10^{-4}$ starting from a fully thermalised state?
5. Suggest one calibration experiment that should be re-run every few minutes on a noisy device and one that can safely run only at the start of each shift, justifying each choice in terms of the drift mechanism it targets.
