# Chapter 20. Quantum Hardware Platforms

[← Previous: Chapter 19](../part-08-noise-and-qec/19-quantum-error-correction-and-fault-tolerance.md) · [Table of Contents](../../README.md) · [Next: Chapter 21 →](21-quantum-control-and-electronics.md)

> **Status:** draft · **Phase:** 1 · **Sections drafted:** 12 / 12

Parts 2–8 have treated the qubit as a clean mathematical object: a unit vector in $\mathbb{C}^2$, acted on by unitaries drawn from a chosen native set (§8.12), measured projectively, and afflicted by an idealised noise model (Chapters 18–19). Real hardware is much more interesting. The qubit can be a microwave-frequency oscillation in a superconducting circuit, the two electronic levels of a trapped atom, the polarisation of a single photon, a localised electron spin in silicon, or a topologically encoded mode in a superconductor-semiconductor hybrid. Each physical embodiment determines the device's native gate set, its connectivity graph, its coherence times, its error model, the engineering effort required to scale it, and the room (or cryostat) it lives in. This chapter is a guided tour of the modalities competing in 2026, the engineering tradeoffs they expose, and the order-of-magnitude numbers a working developer needs to keep in mind when choosing a backend or reading a vendor's specification sheet.

> **How to read this chapter.** §§20.1–20.3 cover superconducting qubits, the modality with the largest installed base and the most code targeting it. §§20.4–20.5 cover trapped ions and neutral atoms — the platforms with the highest reported fidelities and the most flexible connectivity. §§20.6–20.9 cover the alternative modalities (photonic, silicon spin, NV centers, topological) where the engineering trajectory is still being established. §§20.10–20.11 cover analog simulators and annealers, which are not gate-model machines and need their own evaluation frame. §20.12 is a cross-platform comparison and the reference table to keep open when comparing vendor announcements. The chapter assumes Chapter 8 (gates, especially §8.12 native sets) and Chapters 18–19 (noise and error correction); nothing in it depends on a particular modality.

## 20.1 Superconducting Qubits

A superconducting qubit is a macroscopic electrical circuit that behaves quantum-mechanically because (i) it is cooled to a temperature where thermal energy $k_B T$ is small compared to its level spacing $\hbar\omega$, and (ii) one of its inductive elements is a **Josephson junction** — a sandwich of superconductor / insulator / superconductor a few nanometres thick — whose current-phase relation $I(\varphi) = I_c \sin\varphi$ is nonlinear. The nonlinearity is what makes the circuit a qubit rather than a harmonic oscillator: an LC circuit has equally spaced levels and a microwave drive resonant with the $|0\rangle \to |1\rangle$ transition would also excite $|1\rangle \to |2\rangle$, $|2\rangle \to |3\rangle$, and so on. Replacing the linear inductor with a Josephson junction shifts the higher levels off-resonance by the **anharmonicity** $\alpha = \omega_{12} - \omega_{01}$, typically $\alpha/2\pi \approx -200$ to $-300$ MHz for a transmon. A pulse tuned to $\omega_{01}$ then drives the qubit subspace cleanly, with leakage to $|2\rangle$ suppressed by detuning.

Operationally, the circuit sits inside a **dilution refrigerator** at $\sim$10 mK, connected to room-temperature control electronics by attenuated coaxial lines that filter thermal noise at every successively colder stage. Single-qubit gates are microwave pulses of duration $\sim$20–50 ns shaped to suppress leakage (the DRAG technique, Chapter 21). Two-qubit gates use either fixed couplers (cross-resonance on IBM transmons) or tunable couplers (parametric drives on Google, Rigetti, and IQM devices), and run in $\sim$30–100 ns. Readout is dispersive: each qubit is coupled to a microwave **resonator** whose frequency shifts by $\pm \chi$ depending on the qubit state, and a probe tone reflected off the resonator carries that frequency shift back to the room-temperature receiver.

Reported numbers, as of 2026: $T_1$ and $T_2$ around 100 $\mu$s on the best devices (with a long tail of outlier qubits at 300–500 $\mu$s); single-qubit gate fidelity around 99.9%; two-qubit gate fidelity around 99.5%, occasionally crossing 99.9% on highly tuned subsets; readout fidelity around 98–99% per qubit. Devices in the field range from a few tens of qubits (Rigetti's Ankaa, IQM's Garnet) to several hundred (IBM's Heron and Condor families), with the largest announced processors approaching 1000 physical qubits. Coherence and fidelity have improved steadily but not exponentially; the more dramatic recent progress has been in qubit count and in error-correction demonstrations (Chapter 19).

## 20.2 Transmon Architectures

The **transmon** is the dominant superconducting qubit design. It is a Josephson junction shunted by a large capacitor, operated in the regime where the charging energy $E_C$ is small compared to the Josephson energy $E_J$: typically $E_J / E_C \approx 50$–100. This ratio matters because charge noise — slow drifts in the electrostatic environment, the dominant low-frequency noise source in earlier "Cooper-pair box" designs — produces level shifts that scale as $e^{-\sqrt{8 E_J / E_C}}$. The transmon's large $E_J / E_C$ makes that scaling exponentially small, at the cost of reducing the anharmonicity to a modest few hundred MHz. The tradeoff has held: charge noise has been suppressed below the level where it limits coherence, and the modest anharmonicity is manageable with pulse shaping.

Vendor lattices differ in topology. IBM has standardised on the **heavy-hex** lattice — a hexagonal lattice with extra qubits on each edge — which has degree 2 or 3 at every site. The low degree simplifies frequency allocation (each qubit's transition frequency must differ from its neighbours' to avoid spectator crosstalk) and matches the connectivity needs of the surface code's heavy-hex variant. Google's Sycamore and Willow processors use a **square** lattice with degree 4 and tunable couplers, which gives the surface code a more natural embedding at the cost of denser frequency planning. Rigetti uses an octagonal "Aspen" tiling; IQM uses a square lattice with star-shaped subgraphs. Connectivity is *not* all-to-all: every two-qubit gate between non-adjacent qubits compiles into a sequence of SWAPs, and SWAP routing is one of the dominant compiler costs on superconducting hardware (Chapter 23).

A second axis of variation is whether qubit transition frequencies are **fixed** (set by junction parameters at fabrication) or **tunable** (modulated by a flux-bias line threading a SQUID loop in place of a single junction). Fixed-frequency transmons (IBM) are quieter — no flux-noise channel — but require careful fabrication to land at intended frequencies. Tunable transmons (Google, Rigetti) trade flux noise for the ability to retune around fabrication defects, to bring qubits in and out of resonance with their couplers, and to implement fast two-qubit gates by parametric modulation.

## 20.3 Fluxonium Architectures

The **fluxonium** is a different superconducting qubit, built from a small Josephson junction shunted by a large inductance — typically a chain of larger junctions acting as a "superinductor". The qubit transition frequency is much lower than a transmon's, often $\omega_{01}/2\pi \approx 100$–500 MHz, and the wavefunctions of $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ have very little overlap with the dominant dielectric-loss matrix elements that limit transmon $T_1$. The result is dramatically longer $T_1$ — single fluxonium qubits with $T_1$ in the millisecond range have been reported — and higher anharmonicity, which permits faster single-qubit gates.

The cost is engineering complexity. Fluxonium needs a much larger inductor (a chain of $\sim$100 junctions), and the low transition frequency puts the qubit close to the thermal energy at 10 mK, so the steady-state population of $|1\rangle$ is non-negligible unless active reset is used. Two-qubit gate schemes are also less mature than for transmons, and existing fluxonium processors have been small (single-digit to low-tens of qubits, from groups at Maryland, Yale, Atlantic Quantum, and others). Whether the coherence advantage translates into a system-level advantage at scale is one of the live questions in the superconducting community.

## 20.4 Trapped Ions

A trapped-ion qubit is a single atomic ion (typically $^{171}$Yb$^{+}$, $^{40}$Ca$^{+}$, or $^{137}$Ba$^{+}$) held in vacuum by oscillating electric fields in a **Paul trap**. The qubit is encoded in two long-lived internal levels — usually a pair of **hyperfine clock states**, sublevels of the electronic ground state separated by a microwave-frequency splitting that is insensitive to magnetic-field noise to first order. Coherence times are correspondingly long: $T_2$ ranges from seconds to minutes, set by ambient magnetic-field stability and laser noise, not by intrinsic decay.

Gates are driven by lasers. Single-qubit gates are stimulated Raman transitions through a virtual excited state, with typical gate times around 1–10 $\mu$s. Entangling gates use the shared vibrational modes of the ion chain as a quantum bus: the **Mølmer–Sørensen** gate applies a bichromatic laser tone whose beat note is detuned from the motional sidebands of two ions, producing the unitary

$$
\mathrm{MS}(\theta) \;=\; \exp\bigl(-i\\,\theta\\,X \otimes X\bigr),
$$

acting collectively on the ion-pair internal state with the motion left undisturbed. The same generator family is what §8.12 lists as the native two-qubit gate on IonQ and Quantinuum hardware. Gate times are slower than on superconducting devices — $\sim$100 $\mu$s for two-qubit MS gates — but the fidelities are higher: single-qubit gates around 99.99% on the leading platforms, two-qubit gates around 99.9%, and on small chains the best published numbers cross 99.95%.

The decisive architectural advantage is **all-to-all connectivity**: because every ion in a chain shares the same set of vibrational modes, an MS gate can entangle any pair without routing through intermediaries. Quantinuum's H-series and IonQ's Forte and Tempo devices currently operate at $\sim$30–60 trapped qubits with high connectivity; Oxford Ionics and others are pushing toward 100–256 ions per trap. The dominant scaling limitation is that long ion chains have many vibrational modes, the modes become spectrally crowded, and individual addressing becomes harder. Two solutions are in active engineering: **quantum charge-coupled device** ("QCCD") architectures that shuttle ions between trap zones to keep each gate zone small, and **photonic interconnects** that entangle ions in separate traps via single-photon Bell measurements. Quantinuum's H2 device uses the first; modular ion-trap proposals from Oxford and others use the second.

## 20.5 Neutral Atom Arrays

A neutral-atom processor holds individual alkali atoms — usually $^{87}$Rb or $^{88}$Sr — at the focus of an array of tightly focused laser beams ("**optical tweezers**"). The qubit is encoded either in two ground-state hyperfine sublevels (alkali atoms, microwave qubits) or in a clock-state pair of an alkaline-earth species (strontium, optical qubits, even longer coherence). Single-qubit gates are driven by Raman lasers or by microwave fields; coherence times are seconds for hyperfine qubits and tens of seconds for the best clock qubits.

The entangling mechanism is the **Rydberg blockade**. Exciting a ground-state atom to a high principal-quantum-number Rydberg state via a fast laser pulse gives it a huge electric dipole moment; two atoms within $\sim$5–10 $\mu$m of each other in Rydberg states interact strongly enough that the doubly-excited state is shifted out of resonance with the driving laser. A coherent excitation pulse applied to two such atoms therefore prepares the entangled state with one excitation shared between them, from which a controlled-Z gate can be constructed. Gate times are short — $\sim$100–500 ns — and the gate fidelity has been climbing rapidly, with the best 2025–2026 reports approaching 99.5% on two-qubit operations.

The defining architectural feature is **reconfigurability**. The tweezer pattern is generated by acousto-optic deflectors or by spatial light modulators, both of which can move atoms during a circuit. A pair of atoms can be brought into Rydberg-interaction range, gated, and then moved apart; non-local gates therefore cost real time (microseconds to milliseconds of movement) but no SWAP overhead. Vendors include QuEra (Aquila and successors, currently 256 atoms with announced 10,000-atom roadmaps), Pasqal (analog and gate-mode devices), Atom Computing (whose Phoenix system was the first to publish 1000-atom operation), and Infleqtion. The combination of large atom counts and reconfigurable connectivity has made neutral atoms the platform with the most aggressive scaling trajectory of any modality between 2023 and 2026; whether the gate fidelity can be pushed to fault-tolerant thresholds while the array grows is the open question for the rest of the decade.

## 20.6 Photonic Qubits

A photonic qubit encodes information in a single photon: in its polarisation, its path through an interferometer ("dual-rail" — one photon, two modes, with the qubit value given by which mode is occupied), or in a time-bin encoding. Photons interact extremely weakly with the environment at optical frequencies, so coherence in transit is excellent — photonic links are the medium of choice for connecting separated quantum processors regardless of the modality at the endpoints (Chapter 33). The hard part is making them *interact with each other*, since photons in vacuum do not couple at all.

Two architectural approaches solve this. The **KLM** scheme (Knill–Laflamme–Milburn) shows that linear optics, single-photon sources, and photon-number-resolving detectors are universal for quantum computation provided **measurement-induced nonlinearity** is exploited: a probabilistic two-qubit gate succeeds only when an ancilla measurement lands on a specific outcome, and the rest of the circuit branches on that outcome via feedforward. Naive KLM gates have low success probability ($1/16$ for the original construction), but resource-state factories and gate teleportation push the effective probability close to 1 at the cost of large overheads. The **measurement-based** or **fusion-based** model takes this further: a large entangled resource state ("cluster state" or fusion lattice) is prepared once, and the computation is driven entirely by adaptive single-qubit measurements, with the gate model emerging from the measurement pattern. PsiQuantum's architecture is fusion-based and targets silicon-photonics integration at wafer scale.

A separate photonic approach is **continuous-variable** quantum computing on the quadratures of light. Xanadu's Borealis and successor devices implement **Gaussian Boson Sampling** and a path toward universal computation using squeezed-light input states, programmable interferometers, and photon-counting measurements. Borealis runs at room temperature with cryogenic detectors only at the readout stage, and has been used for sampling-style demonstrations of quantum advantage.

The practical attractions of photonic quantum computing are room-temperature operation (apart from detectors), natural networking, and a path to wafer-scale silicon-photonic integration. The challenges are loss (each component absorbs a fraction of incident photons; loss propagates through the whole circuit), the difficulty of building deterministic single-photon sources, and the overheads of measurement-based architectures, where producing the resource state can dominate the qubit count. As of 2026, photonic devices have demonstrated sampling-style quantum advantage and small universal computations, but have not yet matched the fidelities or qubit counts of the matter-based platforms for general-purpose gate-model computation.

## 20.7 Silicon Spin Qubits

A silicon spin qubit encodes information in the spin state of a single electron (or hole) confined in a **quantum dot** in a silicon or silicon-germanium heterostructure. Two architectures are common: **electron-spin** qubits, where $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are spin-down and spin-up under a static magnetic field; and **singlet–triplet** qubits, which encode information in the joint spin of two electrons in adjacent dots, with the qubit basis given by the singlet and one triplet sublevel.

The motivation is manufacturability. Silicon quantum dots are defined by gate electrodes on a silicon substrate using essentially the same fabrication processes — and ideally the same foundry — as classical CMOS transistors. If the manufacturing learning curve translates, a silicon spin processor could eventually scale to billions of qubits on a single wafer with the same economics as classical chips. The physics also helps: $^{28}$Si has zero nuclear spin, so isotopically purified substrates eliminate the dominant magnetic-noise channel, and $T_2$ can reach milliseconds — much longer than a transmon's, and competitive with trapped ions on some measures. Gate times are around 10–100 ns for single-qubit rotations and 10–100 ns for the exchange-coupling-mediated two-qubit gates, much faster than the bare $T_2$ would require.

Reported scales remain small in 2026: Intel's "Tunnel Falls" 12-qubit chip, Diraq's small-scale demonstrations, Quantum Motion's 1024-dot quantum-classical chip, and university groups operating 4–16 qubits. Two-qubit gate fidelities have crossed 99% on small subsystems but have not yet matched the leading transmon or ion platforms. The scaling promise is the major story: if the gate-electrode density of silicon spin processors can ride the CMOS manufacturing curve while the fidelities continue to improve, the modality could leapfrog the others on qubit count within a few processor generations. The question for the rest of the decade is whether the integration challenges — wafer-scale uniformity, cryogenic CMOS control, charge-noise control across millions of dots — can be solved at production scale.

## 20.8 NV Centers and Other Solid-State Platforms

A **nitrogen-vacancy** (NV) center is a point defect in diamond consisting of a substitutional nitrogen atom adjacent to a vacancy. The defect has a paramagnetic ground state whose spin sublevels can be initialised, manipulated by microwave fields, and read out optically — all at *room temperature*. NV-center qubits have long ground-state $T_2$ (milliseconds at room temperature, seconds at cryogenic temperatures with dynamical decoupling) and exceptionally clean optical interfaces.

Despite these attractive properties, NV centers have not become a dominant computing platform. The reasons are practical: optically addressing many NV centers on the same chip is difficult, the coherent inter-NV coupling needed for two-qubit gates is short-ranged and requires precisely positioned defect pairs, and the strong optical interface that makes NVs easy to read out also makes them efficient sensors of every environmental fluctuation. The result is that NV centers have found their main application in **quantum sensing** — magnetometry, thermometry, and biosensing — and as **quantum network nodes**, where the optical interface is a feature rather than a complication. A handful of small NV-based quantum computers have been built (notably the Delft and TU Wien programs), but as of 2026 no NV processor approaches the qubit counts of the superconducting, trapped-ion, or neutral-atom platforms.

Other solid-state defect qubits include silicon-vacancy (SiV) and germanium-vacancy (GeV) centers in diamond, divacancies in silicon carbide, and rare-earth ions in crystalline hosts. Each has been demonstrated at the few-qubit scale; none has yet found a clear path to large-scale gate-model computation, though several remain promising as networking nodes and as quantum-memory elements in hybrid architectures.

## 20.9 Topological Qubits

A **topological qubit** is a qubit whose computational subspace is protected by a topological invariant of the system, so that local perturbations cannot move the state out of the protected subspace. The canonical proposal is the **Majorana zero mode** — a quasiparticle predicted to occur at the ends of certain one-dimensional topological superconductors — whose two species, $\gamma_1$ and $\gamma_2$, satisfy the non-Abelian braiding statistics needed to perform fault-tolerant gates by physically braiding the modes around each other. If realised, the appeal is dramatic: the gate set protected by braiding is exact up to exponentially small corrections in the system size, so a topological qubit would in principle require far less active error correction than the surface code overheads of Chapter 19.

The status in 2026 is that no topological qubit has been experimentally confirmed. Microsoft's "Majorana 1" device, announced in early 2025, claims topological-qubit behaviour in a indium-arsenide / aluminium nanowire architecture, but the underlying physics — whether the observed zero-bias peaks and parity measurements are signatures of true Majorana zero modes or of trivial Andreev bound states that mimic the same signatures — remains contested in the physics community. The 2018–2021 retraction of earlier zero-bias-peak claims (the Kouwenhoven–Microsoft program) is the recent historical cautionary tale, and reviewers have been appropriately demanding of any new claim. The honest summary is that topological qubits are a high-payoff, currently unrealised long bet; if the underlying physics works out, the platform could change the resource picture for fault tolerance, but the engineering and physics community has not yet reached consensus that it does.

## 20.10 Analog Quantum Simulators

An **analog quantum simulator** is a quantum device that implements a *specific* Hamiltonian, often one that is intractable to simulate classically, by directly engineering the interactions in hardware. It is not programmable in the gate-model sense: the user chooses a parameter regime within a fixed Hamiltonian family, prepares an initial state, lets the system evolve under the engineered $H$, and measures. The output is the late-time state, not a circuit output.

Neutral-atom arrays are versatile analog simulators: a tweezer array of Rydberg-coupled atoms can realise an Ising-like Hamiltonian whose parameters can be programmed continuously, and Pasqal markets such devices explicitly for combinatorial optimisation and condensed-matter simulation. Trapped-ion chains realise transverse-field Ising and XY models with tunable interaction ranges. Ultracold atoms in optical lattices, although not generally vendor-supplied, are the workhorses for Hubbard-model and many-body-physics studies in academic laboratories. Photonic and superconducting devices can also be operated in analog modes, though they are more commonly used in gate-model mode.

The analog modality is interesting for two reasons. First, certain quantum-advantage demonstrations (Gaussian boson sampling, large-scale Rydberg arrays) are naturally analog and would be enormously expensive to implement in the gate model. Second, the hardware overheads to engineer a specific Hamiltonian are typically much lower than those to compile that Hamiltonian's time evolution into a generic gate sequence: a 256-atom Rydberg array realises an analog Ising simulator immediately, where the same simulation in the gate model would require deep Trotterised circuits with attendant noise accumulation. The drawback is that analog simulators do not, in general, support error correction — there is no canonical way to apply a stabiliser code to a continuously evolving Hamiltonian — and so their precision is bounded by the hardware's native noise.

## 20.11 Quantum Annealers vs. Gate-Based Machines

A **quantum annealer** is hardware designed to find low-energy configurations of an Ising Hamiltonian by slowly interpolating from an easy-to-prepare ground state to the target problem Hamiltonian, exploiting the **adiabatic theorem**: if the interpolation is slow compared to the inverse-square of the minimum spectral gap along the path, the system stays in the instantaneous ground state and ends in the ground state of the target. The applications target is combinatorial optimisation: any quadratic unconstrained binary optimisation (QUBO) problem can be cast as an Ising model and submitted to the annealer.

**D-Wave** is the dominant vendor, with successive generations of superconducting-flux-qubit machines (Pegasus, Zephyr, and the 2024 Advantage2 prototype) reaching $\sim$5000–7000 qubits with a fixed sparse coupling graph. The qubits are physical objects, but the device does not implement an arbitrary gate set: it implements the transverse-field Ising Hamiltonian with programmable couplings $J_{ij}$ and biases $h_i$, anneals from large transverse field to the programmed problem Hamiltonian, and samples the result.

The relationship to gate-model machines is subtle. Adiabatic and circuit quantum computing are *theoretically* equivalent — adiabatic computation can simulate the circuit model with polynomial overhead and vice versa — but the engineering tradeoffs are entirely different: annealers have many more qubits but lower coherence ($T_2$ on D-Wave qubits is in the tens-of-nanoseconds range, compared to hundreds of microseconds for transmon gate qubits), no native error correction, and no demonstrated speedup over classical heuristics on a benchmark that is universally accepted as fair. Independent reviews through the 2010s and 2020s have found that the strongest classical heuristics (parallel tempering, simulated annealing, specialized SAT solvers) match or beat D-Wave on most benchmarks, though specific problem classes show suggestive scaling differences. Annealers are an interesting tool, particularly for prototyping QUBO formulations of operations-research problems, but as of 2026 they are not delivering the speedups originally hoped for, and they are not on the path to fault-tolerant universal computation.

## 20.12 Cross-Platform Metrics

Comparing modalities means comparing along several axes at once: coherence, gate fidelity, gate time, connectivity, qubit count, cryogenic burden, and the engineering maturity of the path to fault tolerance. The order-of-magnitude numbers below are representative of the leading vendor systems in 2026; individual qubits and special benchmarks routinely beat these values, and quoted records should always be checked against the device's full system-level performance, not against a single best qubit.

**Superconducting transmons.** $T_1, T_2 \sim 100\\,\mu$s; 1-qubit gate fidelity $\sim$99.9%, 2-qubit $\sim$99.5%; gate time 20–100 ns; connectivity nearest-neighbour on a 2D lattice (degree 2–4); qubit count 50–1000 deployed; cryogenic at 10 mK; the most mature error-correction demonstrations (surface-code distance-7 in 2024–2025, sub-threshold logical-qubit performance reported).

**Trapped ions.** $T_2 \sim$ seconds to minutes; 1-qubit fidelity $\sim$99.99%, 2-qubit $\sim$99.9%; gate time 1–100 $\mu$s; connectivity all-to-all within a chain, modular across chains; qubit count 30–256 deployed; room-temperature vacuum chamber with cryogenic detectors; high fidelity at small scale, scaling pathway through QCCD or photonic interconnect.

**Neutral atoms.** $T_2 \sim$ seconds (hyperfine) to tens of seconds (clock states); 1-qubit fidelity $\sim$99.9%, 2-qubit $\sim$99.5% (improving rapidly); gate time 0.1–1 $\mu$s; reconfigurable connectivity via atom rearrangement; qubit count 256–1000+ deployed; cryogenic detectors only; rapid scaling trajectory and recent fault-tolerance demonstrations.

**Photonic.** Effectively unlimited coherence in transit (loss-limited, not decoherence-limited); deterministic gate fidelity bounded by component loss; gate time picoseconds-to-nanoseconds at the component level; connectivity depends on the interferometer graph; system scale measured in modes rather than qubits, with PsiQuantum targeting million-photon resource states; room-temperature optical chips with cryogenic single-photon detectors.

**Silicon spin.** $T_2 \sim$ milliseconds in isotopically purified $^{28}$Si; 1-qubit fidelity $\sim$99.9%, 2-qubit fidelity $\sim$99% on small systems; gate time 10–100 ns; nearest-neighbour connectivity in 2D arrays; current qubit count $\sim$10s; cryogenic at 100 mK–4 K depending on architecture; the scaling story is the CMOS-foundry compatibility argument.

**Topological (Majorana).** No experimentally confirmed qubit as of 2026; if realised, the design promise is intrinsic noise resistance and a reduced fault-tolerance overhead.

**Annealers.** $\sim$5000–7000 qubits on D-Wave; coherence in the tens-of-nanoseconds range; not a gate-model architecture and not in the fault-tolerance race; useful as a QUBO-prototyping tool, not as a universal computer.

The honest summary in 2026 is that no single modality has won, that each of the leading three (superconducting, trapped ion, neutral atom) has plausible technical paths to fault tolerance, and that the decision of which backend to target for a given application now depends on a combination of fidelity, connectivity, gate time, queue access, and the maturity of the software stack — not on any single dominant figure of merit.

## 20.13 Bridge to Chapter 21

Every modality in this chapter is, at the room-temperature interface, a stack of analog electronics: arbitrary-waveform generators producing the microwave or laser pulses, digitisers capturing the readout signals, FPGA logic running the real-time feedback loops, and a software-defined control plane orchestrating the experiment. Chapter 21 opens that stack — the **control and electronics** layer that sits between the qubit and the user's circuit — and explains why control engineering, not qubit physics, is increasingly the limiting factor on system performance. The chapter then moves to calibration: the disciplined process by which the analog stack is brought to a state where the user's logical circuit actually runs the intended gates on the intended qubits.

**Sanity checks before moving on.**

1. A transmon has $T_1 = T_2 = 100\\,\mu$s and a 2-qubit gate of duration $\tau_g = 50$ ns. Estimate the gate-time-to-coherence ratio $\tau_g / T_2$, and from it the floor on achievable gate infidelity that decoherence alone implies. Compare with the reported 99.5% 2-qubit fidelity and identify whether decoherence or coherent errors dominate.

2. A trapped-ion chain has $T_2 = 10$ s and a 2-qubit Mølmer–Sørensen gate of duration $\tau_g = 100\\,\mu$s. Repeat the calculation. What does this say about why ion fidelities exceed transmon fidelities even though the gate is 1000$\times$ slower?

3. On an $n$-qubit superconducting device with nearest-neighbour connectivity, the cost of compiling a non-adjacent two-qubit gate is dominated by SWAP routing. Estimate (order of magnitude) how the routing overhead scales with the graph diameter, and contrast with the constant-cost gate available on a 30-qubit all-to-all trapped-ion device.

4. A neutral-atom array can be reconfigured during a circuit by moving atoms between gate sites at a speed of roughly $1\\,\mu$m / $\mu$s, with characteristic Rydberg-interaction distance $5\\,\mu$m. Estimate the time cost of bringing two atoms together for a gate and contrast with the SWAP routing of the previous question.

5. A photonic dual-rail qubit has component loss $\eta = 0.01$ per beam-splitter element. Estimate the success probability of a circuit with 100 such elements in series, and explain why measurement-based architectures with deterministic resource-state preparation are the photonics community's preferred path to scaling.
