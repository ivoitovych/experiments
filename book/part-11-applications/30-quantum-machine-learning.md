# Chapter 30. Quantum Machine Learning

> **Status:** draft · **Phase:** 5 · **Sections drafted:** 9 / 9

[← Previous: Chapter 29](29-optimization-finance-and-industrial.md) · [Table of Contents](../../README.md) · [Next: Chapter 31 →](31-quantum-sensing-metrology-and-tomography.md)

Quantum machine learning (QML) is, in 2026, the most over-promised and least-delivered application area in quantum computing. The phrase covers three nearly unrelated research programmes that share little except the words "quantum" and "learning": running classical machine-learning workloads on quantum hardware, using machine-learning techniques to characterise quantum systems, and training parameterised quantum circuits as if they were neural networks. The first programme has been gutted by a series of dequantisation results since 2018; the second is the genuine bright spot; the third is an active research area with no demonstrated advantage on any natural task. A chapter that takes QML seriously has to draw those distinctions sharply, because most of the marketing literature does not.

This chapter is therefore deliberately skeptical. It does not say "QML does not work" — there are real, defensible results, especially in the quantum-data category — but it pushes back hard on the framing that any quantum circuit with a trainable parameter is a candidate for production machine learning. The honest 2026 verdict is that QML is a research field, not a deployed technology, and the most defensible use case is learning *about* quantum systems rather than learning *with* them.

> **How to read this chapter.** §30.1 is the taxonomy that everything else hangs on; if you read only one section, read that. §§30.2–30.4 walk through the three categories in order of decreasing scepticism: classical-data ML on quantum hardware (mostly dequantised), ML applied to quantum data and devices (the genuinely useful one), and parameterised quantum circuits as neural networks (research-only). §30.5 covers quantum kernel methods specifically, because they are the one corner of category 1 with rigorous separation results. §30.6 collects the named architectures (VQCs, QCNNs, QGANs) and §30.7 treats hybrid training. §30.8 states the dequantisation lesson as a general rule of thumb. §30.9 is the honest verdict and the bridge to Chapter 31.

## 30.1 Three Categories of QML

The first thing to insist on is that "quantum machine learning" is not a single field. It is at least three:

- **Category 1 — Quantum algorithms for classical ML problems.** The input is classical data (a matrix of feature vectors, a sample from a distribution), the output is a classical artefact (a regression coefficient, a class label, a generative sample), and the quantum computer is invoked as a subroutine that is supposed to outperform classical linear algebra. Quantum linear regression, quantum recommendation systems, quantum principal-component analysis, and quantum support-vector machines built on HHL (§15.5) all sit here. This is the category where the "exponential speedup" headlines come from and where the dequantisation results of Tang and follow-ups have been most destructive.
- **Category 2 — Machine learning of quantum data and devices.** The input is *quantum*: measurement records from a state, syndrome streams from an error-corrected memory, samples from a noisy device, expectation values from a many-body Hamiltonian. The task is to learn something *about* that quantum system — its state, its Hamiltonian, its noise model, its logical-error rate. The "machine learning" can be classical, quantum, or hybrid; what matters is that the data is intrinsically quantum and there is no classical short-cut to producing it. This is the category where QML has genuine, defensible advantages.
- **Category 3 — Parameterised quantum circuits as neural networks.** A quantum circuit with trainable gate angles is treated as a model class, trained by gradient descent on a loss function, and benchmarked against classical neural networks. Variational quantum classifiers, quantum convolutional networks, and quantum GANs all sit here. The framework is intellectually interesting — it raises real questions about expressivity, trainability, and inductive bias — but no instance of it has produced a demonstrated advantage on a natural task.

These three categories have different data-access models, different success criteria, and different reasons to be optimistic or pessimistic. Lumping them together as "QML" makes every discussion about the field harder than it needs to be. The rest of the chapter treats them in turn.

The lazy taxonomy of "classical/quantum data $\times$ classical/quantum algorithm" gives four cells, of which the QQ cell ("quantum data, quantum algorithm") is largely empty in practice: you would need a quantum computer producing data that another quantum computer learns from, which is a research curiosity rather than a workflow anyone runs. The three categories above are the three populated cells.

## 30.2 Category 1: Quantum Algorithms for Classical ML

The textbook examples of Category 1 are: **quantum linear regression** (solve $A\beta = y$ for $\beta$ via HHL, read out a few coefficients), **quantum recommendation systems** (Kerenidis–Prakash 2016: produce a recommendation by sampling from a low-rank reconstruction of a preference matrix, using a quantum subroutine to access the matrix), **quantum principal-component analysis** (Lloyd–Mohseni–Rebentrost 2014: produce a quantum state encoding the top eigenvectors of a density matrix in polylogarithmic time), and **quantum support-vector machines** (Rebentrost–Mohseni–Lloyd 2014: solve the SVM dual via HHL on the kernel matrix).

Each of these claimed an *exponential* speedup — polylogarithmic in the data dimension where classical algorithms are polynomial. Each of these speedups depended on the assumption that classical data could be loaded into a quantum register in time polylogarithmic in the data size. That assumption was usually packaged as a hypothetical device called **QRAM** (quantum random-access memory): a black box that, on input $|i\rangle$, prepares the superposition $\sum_i \alpha_i |i\rangle|x_i\rangle$ for a classical dataset $\\{x_i\\}$ in time $\mathrm{polylog}(N)$. No physical QRAM has been built. Several proposed architectures (bucket-brigade and variants) are unstable under noise; the question of whether QRAM is achievable at scale is genuinely open and the consensus from $\sim 2020$ onward leans pessimistic.

Worse, even if QRAM existed, the speedups disappear under a fair comparison. Ewin Tang's 2018 work on the **recommendation-systems problem** showed that the Kerenidis–Prakash quantum algorithm could be *dequantised*: a classical algorithm, given an analogous classical access model (the ability to sample rows and entries of the matrix with probabilities proportional to squared magnitudes), runs in time polylogarithmic in $N$ — matching the quantum algorithm's scaling up to polynomial factors in the rank, condition number, and error tolerance. The exponential separation was an artefact of comparing the quantum algorithm under its QRAM access model to a classical algorithm without an analogous access model.

The follow-up programme was prolific. By 2021, the Tang-style dequantisation had been extended to quantum PCA, quantum SVMs, quantum linear regression in the low-rank regime, the Lloyd–Mohseni–Rebentrost density-matrix algorithms, and most other named Category-1 results. The general lesson — stated in §30.8 — is that when a quantum algorithm claims an exponential speedup over classical linear algebra and relies on a strong quantum access model, the corresponding classical algorithm under an analogous *classical* sampling-access model is almost always polylogarithmic too. The exponential gap was about the access model, not about quantum computation.

A few Category-1 problems have survived dequantisation, mostly those whose access model genuinely requires quantum oracle calls and where the data is itself produced by a quantum process. For purely classical data the working assumption in 2026 is that an HHL-style speedup, if claimed, is illusory until proven otherwise — and the burden of proof is on the quantum claim.

## 30.3 Category 2: Learning About Quantum Systems

Category 2 is where QML earns its keep. The defining feature is that the data is intrinsically quantum: it comes out of a quantum experiment, an error-corrected memory, or a many-body simulation, and there is no classical way to produce equivalent data in less time than the quantum process took. Learning *from* such data is then a legitimate machine-learning problem, often with rigorous advantages over the naive baseline.

The clearest example is **quantum state tomography**. Reconstructing a general $n$-qubit density matrix to fixed trace-distance error requires $\Omega(4^n)$ measurements in the worst case — every Pauli observable has to be estimated. For many tasks, however, the full density matrix is overkill: one only needs to predict a small set of properties, such as fidelities with target states or expectation values of local observables. The **classical shadows** framework of Huang–Kueng–Preskill (2020) shows that $O(\log M)$ shadow samples — each consisting of a randomised Clifford rotation followed by a computational-basis measurement — suffice to predict $M$ arbitrary linear properties of the state to fixed accuracy, with constants that depend on the locality and norm of the observables. The shadow protocol is a *learning algorithm*: it consumes copies of $\rho$, processes them classically, and outputs predictions about $\rho$ that generalise to observables not seen during training.

Beyond shadows, Category 2 includes:

- **Hamiltonian learning.** Given access to a time-evolved state $e^{-iHt}|\psi_0\rangle$ for known $|\psi_0\rangle$ and chosen $t$, recover the Hamiltonian $H$ within a parameterised family (local, geometrically constrained, $k$-body). Sample-complexity bounds depend on the structure of $H$; recent results (Anshu–Arunachalam–Kueng–Lin and follow-ups) give polynomial sample complexity for low-intersection local Hamiltonians, with the dependence on system size set by the locality structure rather than by Hilbert-space dimension.
- **Decoding quantum error correction.** Surface-code syndromes are streams of binary measurements with spatial and temporal correlations induced by the underlying noise model; classical decoders (minimum-weight perfect matching, union-find, belief-propagation) and learned decoders (recurrent or transformer models trained on simulated syndrome streams) both perform well, with learned decoders providing a measurable improvement on biased or correlated noise where the analytic decoders' assumptions break. This is a Category-2 application: the *data* is the syndrome stream from a quantum device, and the *learner* is typically classical.
- **Learning noise models.** Characterising the noise channel on a device is itself a learning problem. Randomised benchmarking and gate-set tomography are the textbook tools; more recently, machine-learning-based noise characterisation (e.g., learning Pauli noise channels from cycle-benchmarking data, learning crosstalk graphs) has filled in finer-grained pictures of device behaviour, particularly for the crosstalk and leakage modes that simpler protocols miss.
- **Learning quantum states from few copies.** Whether a state has a particular property (low entanglement entropy, low magic, membership in a stabiliser class, low energy under some Hamiltonian) is a decision problem with sample-complexity bounds. Recent work on "shadow learning" and on PAC-style models of quantum learning establishes that many natural property classes are learnable from polynomial copies, in regimes where full tomography would require exponentially many.

The pattern is consistent: when the *data* is genuinely quantum, the learning problem is a real problem, classical baselines are typically weak or absent, and quantum-aware learning algorithms (shadows, learned decoders, Hamiltonian learning) produce measurable wins on tasks people actually care about. This is the corner of QML that is most likely to be deployed in production over the next decade — not as a replacement for classical ML, but as the instrumentation layer of a working quantum computer.

## 30.4 Category 3: Parameterised Quantum Circuits as Neural Networks

The third category treats a quantum circuit with trainable gate parameters as a *model class*. A parameterised circuit $U(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ applied to a fixed input state, optionally with classical pre-encoding of a data point $x$ into the circuit (a **feature map**), produces an output state from which expectation values are read out as the model's prediction. Training is by gradient descent on a loss function evaluated over a dataset, with gradients computed via the **parameter-shift rule** (§8.13) or finite differences.

The framework is broad enough to subsume variational quantum classifiers, quantum convolutional networks, quantum GANs, quantum Boltzmann machines, and others. Its appeal is that parameterised quantum circuits are demonstrably *expressive*: in principle, a wide enough circuit can represent functions that no polynomial-size classical neural network can represent efficiently, on the basis of standard complexity-theoretic separations between $\mathsf{BQP}$ and $\mathsf{BPP}$. In practice, the picture is much less rosy.

Three obstructions dominate.

**Barren plateaus.** McClean–Boixo–Smelyanskiy–Babbush–Neven (2018) showed that for sufficiently expressive (Haar-random) parameterised circuits on $n$ qubits, the gradient of the loss function with respect to any parameter has variance that decays exponentially in $n$. The expected magnitude of the gradient is therefore exponentially small for typical initialisations; gradient descent stalls. Subsequent work extended this to local loss functions (which avoid the worst-case scaling for shallow circuits but reintroduce it at modest depth), to circuits with noise-induced concentration (the noise itself flattens the landscape exponentially in depth), and to quantum convolutional and other "structured" architectures (which avoid plateaus only when the structure is tailored to the problem in a way that essentially encodes the answer). The honest reading is that *generic* parameterised quantum models do not train; *structured* ones might, but the structure has to be domain-specific and the burden of proof is high.

**Noise-induced flatness.** On NISQ hardware (Chapter 25), the gates applied during training are themselves noisy. The composition of noisy channels drives the output state toward the maximally mixed state at a rate exponential in depth; the expectation values used as outputs concentrate near their mean and the loss surface becomes flat for reasons unrelated to the gradient-statistics argument. Empirically, training accuracy on real hardware degrades with circuit depth in ways consistent with this picture even when the noiseless circuit would have been trainable.

**No demonstrated advantage.** On the tasks where classical neural networks have been benchmarked (image classification, language modelling, structured-data regression), no Category-3 architecture has produced a result that classical baselines do not match or beat on the same dataset under fair compute comparison. The best published results are typically on tiny, hand-crafted datasets (the canonical "moons" or "circles" toy problems, or small extracts of MNIST) where the comparison is more illustrative than competitive. The field has produced striking *negative* results — barren plateaus, noise-induced flatness, dequantisation of kernel methods — but no robust positive result that has survived adversarial replication.

Category 3 is therefore best understood as a *research framework* for studying the expressivity and trainability of parameterised quantum models, not as a candidate production technology. The framework has pedagogical value (see §30.9), is connected to genuine questions in complexity theory, and may eventually produce an advantage on some quantum-native task. It has not done so yet.

## 30.5 Quantum Kernel Methods

Quantum kernel methods are the one corner of Category 1 with a rigorous separation result, so they deserve a section to themselves. The setup is a **feature map** $x \mapsto |\phi(x)\rangle$ that encodes a classical data point $x$ into a quantum state. The induced **quantum kernel** is the inner product of feature states:

$$
k(x, x') \;=\; |\langle\phi(x)|\phi(x')\rangle|^2.
$$

A kernel-based classifier (support-vector machine, kernel ridge regression) trained on classical data using $k$ as its kernel is a *quantum* classifier in the sense that the kernel is evaluated by a quantum circuit — typically a swap-test or Hadamard-test estimator running the feature-map circuit twice and computing an inner product — but its model class and training are otherwise classical.

The reason kernel methods are interesting in the QML setting is twofold. First, the kernel-evaluation step is a single circuit of fixed depth (essentially the feature map applied to two inputs), so the barren-plateau and noise-induced-flatness obstructions of §30.4 do not apply: there are no trainable circuit parameters and no gradient descent through the circuit. Second, the kernel encodes the data into a Hilbert space of dimension $2^n$, which under suitable feature-map design gives the classifier access to representations that no polynomial-time classical kernel can match.

The rigorous result is Liu–Arunachalam–Temme (2021): there is a *learning problem* — built around discrete logarithms in a carefully constructed group — for which a quantum kernel SVM achieves polynomial sample complexity and any classical learner under the same access model requires super-polynomial samples, assuming the classical hardness of discrete logarithm. This is the cleanest known separation for a quantum machine-learning problem. It is also, candidly, an artificial problem: the feature map is engineered around the discrete-log structure, and there is no natural dataset for which the construction applies.

For generic, naturally occurring data — image patches, protein sequences, financial time series — quantum kernels do not appear to beat well-tuned classical kernels (Gaussian RBF, learned deep kernels) on benchmarks where both have been measured. The provable advantage is real but narrow; the generic advantage is modest at best. Quantum kernel methods are still the most defensible Category-1 entry, because the kernel evaluation is shallow enough to run on near-term hardware and the framework does not depend on QRAM. They remain a research target with one provable success and many open empirical questions.

## 30.6 Variational Quantum Classifiers, QCNNs, and Quantum GANs

The named Category-3 architectures are worth knowing about, if only to understand what one is *not* getting from them.

**Variational quantum classifiers (VQCs).** Encode a data point $x$ via a feature map $U_\phi(x)$, apply a parameterised circuit $U(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, measure an observable $O$, and use the expectation $\langle\psi(x, \boldsymbol{\theta})|O|\psi(x, \boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle$ as the classifier score. Train $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ on a classical loss. The VQC is the prototype of a Category-3 model and inherits all the Category-3 obstructions: it is subject to barren plateaus when the parameterised block is deep or expressive enough to matter, to noise-induced flatness on real hardware, and to the absence of a demonstrated advantage on natural data.

**Quantum convolutional neural networks (QCNNs).** Cong–Choi–Lukin (2019) proposed a structured circuit ansatz inspired by classical CNNs: alternating layers of local "convolution" gates (acting on $k$-qubit patches) and "pooling" operations that trace out half the qubits while conditioning the remaining ones on measurement outcomes. The structure is restrictive enough to provably avoid barren plateaus at moderate depths and has been shown to detect topological-phase order in 1D and 2D condensed-matter models — which is, notably, a Category-2 problem (learning *about* quantum systems) rather than a Category-3 problem in disguise. QCNNs may turn out to be useful, but their useful applications are in Category 2.

**Quantum generative adversarial networks (QGANs).** Lloyd–Weedbrook (2018) and Dallaire-Demers–Killoran (2018) proposed quantum analogues of GANs in which the generator, the discriminator, or both are parameterised quantum circuits. The motivating intuition was that quantum generators could sample from distributions classical generators cannot match efficiently. In practice, QGANs share the trainability problems of VQCs and add the instability problems of classical GAN training; no QGAN has produced a benchmarked advantage on a real generative task. They are interesting as a stress test of variational training (and as a probe of distributions that are quantum-easy and classical-hard, which connects them to the boson-sampling literature) but not as a production technology.

**Quantum Boltzmann machines, quantum reinforcement learning, quantum autoencoders.** A long list of further architectures has been proposed, each translating a classical ML construct into a parameterised quantum circuit. The general pattern repeats: an academically interesting model, a small-scale demonstration on toy data, no benchmarked advantage over a classical baseline, no production deployment. The reader who wants the full taxonomy can consult Cerezo–Verdon–Huang–Cincio–Coles (2022) or the *Variational Quantum Algorithms* review by Cerezo et al. (2021); for purposes of this chapter the headline is that the named architectures are research objects, not products.

## 30.7 Hybrid Quantum-Classical Training

All the Category-3 training discussed above is *hybrid*: a classical optimiser proposes circuit parameters, a quantum device evaluates the loss (and possibly the gradient via parameter-shift), and the classical optimiser updates the parameters based on the result. The hybrid loop is the same one used in VQE (§15.8) and QAOA (§15.9), and inherits the same practical issues: shot-noise on every expectation-value query, sensitivity to hardware drift between shots, and the need for a classical optimiser robust to stochastic and noisy gradient estimates.

In practice, the classical-side optimiser choices in 2026 QML experiments cluster around three families: gradient-descent variants (SGD with momentum, Adam) using parameter-shift gradients; gradient-free black-box optimisers (SPSA, COBYLA, Nelder–Mead) that side-step the parameter-shift overhead at the cost of slower convergence; and natural-gradient methods (the quantum Fisher information metric of Stokes–Izaac–Killoran–Carleo, 2020) that use the geometry of the parameterised-state manifold to precondition the updates. None of these has emerged as a universal default; the choice is problem- and hardware-dependent.

The shot budget dominates the wall-clock cost of training. A parameter-shift gradient for a model with $p$ parameters requires $2p$ expectation-value evaluations per training step; each evaluation needs enough shots to reduce the shot-noise variance below the signal scale. With $p = 100$ trainable parameters, $S = 10^4$ shots per evaluation, $K = 10^3$ training steps, and a per-shot device time of $1\\,\mathrm{ms}$, the total wall-clock is $2 p S K \cdot 10^{-3}\\,\mathrm{s} = 2 \times 10^6\\,\mathrm{s} \approx 23\\,\mathrm{days}$. This is for a toy-scale problem. Production scaling is not on the table without orders-of-magnitude improvements in either shot rate or per-shot information yield (the latter being the motivation for shadow-based estimators and classical-shadow training proposals).

One subtler point: the hybrid loop's classical step is *much* faster than its quantum step, so the optimiser can afford to do significant classical computation between quantum queries — for instance, fitting a surrogate model of the loss surface and proposing the next query point via Bayesian optimisation. Several QML pipelines exploit this asymmetry; it does not solve the trainability problem, but it does shift some of the cost from the expensive quantum side to the cheap classical side.

## 30.8 The Dequantisation Lesson

The dequantisation programme initiated by Tang's recommendation-systems result generalises into a working rule of thumb. The rule is best stated as a question the reader should ask whenever an "exponential quantum speedup" is claimed for a classical-data problem:

**What is the quantum algorithm's data-access model, and what is the corresponding classical sampling-access model?** If the quantum algorithm assumes QRAM, or block-encoded access to a classical matrix, or any other "constant-time superposition over a classical dataset," then the fair classical comparison is to a classical algorithm with $\ell_2$-sampling access — the ability to sample rows of the data with probability proportional to their squared norm, and entries within a row with probability proportional to their squared magnitude.

Under that *matched* classical access model, the Tang programme has produced classical algorithms with running times that are polylogarithmic in the data dimension for: low-rank linear regression, low-rank matrix recovery, quantum recommendation systems, quantum PCA, quantum SVD, certain classes of quantum kernel methods, and the Lloyd–Mohseni–Rebentrost family. The polynomial dependence on rank, condition number, and accuracy tolerance is typically worse than the quantum algorithm's by a few powers, but the *exponential* gap in problem size has vanished. The quantum speedup claimed in the original paper was an artefact of comparing the quantum algorithm under a strong quantum access model to a classical algorithm under no analogous access model.

The lesson, restated as a checklist:

1. Identify the quantum algorithm's input access model precisely. ("QRAM-loaded state $\sum_i \sqrt{p_i}|i\rangle|x_i\rangle$" is a much stronger assumption than "classical matrix in RAM.")
2. Construct the matched classical access model. ($\ell_2$-sampling, or query access to entries plus row norms, depending on the structure.)
3. Look for a classical algorithm under that matched access model. If one exists at polylogarithmic dimension dependence, the quantum exponential speedup is dequantised.
4. The honest comparison is then between the (dequantised) classical polylog algorithm and the quantum polylog algorithm — typically the gap is polynomial in rank or condition number, sometimes in favour of the quantum side, often not.

The exceptions to the dequantisation pattern are instructive. **Shor's algorithm** for factoring (§15.2) and the **discrete-log algorithm** (§15.4) survive because their access model is to an oracle for $f(x) = a^x \bmod N$, not to a classical dataset; there is no analogous classical sampling access. **HHL** for genuinely quantum input states (states produced by another quantum subroutine, not loaded from classical memory) also survives, for the same reason. The dequantisation lesson is specifically about *classical-data* problems with hypothetical quantum data-access oracles, and it should be the first lens applied whenever such a problem comes up.

## 30.9 Honest Verdict, Pedagogical Value, and Bridge to Chapter 31

The QML field in 2026 is not where the QML field of 2014 promised it would be. The exponential speedups have mostly evaporated under dequantisation; the variational quantum networks have run into barren plateaus and noise; the quantum kernels have a single artificial separation result and no compelling natural-data advantage; the QRAM assumption that underwrote half the field is still a hypothetical device. The hype cycle has peaked and is on its way down.

What is left, on a sober reading:

- Category 2 — learning *about* quantum systems — is genuinely useful and is on the path to production. Classical shadows, Hamiltonian learning, learned decoders, and noise-model learning are real tools that real device teams use today, and the trend line is positive.
- Quantum kernel methods are the best Category-1 entry: a real provable separation on an artificial problem, a clean trainability story (no plateau because there is nothing to train), and a near-term implementation cost. They are not yet beating classical kernels on natural data, but they are not embarrassing either.
- Category 3 is research. It is interesting research with real connections to complexity theory and to the expressivity of quantum circuits, but it is not a production technology, and the empirical record on natural data is thin.

The pedagogical value of QML is worth flagging even when the production value is low. Parameterised quantum circuits are an excellent framework for thinking about circuit expressivity, about the geometry of parameterised-state manifolds, about the interplay between noise and trainability, and about the limits of variational methods. A reader who works through a QML construction — defining a feature map, computing parameter-shift gradients, hitting a barren plateau, redesigning the ansatz — has internalised most of what one needs to know about variational quantum algorithms in general, including the ones (VQE, QAOA) that have more legitimate near-term applications. QML is a good *teaching* topic even when it is a poor *deployment* topic.

The most defensible single sentence about QML in 2026 is: **the most reliable thing a quantum computer can learn is something about itself, or about another quantum system.** Production machine learning for classical data is, with the partial exception of artificial-problem quantum kernels, not on the table this decade.

Chapter 31 turns to the closely related but cleaner subject of **quantum sensing, metrology, and tomography.** Where QML asked "can a quantum computer learn a classical pattern?" and got an ambiguous answer, sensing and metrology ask "can a quantum system measure a physical quantity better than a classical system?" — and the answer there is yes, with rigorous quantum-Cramér–Rao bounds, with deployed atomic clocks and gravimeters, and with a much firmer foundation. The Category-2 spirit of this chapter — learning *about* quantum and physical systems with quantum tools — is the spirit of all of Chapter 31.
