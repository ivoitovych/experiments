# Chapter 20. Quantum Hardware Platforms

Parts 2–8 have treated the qubit as a clean mathematical object: a unit vector in $\mathbb{C}^2$, acted on by unitaries drawn from a chosen native set (§8.12), measured projectively, and afflicted by an idealized noise model (Chapters 18–19). Real hardware is much more interesting. The qubit can be a microwave-frequency oscillation in a superconducting circuit, the two electronic levels of a trapped atom, the polarization of a single photon, a localized electron spin in silicon, or — still experimentally unconfirmed (§20.9) — a topologically encoded mode in a superconductor-semiconductor hybrid. Each physical embodiment determines the device's native gate set, its connectivity graph, its coherence times, its error model, the engineering effort required to scale it, and the room (or cryostat) it lives in. This chapter is a guided tour of the modalities competing in 2026, the engineering tradeoffs they expose, and the order-of-magnitude numbers a working developer needs to keep in mind when choosing a backend or reading a vendor's specification sheet.

> **How to read this chapter.** §§20.1–20.3 cover superconducting qubits, the modality with arguably the largest installed base and the most code targeting it. §§20.4–20.5 cover trapped ions and neutral atoms — the platforms with the highest reported fidelities and the most flexible connectivity. §§20.6–20.9 cover the alternative modalities (photonic, silicon spin, NV centers, topological) where the engineering trajectory is still being established. §§20.10–20.11 cover analog simulators and annealers, which are not gate-model machines and need their own evaluation frame. §20.12 is a cross-platform comparison and the reference table to keep open when comparing vendor announcements. The chapter assumes Chapter 8 (gates, especially §8.12 native sets) and Chapters 18–19 (noise and error correction); nothing in it depends on a particular modality.

> **Moving-target warning — snapshot as of May 2026.** Every qubit count, fidelity, and vendor claim in this chapter is a dated snapshot and *dates quickly*. [Appendix F](../99-back-matter/appendix-f-hardware-snapshot-2026.md) is the single maintained home for the perishable numbers — when this chapter and Appendix F disagree, trust Appendix F, and re-verify against primary sources before relying on any specific figure.

## 20.1 Superconducting Qubits

A superconducting qubit is a macroscopic electrical circuit that behaves quantum-mechanically because (i) it is cooled to a temperature where thermal energy $k_B T$ is small compared to its level spacing $\hbar\omega$, and (ii) one of its inductive elements is a **Josephson junction** — a sandwich of superconductor / insulator / superconductor a few nanometers thick — whose current-phase relation $I(\varphi) = I_c \sin\varphi$ is nonlinear. The nonlinearity is what makes the circuit a qubit rather than a harmonic oscillator: an LC circuit has equally spaced levels and a microwave drive resonant with the $|0\rangle \to |1\rangle$ transition would also excite $|1\rangle \to |2\rangle$, $|2\rangle \to |3\rangle$, and so on. Replacing the linear inductor with a Josephson junction shifts the higher levels off-resonance by the **anharmonicity** $\alpha = \omega_{12} - \omega_{01}$, typically $\alpha/2\pi \approx -200$ to $-300$ MHz for a transmon. A pulse tuned to $\omega_{01}$ then drives the qubit subspace cleanly, with leakage to $|2\rangle$ suppressed by detuning.

Operationally, the circuit sits inside a **dilution refrigerator** at $\sim$10 mK, connected to room-temperature control electronics by attenuated coaxial lines that filter thermal noise at every successively colder stage. Single-qubit gates are microwave pulses of duration $\sim$20–50 ns shaped to suppress leakage (the DRAG technique, Chapter 21). Two-qubit gates use either fixed couplers (cross-resonance on IBM transmons) or tunable couplers (parametric drives on Google, Rigetti, and IQM devices), and run in $\sim$30–100 ns. Readout is dispersive: each qubit is coupled to a microwave **resonator** whose frequency shifts by $\pm \chi$ depending on the qubit state, and a probe tone reflected off the resonator carries that frequency shift back to the room-temperature receiver.

Reported numbers, as of 2026: $T_1$ and $T_2$ around 100 $\mu$s as device-wide medians, with best-in-class devices reporting $T_1 \sim 100$–400 $\mu$s and $T_2 \sim 100$–300 $\mu$s (the ranges Chapter 18 quotes) and individual outlier qubits beyond that; single-qubit gate fidelity around 99.9%, with best-in-class near 99.95%; two-qubit gate fidelity around 99.5%, occasionally crossing 99.9% on highly tuned subsets; single-shot readout fidelity around 98–99% per qubit in simultaneous full-device operation, with well-tuned individual qubits reaching 99–99.9% depending on integration time and assignment threshold. Devices in the field range from a few tens of qubits (Rigetti's Ankaa, IQM's Garnet) to several hundred (IBM's Heron and Condor families), with the largest announced processor (Condor, 2023) exceeding 1000 physical qubits at 1,121. Coherence and fidelity have improved steadily but not exponentially; the more dramatic recent progress has been in qubit count and in error-correction demonstrations (Chapter 19).

## 20.2 Transmon Architectures

The **transmon** is the dominant superconducting qubit design. It is a Josephson junction shunted by a large capacitor, operated in the regime where the charging energy $E_C$ is small compared to the Josephson energy $E_J$: typically $E_J / E_C \approx 50$–100. This ratio matters because charge noise — slow drifts in the electrostatic environment, the dominant low-frequency noise source in earlier "Cooper-pair box" designs — produces level shifts that scale as $e^{-\sqrt{8 E_J / E_C}}$. The transmon's large $E_J / E_C$ makes that scaling exponentially small, at the cost of reducing the anharmonicity to a modest few hundred MHz. The tradeoff has held: charge noise has been suppressed below the level where it limits coherence, and the modest anharmonicity is manageable with pulse shaping.

Vendor lattices differ in topology. IBM has standardized on the **heavy-hex** lattice — a hexagonal lattice with extra qubits on each edge — which has degree 2 or 3 at every site. The low degree simplifies frequency allocation (each qubit's transition frequency must differ from its neighbors' to avoid spectator crosstalk) and matches the connectivity needs of the surface code's heavy-hex variant. Google's Sycamore and Willow processors use a **square** lattice with degree 4 and tunable couplers, which gives the surface code a more natural embedding at the cost of denser frequency planning. Rigetti's earlier Aspen generation used an octagonal tiling; the current Ankaa generation uses a square lattice with tunable couplers; IQM uses a square lattice with star-shaped subgraphs. Connectivity is *not* all-to-all: every two-qubit gate between non-adjacent qubits compiles into a sequence of SWAPs, and SWAP routing is one of the dominant compiler costs on superconducting hardware (Chapter 23).

A second axis of variation is whether qubit transition frequencies are **fixed** (set by junction parameters at fabrication) or **tunable** (modulated by a flux-bias line threading a SQUID loop — a small superconducting ring interrupted by two junctions, whose effective inductance a magnetic flux can tune — in place of a single junction). Fixed-frequency transmons (IBM) are quieter — no flux-noise channel — but require careful fabrication to land at intended frequencies. Tunable transmons (Google, Rigetti) trade flux noise for the ability to retune around fabrication defects, to bring qubits in and out of resonance with their couplers, and to implement fast two-qubit gates by parametric modulation.

## 20.3 Fluxonium Architectures

The **fluxonium** is a different superconducting qubit, built from a small Josephson junction shunted by a large inductance — typically a chain of larger junctions acting as a "superinductor". The qubit transition frequency is much lower than a transmon's, often $\omega_{01}/2\pi \approx 100$–500 MHz, and the wavefunctions of $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ have very little overlap with the dominant dielectric-loss matrix elements that limit transmon $T_1$. The result is dramatically longer $T_1$ — single fluxonium qubits with $T_1$ in the millisecond range have been reported — and higher anharmonicity, which permits faster single-qubit gates.

The cost is engineering complexity. Fluxonium needs a much larger inductor (a chain of $\sim$100 junctions), and the low transition frequency puts the qubit close to the thermal energy at 10 mK, so the steady-state population of $|1\rangle$ is non-negligible unless active reset is used. Two-qubit gate schemes are also less mature than for transmons, and existing fluxonium processors have been small (single-digit to low-tens of qubits, from groups at Maryland, Yale, Atlantic Quantum, and others). Whether the coherence advantage translates into a system-level advantage at scale is one of the live questions in the superconducting community.

## 20.4 Trapped Ions

A trapped-ion qubit is a single atomic ion (typically $^{171}$Yb$^{+}$, $^{40}$Ca$^{+}$, or $^{137}$Ba$^{+}$) held in vacuum by the radio-frequency electric fields of a **Paul trap**, usually with static fields closing the trap along its axis. The qubit is encoded in two long-lived internal levels, and the choice depends on the species. Ions with nonzero nuclear spin ($^{171}$Yb$^{+}$, $^{137}$Ba$^{+}$) offer a pair of **hyperfine clock states** — sublevels of the electronic ground state separated by a microwave-frequency splitting that, at the right operating point, is insensitive to magnetic-field noise to first order. $^{40}$Ca$^{+}$ has zero nuclear spin and *no* hyperfine structure: calcium qubits are instead **optical qubits** — the ground state paired with a metastable $D_{5/2}$ level, driven on a narrow quadrupole transition — or Zeeman qubits in the ground-state sublevels. Coherence times are long but encoding-dependent: hyperfine clock qubits reach $T_2$ of seconds to minutes (set by magnetic-field stability, the local oscillator, and dynamical decoupling, not by intrinsic decay), while optical qubits are bounded by the metastable level's $\sim$1 s lifetime and by laser phase noise.

Gates are driven by lasers. Single-qubit gates are stimulated Raman transitions through a virtual excited state, with typical gate times around 1–10 $\mu$s. Entangling gates use the shared vibrational modes of the ion chain as a quantum bus: the **Mølmer–Sørensen** gate applies a bichromatic laser tone whose beat note is detuned from the motional sidebands of two ions, producing the unitary

$$
\mathrm{MS}(\theta) \;=\; \exp\bigl(-i\\,\theta\\,X \otimes X\bigr),
$$

acting collectively on the ion-pair internal state with the motion left undisturbed. The same generator family is what §8.12 lists as the native two-qubit gate on IonQ and Quantinuum hardware. Gate times are slower than on superconducting devices — $\sim$100 $\mu$s for two-qubit MS gates — but the fidelities are higher: single-qubit gates around 99.99% on the leading platforms, two-qubit gates around 99.9%, and on small chains the best published numbers cross 99.95%.

The decisive architectural advantage is **all-to-all connectivity**: because every ion in a chain shares the same set of vibrational modes, an MS gate can entangle any pair without routing through intermediaries. Quantinuum's H-series and IonQ's Forte and Tempo devices currently operate at $\sim$30–60 trapped qubits with high connectivity; Oxford Ionics and others are pushing toward 100–256 ions per trap. The dominant scaling limitation is that long ion chains have many vibrational modes, the modes become spectrally crowded, and individual addressing becomes harder. Two solutions are in active engineering: **quantum charge-coupled device** ("QCCD") architectures that shuttle ions between trap zones to keep each gate zone small, and **photonic interconnects** that entangle ions in separate traps via single-photon Bell measurements. Quantinuum's H2 device uses the first; modular ion-trap proposals from Oxford and others use the second.

## 20.5 Neutral Atom Arrays

A neutral-atom processor holds individual alkali atoms — usually $^{87}$Rb or $^{88}$Sr — at the focus of an array of tightly focused laser beams ("**optical tweezers**"). The qubit is encoded either in two ground-state hyperfine sublevels (alkali atoms, microwave qubits) or in a clock-state pair of an alkaline-earth species (strontium, optical qubits, even longer coherence). Single-qubit gates are driven by Raman lasers or by microwave fields; coherence times are seconds for hyperfine qubits and tens of seconds for the best clock qubits.

The entangling mechanism is the **Rydberg blockade**. Exciting a ground-state atom to a high principal-quantum-number Rydberg state via a fast laser pulse makes it enormously polarizable; two atoms within a blockade radius of roughly 5–10 $\mu$m (the exact figure depends on the Rydberg state and the geometry) interact — via van der Waals or dipole–dipole forces — strongly enough that the doubly-excited state is shifted out of resonance with the driving laser. A controlled-Z gate is built from a short pulse sequence driving the ground–Rydberg transition on both atoms: because the blockade forbids double excitation, the amplitude in which both atoms would be excited follows a different path — and accumulates a different phase — than the amplitudes in which only one or neither would, and with the pulse parameters chosen appropriately the net result is exactly the conditional $\pi$ phase of CZ. Gate times are short — $\sim$100–500 ns — and the gate fidelity has been climbing rapidly: 99.5% on two-qubit operations was demonstrated in 2023, and subsequent reports have pushed beyond it (Chapter 18 quotes the same trajectory).

The defining architectural feature is **reconfigurability**. The tweezer pattern is generated by acousto-optic deflectors or by spatial light modulators, both of which can move atoms during a circuit. A pair of atoms can be brought into Rydberg-interaction range, gated, and then moved apart; non-local gates therefore cost real time (microseconds to milliseconds of movement) but no SWAP overhead. Vendors include QuEra (Aquila and successors, currently 256 atoms with announced 10,000-atom roadmaps), Pasqal (analog and gate-mode devices), Atom Computing (whose second-generation system was the first to operate more than 1,000 atomic sites, in 2023), and Infleqtion. The combination of large atom counts and reconfigurable connectivity has made neutral atoms one of the fastest-scaling platforms of 2023–2026 — measured in trapped-atom counts, with the caveat that sites, loaded atoms, and gate-capable qubits are three different numbers; whether the gate fidelity can be pushed to fault-tolerant thresholds while the array grows is the open question for the rest of the decade.

## 20.6 Photonic Qubits

A photonic qubit encodes information in a single photon: in its polarization, its path through an interferometer ("dual-rail" — one photon, two modes, with the qubit value given by which mode is occupied), or in a time-bin encoding. Photons interact extremely weakly with the environment at optical frequencies, so coherence in transit is excellent — photonic links are the medium of choice for connecting separated quantum processors regardless of the modality at the endpoints (Chapter 33). The hard part is making them *interact with each other*, since photons in vacuum have no appreciable direct interaction at these energies.

Two architectural approaches solve this. The **KLM** scheme (Knill–Laflamme–Milburn) shows that linear optics, single-photon sources, and photon-number-resolving detectors are universal for quantum computation provided **measurement-induced nonlinearity** is exploited: a probabilistic two-qubit gate succeeds only when an ancilla measurement lands on a specific outcome, and the rest of the circuit branches on that outcome via feedforward. Naive KLM gates have low success probability ($1/16$ for the original construction), but resource-state factories and gate teleportation push the effective probability close to 1 at the cost of large overheads. The **measurement-based** or **fusion-based** model takes this further: a large entangled resource state ("cluster state" or fusion lattice) is prepared once, and the computation is driven entirely by adaptive single-qubit measurements, with the gate model emerging from the measurement pattern. PsiQuantum's architecture is fusion-based and targets silicon-photonics integration at wafer scale.

A separate photonic approach is **continuous-variable** quantum computing on the quadratures of light. Xanadu's Borealis and successor devices implement **Gaussian Boson Sampling** and a path toward universal computation using squeezed-light input states, programmable interferometers, and photon-counting measurements. Borealis runs at room temperature with cryogenic detectors only at the readout stage, and has been used for sampling-style demonstrations of quantum advantage.

The practical attractions of photonic quantum computing are room-temperature operation (apart from detectors), natural networking, and a path to wafer-scale silicon-photonic integration. The challenges are loss (each component absorbs a fraction of incident photons; loss propagates through the whole circuit), the difficulty of building deterministic single-photon sources, and the overheads of measurement-based architectures, where producing the resource state can dominate the qubit count. As of 2026, photonic devices have demonstrated sampling-style quantum advantage and small universal computations, but have not yet demonstrated general-purpose gate-model computation at a scale comparable to the matter-based platforms — with the caveat that the comparison is not unit-for-unit, since photonic architectures count modes, photons, and resource states rather than persistent qubits.

## 20.7 Silicon Spin Qubits

A silicon spin qubit encodes information in the spin state of a single electron (or hole) confined in a **quantum dot** in a silicon or silicon-germanium heterostructure. Two architectures are common: **electron-spin** qubits, where $|0\rangle$ and $|1\rangle$ are spin-down and spin-up under a static magnetic field; and **singlet–triplet** qubits, which encode information in the joint spin of two electrons in adjacent dots, with the qubit basis given by the singlet and one triplet sublevel.

The motivation is manufacturability. Silicon quantum dots are defined by gate electrodes on a silicon substrate using essentially the same fabrication processes — and ideally the same foundry — as classical CMOS transistors. If the manufacturing learning curve translates — an aspiration, not a consequence: cryogenic wiring, control multiplexing, cooling power, and yield are all unsolved beyond ordinary transistor manufacture — a silicon spin processor could eventually reach qubit counts and economics far beyond today's platforms. The physics also helps: $^{28}$Si has zero nuclear spin, so isotopically purified substrates eliminate the dominant magnetic-noise channel, and $T_2$ can reach milliseconds — much longer than a transmon's, and competitive with trapped ions on some measures. Gate times are roughly 100 ns–1 µs for single-qubit rotations (tens of nanoseconds in the fastest devices driven by electric dipole spin resonance, EDSR) and 10–100 ns for the exchange-coupling-mediated two-qubit gates, much faster than the bare $T_2$ would require.

Reported scales remain small in 2026: Intel's "Tunnel Falls" 12-qubit chip, Diraq's small-scale demonstrations, Quantum Motion's 1024-site CMOS-integrated characterization chip (with GlobalFoundries) — an array of quantum-dot devices for statistical characterization, not 1,024 operating qubits — and university groups operating 4–16 qubits. Two-qubit gate fidelities have crossed 99% on small subsystems but have not yet matched the leading transmon or ion platforms. The scaling promise is the major story: if the gate-electrode density of silicon spin processors can ride the CMOS manufacturing curve while the fidelities continue to improve, the modality could close the qubit-count gap quickly — a forecast contingent on yield, wiring, and cryogenic control that no one has yet demonstrated at scale. The question for the rest of the decade is whether the integration challenges — wafer-scale uniformity, cryogenic CMOS control, charge-noise control across millions of dots — can be solved at production scale.

## 20.8 NV Centers and Other Solid-State Platforms

A **nitrogen-vacancy** (NV) center is a point defect in diamond consisting of a substitutional nitrogen atom adjacent to a vacancy. The defect has a paramagnetic ground state whose spin sublevels can be initialized, manipulated by microwave fields, and read out optically — all at *room temperature*. NV-center qubits have long ground-state $T_2$ (milliseconds at room temperature, seconds at cryogenic temperatures with dynamical decoupling) and exceptionally clean optical interfaces.

Despite these attractive properties, NV centers have not become a dominant computing platform. The reasons are practical: optically addressing many NV centers on the same chip is difficult, the coherent inter-NV coupling needed for two-qubit gates is short-ranged and requires precisely positioned defect pairs, and the strong optical interface that makes NVs easy to read out also makes them efficient sensors of every environmental fluctuation. The result is that NV centers have found their main application in **quantum sensing** — magnetometry, thermometry, and biosensing — and as **quantum network nodes**, where the optical interface is a feature rather than a complication. A handful of small NV-based quantum computers have been built (notably the Delft (QuTech) and Stuttgart programs, and Quantum Brilliance's room-temperature devices), but as of 2026 no NV processor approaches the qubit counts of the superconducting, trapped-ion, or neutral-atom platforms.

Other solid-state defect qubits include silicon-vacancy (SiV) and germanium-vacancy (GeV) centers in diamond, divacancies in silicon carbide, and rare-earth ions in crystalline hosts. Each has been demonstrated at the few-qubit scale; none has yet found a clear path to large-scale gate-model computation, though several remain promising as networking nodes and as quantum-memory elements in hybrid architectures.

## 20.9 Topological Qubits

A **topological qubit** is a qubit whose computational subspace is protected by a topological invariant of the system, so that local perturbations couple to the encoded information only through strongly — ideally exponentially — suppressed processes. The canonical proposal is the **Majorana zero mode** — a quasiparticle predicted to occur at the ends of certain one-dimensional topological superconductors. The mode operators $\gamma_1, \gamma_2, \ldots$ are individual Majoranas (a single logical qubit needs at least four of them, i.e. two pairs), and exchanging them — *braiding* — realizes non-Abelian statistics. If realized, the appeal is dramatic: ideal braiding operations are topologically exact up to corrections exponentially small in the mode separation and spectral gap. The suppression covers smooth local perturbations only: quasiparticle poisoning, diabatic errors, thermal excitations, and measurement errors are *not* automatically exponentially small and must be engineered away separately. The catch is that Majorana braiding alone generates only the **Clifford group**; reaching universality still requires a non-Clifford resource (e.g. an injected $T$ state, §32.4), so a topological qubit reduces but does not eliminate the error-correction overheads of Chapter 19.

The status in 2026 is that no topological qubit has been experimentally confirmed. Microsoft's "Majorana 1" device, announced in early 2025, claims topological-qubit behavior in an indium-arsenide / aluminum nanowire architecture, but the underlying physics — whether the observed zero-bias peaks and parity measurements are signatures of true Majorana zero modes or of trivial Andreev bound states that mimic the same signatures — remains contested in the physics community. The 2018–2021 retraction of earlier zero-bias-peak claims (the Kouwenhoven–Microsoft program) is the recent historical cautionary tale, and reviewers have been appropriately demanding of any new claim. The honest summary is that topological qubits are a high-payoff, currently unrealized long bet; if the underlying physics works out, the platform could change the resource picture for fault tolerance, but the engineering and physics community has not yet reached consensus that it does.

## 20.10 Analog Quantum Simulators

An **analog quantum simulator** is a quantum device that implements a *specific* Hamiltonian, often one that is intractable to simulate classically, by directly engineering the interactions in hardware. It is not programmable in the gate-model sense: the user chooses a parameter regime within a fixed Hamiltonian family, prepares an initial state, lets the system evolve under the engineered $H$, and measures. The output is whatever measurement the experiment makes on the evolved system — time-resolved observables, correlation functions, spectra, or samples at chosen evolution times — rather than the readout of a compiled circuit.

Neutral-atom arrays are versatile analog simulators: a tweezer array of Rydberg-coupled atoms can realize an Ising-like Hamiltonian whose parameters can be programmed continuously, and Pasqal markets such devices explicitly for combinatorial optimization and condensed-matter simulation. Trapped-ion chains realize transverse-field Ising and XY models with tunable interaction ranges. Ultracold atoms in optical lattices, although not generally vendor-supplied, are the workhorses for Hubbard-model and many-body-physics studies in academic laboratories. Photonic and superconducting devices can also be operated in analog modes, though they are more commonly used in gate-model mode.

The analog modality is interesting for two reasons. First, certain quantum-advantage demonstrations (Gaussian boson sampling, large-scale Rydberg arrays) are naturally analog and would be enormously expensive to implement in the gate model. Second, the hardware overheads to engineer a specific Hamiltonian are typically much lower than those to compile that Hamiltonian's time evolution into a generic gate sequence: a 256-atom Rydberg array realizes an analog Ising simulator immediately, where the same simulation in the gate model would require deep Trotterized circuits with attendant noise accumulation. The drawback is that analog simulators currently lack any mature, general error-correction scheme. Proposals exist — energy-gap penalties, protected subspaces, autonomous (engineered-dissipation) correction, digital-analog hybrids — but nothing yet plays the role that the stabilizer machinery of Chapter 19 plays for circuits. In practice an analog simulator's precision is set by its calibration and native noise, and the error accumulates with evolution time and system size instead of being suppressible at will.

## 20.11 Quantum Annealers vs. Gate-Based Machines

A **quantum annealer** is hardware designed to find low-energy configurations of an Ising Hamiltonian by slowly interpolating from an easy-to-prepare ground state to the target problem Hamiltonian, exploiting the **adiabatic theorem**: run slowly enough and the system stays in the instantaneous ground state, ending in the ground state of the target. The textbook sufficient condition scales as the inverse square of the minimum spectral gap along the path, but rigorous versions of the theorem also involve derivatives of the Hamiltonian and matrix elements between eigenstates, different formulations give different gap powers, and the whole statement is a closed-system idealization. The target application is combinatorial optimization: any quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) problem can be cast as an Ising model and submitted to the annealer — though embedding a densely connected problem into the hardware's sparse coupling graph consumes chains of physical qubits per problem variable and costs precision.

**D-Wave** is the dominant vendor, with successive generations of superconducting-flux-qubit machines (Pegasus, Zephyr, and the 2024 Advantage2 prototype) reaching $\sim$5000–7000 qubits with a fixed sparse coupling graph. The qubits are physical objects, but the device does not implement an arbitrary gate set: it implements the transverse-field Ising Hamiltonian with programmable couplings $J_{ij}$ and biases $h_i$, anneals from large transverse field to the programmed problem Hamiltonian, and samples the result.

The relationship to gate-model machines is subtle. *Ideal* adiabatic quantum computation — with general (non-stoquastic) Hamiltonians, closed-system dynamics, and arbitrary control precision — is polynomially equivalent to the circuit model. A commercial annealer inherits none of that theorem: it implements one stoquastic transverse-field Ising family, operates at finite temperature in strong contact with its environment, and need not remain in the instantaneous ground state at all; it is best understood as a physical sampler of low-energy configurations. The engineering profile differs accordingly: annealers have many more physical qubits (a large fraction of which minor embedding consumes), coherence in the tens-of-nanoseconds range (a number not directly comparable to a transmon gate qubit's $T_2$, since an annealer never executes a coherent gate sequence), no deployed error correction (suppression schemes have been studied; none is standard), and no demonstrated speedup over classical heuristics on a benchmark that is universally accepted as fair. Independent reviews through the 2010s and 2020s have found that the strongest classical heuristics (parallel tempering, simulated annealing, specialized SAT solvers) match or beat D-Wave on most benchmarks, though specific problem classes show suggestive scaling differences. Annealers are an interesting tool, particularly for prototyping QUBO formulations of operations-research problems, but as of 2026 they are not delivering the speedups originally hoped for, and they are not on the path to fault-tolerant universal computation.

## 20.12 Cross-Platform Metrics

Comparing modalities means comparing along several axes at once: coherence, gate fidelity, gate time, connectivity, qubit count, cryogenic burden, and the engineering maturity of the path to fault tolerance. The order-of-magnitude numbers below are representative of the leading vendor systems in 2026; individual qubits and special benchmarks routinely beat these values, and quoted records should always be checked against the device's full system-level performance, not against a single best qubit.

**Superconducting transmons.** $T_1, T_2 \sim 100\\,\mu$s; 1-qubit gate fidelity $\sim$99.9%, 2-qubit $\sim$99.5%; gate time 20–100 ns; connectivity nearest-neighbor on a 2D lattice (degree 2–4); qubit count 50–1000 deployed; cryogenic at 10 mK; the most mature error-correction demonstrations (surface-code distance-7 in 2024–2025, sub-threshold logical-qubit performance reported).

**Trapped ions.** $T_2 \sim$ seconds to minutes; 1-qubit fidelity $\sim$99.99%, 2-qubit $\sim$99.9%; gate time 1–10 $\mu$s single-qubit and $\sim$100 $\mu$s two-qubit; connectivity all-to-all within a chain, modular across chains; qubit count $\sim$30–60 deployed, with 100–256-ion traps in development; state readout by room-temperature fluorescence detection (PMTs or cameras); high fidelity at small scale, scaling pathway through QCCD or photonic interconnect.

**Neutral atoms.** $T_2 \sim$ seconds (hyperfine) to tens of seconds (clock states); 1-qubit fidelity $\sim$99.9%, 2-qubit $\sim$99.5% (improving rapidly); gate time 0.1–1 $\mu$s; reconfigurable connectivity via atom rearrangement; site count 256–1000+ (trapped sites; loaded, gate-capable qubit counts are lower); state readout by room-temperature fluorescence imaging (EMCCD/sCMOS cameras); rapid scaling trajectory and recent fault-tolerance demonstrations.

**Photonic.** Coherence in transit limited by loss rather than decoherence; deterministic gate fidelity bounded by component loss; gate time picoseconds-to-nanoseconds at the component level; connectivity depends on the interferometer graph; system scale measured in modes rather than qubits, with PsiQuantum's roadmap targeting million-photon resource states; room-temperature optical chips with cryogenic single-photon detectors.

**Silicon spin.** $T_2 \sim$ milliseconds in isotopically purified $^{28}$Si; 1-qubit fidelity $\sim$99.9%, 2-qubit fidelity $\sim$99% on small systems; gate time tens of ns–1 $\mu$s single-qubit and 10–100 ns two-qubit exchange; nearest-neighbor connectivity in 2D arrays; current qubit count $\sim$10s; cryogenic at 100 mK–4 K depending on architecture; the scaling story is the CMOS-foundry compatibility argument.

**Topological (Majorana).** No experimentally confirmed qubit as of 2026; if realized, the design promise is intrinsic noise resistance and a reduced fault-tolerance overhead.

**Annealers.** $\sim$5000–7000 qubits on D-Wave; coherence in the tens-of-nanoseconds range; not a gate-model architecture and not in the fault-tolerance race; useful as a QUBO-prototyping tool, not as a universal computer.

The honest summary in 2026 is that no single modality has won, that each of the leading three (superconducting, trapped ion, neutral atom) has plausible technical paths to fault tolerance, and that the decision of which backend to target for a given application now depends on a combination of fidelity, connectivity, gate time, queue access, and the maturity of the software stack — not on any single dominant figure of merit.

## 20.13 Bridge to Chapter 21

Every modality in this chapter is, at the room-temperature interface, a stack of analog electronics: arbitrary-waveform generators producing the microwave or laser pulses, digitizers capturing the readout signals, FPGA logic running the real-time feedback loops, and a software-defined control plane orchestrating the experiment. Chapter 21 opens that stack — the **control and electronics** layer that sits between the qubit and the user's circuit — and explains why control engineering, not qubit physics, is increasingly the limiting factor on system performance. The chapter then moves to calibration: the disciplined process by which the analog stack is brought to a state where the user's logical circuit actually runs the intended gates on the intended qubits.

**Sanity checks before moving on.**

1. A transmon has $T_1 = T_2 = 100\\,\mu$s and a 2-qubit gate of duration $\tau_g = 50$ ns. Estimate the gate-time-to-coherence ratio $\tau_g / T_2$, and from it the floor on achievable gate infidelity that decoherence alone implies. Compare with the reported 99.5% 2-qubit fidelity: the observed infidelity is roughly ten times the decoherence floor, so list the candidate sources of the difference — coherent control error, leakage, crosstalk, calibration drift — rather than attributing it to a single cause.
