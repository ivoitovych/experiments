# Chapter 36. How to Judge Claims in Quantum Computing

> **Status:** prereviewed · **Phase:** 6 · **Sections drafted:** 8 / 8

[← Previous: Chapter 35](35-interpretational-and-conceptual-pitfalls.md) · [Table of Contents](../../README.md) · [Next: Chapter 37 →](37-endgame.md)

Quantum computing has the misfortune of being interesting enough to attract press releases and technical enough that most readers cannot quickly check what the press releases say. The gap between "this is a beautiful experiment" and "this changes what an industrial customer should buy" is wide, frequently mis-stated, and only occasionally bridged in good faith. This chapter is the field manual for the skeptical reader: how to distinguish a defensible claim from a marketed one, how to read a hardware paper, an algorithm paper, and a benchmark report, and how to weigh a roadmap against the physical-scaling reality the previous chapters built up. It is also the chapter that names specific case studies — Sycamore and Jiuzhang (advantage experiments), the IBM utility-scale experiments, Schnorr 2023 (a claimed RSA break), the Tang dequantization cascade — and uses them as worked examples of the failure modes the chapter catalogs.

> **How to read this chapter.** §36.1 sketches the landscape of claim sources and the incentives shaping each. §36.2 is the load-bearing section: five red flags that recur in nearly every over-claimed result. §36.3 covers the dequantization / "quantum-inspired" pattern, the single most important development for evaluating "exponential speedup" claims outside the abelian-HSP family. §36.4 catalogs the NISQ marketing-versus-capability gap. §36.5 gives concrete reading checklists for hardware papers, algorithm papers, benchmarks, and vendor decks. §36.6 reframes the "quantum advantage by 2030" timeline question in terms of physical-scaling laws that have to hold. §36.7 walks the supremacy / advantage / utility saga from 2019 to 2026 through specific case studies (Sycamore, Schnorr, the IBM utility-scale experiment, boson sampling). §36.8 closes with the peer-review pipeline and practical advice for the working engineer.

## 36.1 Hype Detection

The first useful move is to characterize *where* a quantum-computing claim comes from, because the failure modes are different. Four sources dominate the 2026 information stream.

**Academic preprints.** The arXiv quant-ph queue posts roughly $100$ papers a week. The papers most likely to be read carelessly are the ones with a hardware demonstration and a number in the abstract — qubit count, fidelity, runtime ratio. Academic claims tend to be precise about *what was measured* and looser about *what the measurement implies*; the abstract and the conclusions often have the loosest framing in the document. Read the methods and the appendices, not the abstract.

**Journal publications.** Peer review filters the most obvious errors but does not check, in any depth, the framing of the result against the field's broader claims. *Nature* and *Science* in particular reward bold framing, and the editorial trim required to fit a 2,000-word format is itself a hype amplifier. The journal version is usually a strictly weaker source than the arXiv version plus the supplement.

**Vendor announcements and press releases.** Optimized for the audience of customers, investors, and journalists, in that order. The measured quantity is almost always real; the comparison to classical, the comparison to competitors, and the extrapolation to future capabilities are almost always shaped by what the announcement is trying to do. Press releases are not lying — they are selecting and framing.

**Conference keynotes and analyst reports.** A separate category because they synthesize across the prior three and add their own framing. Useful as orienting summaries; rarely a primary source for any specific claim.

The incentive landscape is asymmetric. A careful experimentalist has a strong interest in *appearing* to have made an important advance, and a weaker (sometimes nonexistent) interest in *correcting* the field's perception of how important. The classical-simulation specialists who repeatedly closed advantage gaps in 2019–2024 (see §36.3) have an opposite incentive — to show that they were not surpassed — and the resulting tension produces most of the field's self-correction. Reading both sides is the only way to triangulate.

A practical heuristic: any claim that gets simpler as it moves from preprint to journal to press release to analyst summary is being shaped, not distilled. The information content of the framing decreases at each step; the implication content goes up. Track back to the preprint, read the methods, and form your own framing.

## 36.2 Benchmarking Claims and Their Caveats

Five red flags recur often enough in quantum-computing claims that recognizing them is most of what skeptical reading requires. None of them is a guarantee that the underlying work is bad; each is a sign that the framing is doing more work than the measurements.

**Red flag 1: qubit count without fidelity.** "A $1{,}000$-qubit processor" tells you almost nothing about what the device can compute. From Chapter 25, a crude first-cut comparison is the toy-model product $n d \epsilon$ (surviving global fidelity $\approx e^{-n d \epsilon / 2}$ under independent, equal per-gate errors) — a heuristic, not a budget law, since real capability depends on the actual gate mix, idle/readout/leakage errors, error correlations, connectivity, calibration stability, and the observable being estimated. But even the first cut shows that a chip with $1{,}000$ qubits at $\epsilon = 10^{-2}$ fares *worse* than one with $100$ qubits at $\epsilon = 10^{-3}$ at comparable depth and gate density. Any claim that quotes qubit count without quoting two-qubit fidelity and connectivity is selecting the kindest metric — and a median fidelity alone still hides the tails, the bad couplers, drift, and simultaneous-operation degradation; ask for distributions. The compound metrics — quantum volume, algorithmic qubits, CLOPS — exist exactly because raw qubit count is not the right axis, though each is protocol-defined, vendor-influenced, and gameable in its own way.

**Red flag 2: "quantum advantage" without classical baseline.** A claim of advantage requires both a quantum runtime and a *classical* runtime on the same problem, and the classical runtime should be the best known, not the textbook one. The 2019–2024 history (§36.3) is a sequence of advantage claims whose classical baselines were improved by orders of magnitude within months of publication. If a paper or press release does not name the specific classical algorithm it is comparing against, the result is uninterpretable.

**Red flag 3: asymptotic results applied to concrete instances.** Many quantum-algorithm results have the form "$O(\sqrt N)$ versus classical $O(N)$" or "$O(\log N)$ versus classical $\mathrm{poly}(N)$". These asymptotic statements *do not transfer* to any specific $N$ without an analysis of constants and additive overheads. Grover at $N = 2^{30}$ requires about $(\pi/4)\sqrt{N} \approx 26{,}000$ oracle queries (same order as $2^{15}$), and each query costs whatever a reversible circuit for the predicate costs — trivial for a toy predicate, enormous for a complex one; there is no universal per-query gate count. On a structured instance a classical SAT solver exploits the clause structure directly, while Grover can use it only inside the oracle, so the *wall-clock* comparison typically favors the classical solver far beyond any near-term $N$ — where, or whether, the crossover comes depends on the predicate's circuit cost, the fault-tolerance overhead, and classical parallelism. "Quadratic speedup" and "exponential speedup" are statements about asymptotic scaling in a specified access model — often relative to the best *known* classical algorithm rather than a proven lower bound; they are not predictions about any specific problem until the constants are pinned down.

**Red flag 4: "exponential" without resource accounting.** Closely related but worth its own entry. "Exponential speedup" usually means a polynomial-versus-exponential separation in the asymptotic complexity, but in the resource-accounting sense (T-count, depth, ancilla, magic states) the gap can shrink dramatically. HHL's nominal exponential speedup over Gaussian elimination evaporates once you charge for data loading (Chapter 15, §15.5). Hamiltonian-simulation speedups are real but require the input Hamiltonian to be specified as a local or block-encoded form; classical Monte Carlo on a problem that does not naturally admit such a form is not a fair comparison. The right framing is always: under which *input-access model* is the speedup claimed, and is that the access model the application actually has?

**Red flag 5: demos that conflate hardware and algorithm cost.** A demonstration of a 50-qubit VQE on a small molecule may be a hardware achievement (longer coherence, better calibration, lower crosstalk) and not an algorithm achievement (because the same molecule was solved classically in milliseconds), or it may be both, or it may be neither. The press release will frame it as "computing a molecule" without distinguishing what is novel. Hardware progress and algorithm progress live on different axes; conflating them confuses what is being claimed.

The skeptical reader's first pass through any quantum-computing claim should ask, in order: which red flag is the framing most exposed to, what is the corresponding caveat, and does the paper address it explicitly? The papers that *do* address these caveats are usually the same ones that survive scrutiny over the following years.

## 36.3 "Quantum-Inspired" vs. Actual Quantum Computing

The cleanest case study in over-claimed quantum speedup is the **dequantization cascade** that started with Ewin Tang's 2018 paper. The pattern, briefly stated and then unpacked: a quantum algorithm achieves a $\mathrm{poly}\log N$ runtime for some matrix-input problem (recommendation systems, low-rank PCA, supervised clustering, low-rank linear regression, kernel methods), assuming the input is supplied via a quantum-RAM (QRAM) oracle that loads classical data into a quantum state in $O(\log N)$ time. The dequantization move: build a *classical* algorithm with the same $\mathrm{poly}\log N$ asymptotic runtime, under a classical analog of the same access assumption — "sample-and-query" access, which gives the classical algorithm the ability to read individual matrix entries and to sample rows according to their $\ell_2$ norm in $O(\log N)$ time. The classical algorithm matches the quantum algorithm up to polynomial dependence on the matrix's *stable rank* (roughly, the effective number of dominant singular directions, $\\|A\\|_F^2 / \\|A\\|^2$) — a dependence the quantum algorithm also has but rarely surfaces in the original presentation.

Tang's 2018 dequantization of the quantum recommendation-systems algorithm of Kerenidis–Prakash was the first; the cascade extended through PCA (Tang 2018), supervised clustering (Tang 2019), low-rank linear regression (Gilyén–Lloyd–Tang 2018; Chia–Gilyén–Li–Lin–Tang–Wang 2020), and several kernel methods. By 2022 the consensus was that the entire family of "QML on classical data via QRAM" speedups was dequantized — not in the sense that the quantum algorithms were wrong, but in the sense that the *speedup over classical computation* they claimed was an artifact of the input-access model, not a fundamental quantum advantage.

What survived the dequantization wave is worth stating precisely. Hamiltonian simulation survives: simulating the dynamics of *suitably constructed* local Hamiltonians to the required accuracy is $\mathrm{BQP}$-complete — an efficient general classical simulation would imply $\mathrm{BQP} = \mathrm{BPP}$ — though the caveats matter: the Hamiltonian must be given succinctly (local, sparse, or block-encoded terms — itself an access model), $\mathrm{BQP} \neq \mathrm{BPP}$ is an unproven conjecture, and many specific instances (stabilizer, free-fermion, low-entanglement, sign-free, short-time) are classically tractable. Abelian hidden subgroup problems (Shor, discrete log) survive for a different reason — not $\mathrm{BQP}$-completeness, which they are not known to have, but because their speedup never rested on a QRAM-style access assumption in the first place: the input is a short classical string. Sampling problems — boson sampling and random-circuit sampling — survive as advantage claims because classical simulation of them is hard under plausible but unproven complexity assumptions; note that XEB is a verification statistic, not the definition of the hardness (spoofing a high XEB score and sampling accurately are different tasks), and the tasks have no established direct application (§36.7). Quantum machine learning on *quantum data* — characterizing states and processes produced by quantum experiments — survives because the input is intrinsically quantum and no classical access analog exists.

The vocabulary that emerged from the dequantization cascade is now standard. **Quantum-inspired** algorithms are classical algorithms that take a quantum algorithm's structure (the use of $\ell_2$-norm sampling, the SVD-based polynomial transformations) and run on classical hardware. They are *not* quantum algorithms with a classical-fallback mode; they are classical algorithms whose discovery was prompted by reading the quantum literature. The commercial QML products from several startups in 2021–2023 that branded themselves "quantum-inspired" were running these classical algorithms — not on quantum hardware, not in a quantum-simulator, but as classical software with a clever sampling primitive. They were sometimes fast, sometimes not, but they were not quantum computers.

The lesson for evaluating a QML claim in 2026: any claimed exponential speedup over classical that does not explicitly address the dequantization question, and that does not survive the substitution of the QRAM oracle by the equivalent classical sample-and-query access, is presumptively not a speedup. The papers that *do* address it — that exhibit a classical-input problem genuinely outside the reach of sample-and-query classical algorithms — are rare and worth reading carefully.

A related but distinct idea is the **classical-shadow** framework (Chapter 11, §11.5), which is sometimes confused with dequantization. Classical shadows are a classical *post-processing* technique applied to measurements of a quantum state; they reduce the sample complexity of quantum-state characterization but they do not eliminate the quantum part — the quantum device still has to prepare the state. Dequantization eliminates the quantum part entirely; classical shadows make the quantum part more efficient. The two ideas live on adjacent shelves and point in opposite directions; conflating them is a common error in informal discussion.

## 36.4 NISQ Marketing vs. Capability

The NISQ-era marketing-vs-capability gap deserves its own section because it is the gap most engineers encounter directly. Chapter 25 cataloged the physical constraints; this section catalogs the framing patterns that obscure those constraints.

**"Production-ready quantum computing."** This phrase appears in vendor marketing in roughly the way "enterprise-grade" appears in classical software: as a signal of intent rather than a description of the artifact. The 2026 reality is that no production workload at any quantum-cloud customer is materially better served by the quantum hardware than by classical alternatives. Production-ready means "available on a cloud API with a billing system" — a true statement about the operational maturity, not about the capability.

**"X-times faster than classical."** Almost always a comparison against either a specific classical implementation chosen unfavorably or against a textbook asymptotic complexity rather than a state-of-the-art classical algorithm. The IBM utility-scale claim (§36.7) is a defensible "above the reach of exact statevector simulation" claim that became "above the reach of any classical method" in some downstream coverage; the underlying paper was more careful than the coverage.

**"Quantum advantage demonstrated on real-world problem."** The "real-world" qualifier almost always refers to a problem instance that resembles a real-world problem in structure but not in size or commercial relevance. Optimization on a $20$-vertex graph is real-world in the sense that traffic routing involves graphs; it is not real-world in the sense that a logistics company would care about the answer. The honest framing is "we ran a small instance of a problem whose large instances would matter, and the small instance was solvable classically too."

**"Hybrid quantum-classical algorithm."** True but uninformative. Every algorithm that runs on NISQ hardware is hybrid because the optimizer, the compiler, the readout interpretation, and the experimental control are all classical. "Hybrid" by itself does not establish that the quantum part is doing any computational work that the classical part is not. A useful question is: what specifically would degrade if the quantum subroutine were replaced by a classical heuristic of the same wall-clock cost? If the answer is unclear, the quantum part may not be contributing.

**"Quantum-enhanced X."** The "enhanced" framing is a tell that the comparison is being avoided. Quantum-enhanced sensing is a real category (Chapter 31) with provable advantages; quantum-enhanced optimization and quantum-enhanced ML are mostly marketing.

The honest reading of NISQ marketing is that the field is in the *capability-building* phase, not the *capability-deployment* phase. Vendors are right to build tools and customer relationships now; customers are right to invest in tooling, training, and small experiments. The framing problem is treating capability-building as if it were capability-deployment.

## 36.5 Practicality vs. Asymptotic Advantage

The reading checklist for the four kinds of source the practitioner most often encounters.

**How to read a quantum hardware paper.**

- *Who measured what.* Identify the device, the experimental group, the calibration date, the measurement protocol. A median two-qubit fidelity reported in the body of the paper is different from a best-pair fidelity reported in a press release.
- *With which calibration.* Calibration drifts on most platforms over hours-to-days timescales. A paper that reports a single best-day result is reporting an upper bound on routine performance; the time-averaged performance over a real experiment is usually substantially lower.
- *How reproducible.* The methods section should make it possible to write the experiment's control sequence on a comparable device and expect a comparable result. If it does not — if the result depends on undocumented pulse-shaping, ancillary classical filtering, or post-selection — the result is not yet a capability of the platform; it is a capability of one particular run.
- *What was post-selected.* Many demonstrations report results conditional on heralding events, mid-circuit measurement outcomes, or rejecting "bad shots". Post-selection is legitimate when its overhead is reported; it becomes a red flag when the overhead is buried.

**How to read a quantum algorithm paper.**

- *Oracle model versus gate-cost model.* An algorithm with $O(\sqrt N)$ oracle queries is not the same as an algorithm with $O(\sqrt N)$ gate-count cost; the oracle implementation may dominate. Phase estimation costs $O(1/\epsilon)$ controlled-$U$ applications, but each application may cost millions of gates.
- *Assumed input access.* QRAM? Block encoding? Local Hamiltonian decomposition? Streaming oracle? The input-access assumption is part of the problem statement (§36.3 above). Speedups claimed under one model do not transfer to another.
- *What is the output.* A quantum algorithm whose output is the *amplitude-encoded* state $|x\rangle = \sum_i x_i |i\rangle / \\|x\\|$ has not solved the problem of computing the vector $x$: learning all $N$ amplitudes to useful precision requires tomography-like readout — at least linear in $N$, with further factors from the target precision and error model — and even writing down $N$ numbers classically costs $\Omega(N)$. HHL is the canonical example; its honest use case is estimating a global observable or an inner product of the solution, not reading out coordinates. The output specification matters as much as the runtime specification.
- *Constants and overheads.* "$O(\log N)$" hides everything. Look for the explicit polynomial in $1/\epsilon$, $\kappa$, sparsity $s$, and the block-encoding normalization $\alpha$ (for an LCU construction this is the coefficient one-norm, but in general it is construction-dependent; Chapter 16, §16.4). These are parameter dependences rather than constants — they scale with the input — and they frequently determine whether the algorithm is practical at all; since most such analyses target fault-tolerant machines, "fits on near-term hardware" is not the right practicality test.

**How to read a benchmark.**

- *Which metric.* Cross-entropy benchmarking, heavy-output generation, quantum volume, algorithmic qubits, CLOPS, application-specific accuracy — each measures something different. The choice of metric is itself an editorial decision.
- *Against which baseline.* The classical baseline should be the *best known* classical algorithm, not the textbook one, and it should be run on hardware comparable in cost to the quantum machine. A quantum benchmark beaten by a tensor-network simulation on a laptop is still a benchmark — it has simply failed to demonstrate classical intractability; and the classical result proves that one particular algorithm and implementation wins, not that the task is universally easy.
- *On which problem distribution.* Random-circuit sampling on circuits of random *local* gates (which approach unitary designs; hardware experiments never implement literal global Haar-random unitaries) is harder for classical algorithms than sampling on Clifford-rich circuits — and circuits with mostly Clifford gates plus sparse non-Clifford resources can remain classically tractable, depending on the magic and structure. The problem distribution determines what the benchmark is benchmarking.
- *Whose hands.* Vendor-supplied benchmarks on vendor-supplied hardware are useful but not independent. The best benchmarks are run by uninterested third parties on the same hardware with the same code.

**How to read a vendor marketing claim.**

- *What was actually measured.* Strip the framing; identify the underlying experimental quantity. Usually a fidelity, a runtime, a qubit count, or a benchmark score. The measurement is usually real.
- *What was extrapolated.* Roadmaps, capability projections, and "quantum advantage by year X" statements are extrapolations from current measurements via assumed scaling laws. The assumed scaling laws are not always physical.
- *What was compared.* If the marketing claims an advantage, identify the classical comparison: which algorithm, on which hardware, at what cost.
- *What was omitted.* Median two-qubit fidelity, connectivity, classical-control overhead, shot rate, calibration drift, post-selection overhead — any omission of a number that would weaken the claim is informative.

## 36.6 Commercial Readiness vs. Research Promise

The "quantum advantage by 2030" claim is the most common roadmap statement in 2026, and it is precise enough to evaluate. Reframing it in terms of physical-scaling laws (Chapter 25, §25.6): cryptographically relevant Shor on RSA-2048 requires roughly $10^7$ physical qubits at surface-code distance $\approx 27$ with two-qubit error around $10^{-3}$. Useful chemistry beyond classical reach requires $\sim 10^2$ to $\sim 10^3$ logical qubits and $\sim 10^9$ to $\sim 10^{12}$ $T$-gates, which translates to a similar physical-qubit footprint.

What has to happen between 2026 and 2030 for the most optimistic version of the roadmap to come true.

- *Physical qubit count must continue its $\sim 1.5\times$ per year growth.* Starting from $\sim 10^3$ qubits per chip in 2026, four years of $1.5\times$ gives $\sim 5{,}000$ per chip by 2030 — call it $\sim 10^4$ only if several chips are networked into one module. That is three orders of magnitude short of the $\sim 10^7$ cryptographic regime, and still well short of the "useful chemistry" regime: $10^2$–$10^3$ surface-code logical qubits at useful distances, plus magic-state factories, imply physical counts in the $10^6$ range, not $10^4$. Four data points also do not establish a stable exponential — packaging, control wiring, and yield produce discontinuities.
- *Two-qubit fidelity must hold or improve.* Median two-qubit fidelity at $99.5$ percent is sufficient for small-distance surface codes; pushing toward $99.9$ percent reduces the required code distance and the physical-qubit overhead per logical qubit substantially.
- *Magic-state distillation must scale.* The current demonstrations are at the level of a handful of distilled $T$-states; the required level for a useful chemistry run is $10^9$ to $10^{12}$ per algorithmic run. Distillation throughput is a current research bottleneck, not a fundamental obstacle, but the engineering gap is large.
- *Connectivity and classical control must scale together.* A device with a $10^3$ Hz shot rate executes $10^6$ shots per kilosecond regardless of qubit count — one shot runs the whole circuit — and that figure matters for variational workloads only relative to the parameter count, observable groupings, and optimizer iterations they need. Fault-tolerant chemistry and Shor roadmaps are paced by a different quantity altogether: logical cycle time and magic-state factory throughput, not NISQ shot rate. Scaling the control electronics, the cryogenic plumbing, and the room-temperature processing in step with qubit count is a systems-engineering exercise the field has not yet completed at scale.

Each of these scaling assumptions is plausible; none is guaranteed. The 2010-vintage projections for 2020 overestimated fault-tolerant timing and underestimated NISQ; the 2020-vintage projections for 2025 overestimated fault-tolerant timing again; the 2026-vintage projections are no more reliable than their predecessors, and the structural reason for the over-optimism — that progress feels exponential when one is in the middle of an exponential ramp, even when the exponent is smaller than hoped — has not gone away. The defensible reading is a condition, not a year: the *useful chemistry* regime requires the scaling assumptions above to hold simultaneously for years, and the *cryptographically relevant* regime requires substantially more of the same; the uncertainty on both is wide. A confident narrow window — early or late — is a claim that deserves the same evidence audit as any other claim in this chapter, not deference.

## 36.7 What Counts as Evidence

Three case studies in detail. Each illustrates a specific failure mode and each contains a generalizable lesson.

**Sycamore (Google, 2019).** A 53-qubit superconducting processor sampled from the output distribution of a depth-20 random circuit in $\sim 200$ seconds. The classical comparison cited in the original paper estimated $\sim 10{,}000$ years on the leading supercomputer of the time, supporting a claim of "quantum supremacy". The classical estimate was based on Schrödinger-style statevector simulation and did not account for tensor-network and secondary-storage techniques. IBM responded within weeks with a refined classical estimate of $\sim 2.5$ days using secondary storage, and subsequent work brought equivalent problems down to hours on commodity GPUs. The Sycamore result was a real demonstration that the random-circuit-sampling task was *above the reach of the classical algorithms in production at the time*, but the framing as "quantum supremacy" depended on a classical baseline that moved within months. The lesson: classical baselines are not static, and any advantage claim against a contemporary baseline implicitly assumes the baseline does not improve quickly.

**The Schnorr-method factoring claims (2021–2023).** Claus-Peter Schnorr's 2021 preprint claimed a fast *classical* lattice-reduction route to factoring ("this destroys the RSA cryptosystem", the abstract said); in late 2022–early 2023, Yan et al. built a *hybrid* proposal on top of it, combining Schnorr's lattice method with a small QAOA subroutine and claiming RSA-2048 reach with a few hundred qubits — versus the millions the Gidney–Ekerå estimates demand. Both rounds generated significant press attention. The technical community's evaluation was uniformly negative in both cases: the classical lattice-reduction step does not scale as claimed, the quantum subroutine's contribution to the asymptotics was overstated, and follow-up implementations on small instances showed the expected speedup does not materialize. By mid-2024 the consensus had settled on both proposals being creative but unsuccessful attempts. The lesson: a low-resource-count factoring claim is, in 2026, a strong prior that the analysis is incomplete; the Gidney–Ekerå estimates are not tight but they are within an order of magnitude of what the algorithm actually costs, and any claim that beats them by two or more orders of magnitude should be treated as presumptively wrong until checked.

**IBM utility-scale Ising-model experiment (2023–2024).** A 127-qubit Eagle processor computed expectation values for an Ising-model time evolution at circuit depths beyond exact classical statevector simulation. IBM framed the result as "utility" from the outset (the paper's title: "Evidence for the utility of quantum computing before fault tolerance") while asserting the computation lay beyond brute-force classical methods. Within months, multiple classical-simulation teams — using matrix-product states, isometric tensor networks, and neural-network quantum state ansätze — reproduced the published expectation values on commodity GPUs in less wall-clock time, narrowing what "beyond classical methods" could mean. "Utility", in IBM's framing, meant reliable quantum computation at a scale beyond brute-force exact methods — a claim about the device working, not about beating classical computation. What the classical reproductions establish is narrower than any "production tool" reading: agreement validated the *accuracy* of the device on the tested observables, and nothing more — if classical simulation delivers the same answers faster and cheaper, practical production utility remains unestablished. Judge the framing against the exact wording of the original and follow-up papers, not against retrospective paraphrases. The lesson: "utility" is a useful and defensible category distinct from "advantage", and the field's vocabulary is gradually settling into a healthier distinction between *the device works at scale* and *the device does something classical cannot do*. The first is true and increasingly common; the second remains rare.

A fourth case study, in compressed form, is the **boson-sampling complexity** sequence. The Aaronson–Arkhipov boson-sampling proposal of 2011 motivated a series of experiments — Jiuzhang (2020), Borealis (2022), and others — claiming exponential classical-simulation hardness for the sampling task. The complexity-theoretic foundations of the claim depend on conjectures about the hardness of computing matrix permanents to high accuracy; the classical-algorithm side has narrowed the implied advantage by exploiting structure in Gaussian-boson-sampling distributions and by using approximate-sampling techniques. The current state of the boson-sampling claims is unsettled and depends on which classical-simulation algorithm is taken as the comparison; the experiments are real demonstrations of large photonic systems, the complexity-theoretic claim remains the subject of active research. The lesson: even claims grounded in formal complexity-theoretic conjectures can shrink as classical algorithms improve, and a "provably hard" claim depends on a conjecture whose status the reader should check.

## 36.8 What Counts as Engineering Progress

The peer-review pipeline in physics and computer science has a specific structure worth knowing. **arXiv** is the primary venue for first publication; nearly every paper that will eventually appear in a journal appears first as a preprint, sometimes months or years earlier. Preprints are not peer-reviewed but they are public and citeable. **Journals** add peer review, which catches obvious errors and standardizes presentation but does not, in general, evaluate the framing of a result against the broader literature. **Conferences** in computer science (QIP, STOC, FOCS for the theoretical side; APS March Meeting for physics) provide additional filtering, with QIP in particular maintaining a high bar for novelty in quantum-information results.

The reader's trust at each stage scales with the stage's filtering. A new arXiv preprint by an unknown author claiming an exponential improvement on a known result should be treated with strong skepticism until the community responds (the response usually appears within weeks, in the form of follow-up preprints, social-media commentary by specialists, or seminar discussions). A preprint by an established group with a track record in the area is worth reading for technical content even before community response. A journal publication of a result that was originally a preprint is rarely more reliable than the preprint, but the supplementary material in journal versions sometimes contains details the preprint did not.

A useful heuristic for triangulating: follow specific authors and groups rather than press releases. The community of working researchers in any subfield is small — usually a few hundred people for any specific topic — and the reputation effects are strong enough that long-term reliability of an author's claims is a meaningful signal. Specific authors and groups whose claims have held up over the 2018–2026 window are worth knowing by name; the field is small enough that this is a tractable exercise.

Practical advice for an engineer trying to keep up with the field without being misled.

- *Build small experiments yourself.* The single most informative thing an engineer can do with a quantum claim is to reproduce a scaled-down version of it on a simulator or on cloud hardware. Most claims survive a small-scale reproduction; the ones that do not are the ones to be skeptical of. Chapter 26 covers the practical access setups for cloud hardware, and the cost of a small-scale reproduction is usually under a hundred dollars.
- *Replicate before adopting.* Before incorporating a quantum technique into an internal project, run it independently on a problem instance with a known classical answer. If the quantum technique matches the classical answer the technique works; if it disagrees, find out why before scaling up. This is true of any new technique, classical or quantum; quantum techniques deserve extra caution because the failure modes are less familiar.
- *Follow specific authors, not press releases.* The signal-to-noise ratio of arXiv preprints from groups with a track record is dramatically higher than the signal-to-noise ratio of vendor press releases. Build a reading list of $5$–$10$ authors whose preprints you read in full and supplement with broad-but-shallow scanning of the rest.
- *Trust the supplements, not the abstracts.* Methods sections, supplementary material, and appendices contain the details that determine whether a claim survives scrutiny. Abstracts are framing.
- *Wait for the response cycle.* For any claim that matters to your work, wait six to twelve months for the community response before acting on it. Most over-claimed results are corrected on that timescale; the ones that survive are stronger evidence than the original claim.
- *Distinguish capability from capability-readiness.* A demonstration that a quantum technique *can* do something is not a demonstration that the technique is *ready* to do it in production. The gap between the two is usually years and sometimes decades.

The field is genuinely moving forward, the hardware is genuinely improving, and the algorithmic toolkit (Chapter 16) is genuinely deeper than it was a decade ago. The skeptical reading discipline is not about doubting the progress; it is about correctly attributing the progress to the right axis. Hardware is improving; algorithm constants are coming down; the input-access question is better understood; the boundaries of dequantization are clearer. The places where the field is *not* moving forward as fast as the marketing suggests are real, and an engineer's job is to know which axis a given claim is making progress on.

Chapter 37 closes the book by stepping back from the specifics and asking what the next decade looks like — which problems are tractable, which are bottlenecked on hardware, which on algorithms, and which on the systems-engineering exercises this book has largely treated as background. The skeptical reading discipline of this chapter is the lens for Chapter 37: the endgame is plausible, not certain, and the difference is what the working engineer needs to plan against.
