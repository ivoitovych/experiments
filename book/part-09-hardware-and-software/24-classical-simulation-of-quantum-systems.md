Every quantum algorithm in this book has, at some point in its life, been simulated classically. Test suites for Qiskit, Cirq, and PennyLane run on classical hardware; pre-deployment validation of a variational circuit runs on a classical simulator; the very claim "this device beat classical simulation" is meaningful only insofar as the corresponding classical computation has been characterized. Classical simulation of quantum systems is therefore not the failure mode of quantum computing but its constant companion: the verification layer, the debugging surface, the classical baseline — an upper bound on classical cost, never a lower bound — against which advantage is measured, and — for many physically interesting Hamiltonians — the production tool that is still doing the science.

This chapter surveys the families of classical simulation algorithms that the rest of the book has implicitly relied on. Statevector simulation is the brute-force baseline. Density-matrix simulation handles noise at the price of squaring the dimension. Stabilizer simulation collapses Clifford-only dynamics to polynomial cost via the Gottesman–Knill theorem (§8.10), and near-Clifford extensions push the boundary by paying exponentially in the *non-Clifford* part only. Tensor networks — MPS, PEPS, DMRG, TEBD; each acronym is expanded where its method is introduced in §§24.7–24.10 — exploit limited entanglement to compress the state, and dominate large parts of condensed-matter simulation in low-entanglement regimes (quantum Monte Carlo, exact diagonalization, DMFT, and other methods rule elsewhere). Schrödinger–Feynman path summation trades memory for time on shallow circuits. Each method has a regime in which it is the best tool, and a regime beyond which it degrades — often gradually and instance-dependently, rarely at a provable sharp boundary.

> **How to read this chapter.** §24.1–§24.4 are mandatory: they fix the cost model, the brute-force baselines, and the noise-aware extension that every later method is measured against. §24.5–§24.6 (stabilizer and near-Clifford) connect directly to the Clifford+T material of §8.10 and to fault-tolerant simulation in Chapter 19. §24.7–§24.10 (tensor networks, DMRG, TEBD, variational) are the production tools for many-body physics and are the lever the recent "classical pushback" results pull. §24.11–§24.13 cover hybrid path-sum methods, GPU and distributed execution, and noise simulators. §24.14–§24.15 close the loop with the quantum-advantage frontier and a decision tree for picking a simulator. Readers focused on hardware can skim §24.7–§24.10 on first pass; readers focused on algorithms should not.

## 24.1 Why Classical Simulation Matters

Three distinct constituencies use classical simulators, and conflating their requirements is the source of most confusion about what the simulators are *for*.

The **algorithm developer** uses simulation to test and debug. A 20-qubit variational circuit can be fully exercised on a laptop; statevector access lets the developer assert that the prepared state matches the analytic target, examine intermediate amplitudes during a Grover iteration, or check that an ansatz actually has the expressibility its paper claims. None of this is possible on real hardware: a quantum device returns samples, not amplitudes, and tomographic reconstruction of a 20-qubit state would consume an astronomically impractical number of shots. The simulator is by far the most convenient place where the *internals* of a quantum algorithm are observable.

The **hardware characterizer** uses simulation to model noise. A density-matrix simulator can ingest a calibrated noise model — gate errors, readout errors, $T_1$ and $T_2$ decoherence channels — and predict the output distribution a real device should produce. Comparing that prediction against the device's measured distribution tests the *noise model* against the hardware (cross-entropy benchmarking's ideal probabilities come from noiseless simulation, §22.10; randomized benchmarking is analyzed statistically; tomography reconstructs from experimental data). The simulator is a model of the device, not a ground-truth oracle: when prediction and measurement disagree, either side may be at fault.

The **complexity theorist and the vendor's competitor** use simulation to set the classical baseline. Every claim of "quantum advantage" is a claim of the form "the best *known* classical algorithm needs time $T$ to perform this sampling task." The number $T$ is established by *running* — or carefully estimating — the best classical simulator, which yields an *upper bound* on the classical cost, never a lower bound over all algorithms; that is exactly why the frontier keeps moving. When a 2019 paper reported Sycamore had performed a sampling task in 200 seconds that would cost a classical supercomputer 10 000 years, that 10,000-year figure was a classical-simulation estimate. Subsequent work (§24.14) cut the believed classical cost by orders of magnitude. Classical simulation is the moving frontier that defines what "quantum advantage" even means.

Three additional, smaller-scale uses round out the picture. **Education and prototyping**: every reader who has stepped through a Bell-pair circuit in Qiskit has used a simulator. **Compiler validation**: equivalence-checking that a transpiled circuit implements the same unitary as the source, on small enough instances to verify exhaustively (§23.14). **Algorithm research outside the universal model**: variational ansätze for chemistry, ground-state finding via DMRG, and time evolution of one-dimensional quantum magnets are *production-grade* classical-simulation workloads where the question is not "can the simulator keep up?" but "what physics has it taught us?"

## 24.2 The Exponential-Scaling Barrier

A pure state on $n$ qubits is a complex unit vector in $\mathbb{C}^{2^n}$. Storing it in dense form requires $2^n$ complex numbers; in IEEE double precision (16 bytes per complex number), that is

$$
M(n) \;=\; 16 \cdot 2^n \text{ bytes}.
$$

The threshold values matter and recur throughout the chapter. At $n = 30$, $M = 16$ GiB — feasible on a workstation. At $n = 33$, $M = 128$ GiB — feasible on a large server. At $n = 35$, $M = 512$ GiB — borderline on a single shared-memory node. At $n = 40$, $M = 16$ TiB — distributed memory across an HPC cluster. At $n = 50$, $M = 16$ PiB — beyond the aggregate memory of most national supercomputers; reported $\sim$50-qubit "full-state" results rely on reduced precision, compressed encodings, or selected amplitudes rather than dense complex128 storage.

Time scales no better. Applying a single-qubit gate to a dense statevector touches every amplitude, so the per-gate cost is $\Theta(2^n)$ memory references; a circuit of $g$ gates applied one at a time costs $\Theta(g \\, 2^n)$ in the dense model (a depth-$d$ layer can hold many gates — depth and gate count are different quantities). A generic two-qubit gate updates quadruples with a $4 \times 4$ transform — several times the single-qubit kernel's arithmetic, not merely double. Memory bandwidth, not raw FLOPs, is usually the binding constraint for unfused kernels: memory systems deliver hundreds of GB/s to a few TB/s, and a single gate on a 33-qubit state streams at least one read and one write of most of its 128 GiB.

The exponential is in the *width* of the quantum register, not its *depth*. A 1000-gate circuit on 20 qubits is comfortable; a 100-gate circuit on 50 qubits is not — *for dense statevector simulation*; Clifford, low-entanglement, or amplitude-targeted structure can crack a 50-qubit case where it exists. Every method in this chapter is, in one way or another, a tactic for avoiding storing all $2^n$ amplitudes:

- **Stabilizer simulation** (§24.5) stores a polynomial-size tableau and is exact for Clifford circuits.
- **Tensor networks** (§24.7–§24.10) store a factored representation whose size grows with *entanglement*, not with $n$.
- **Schrödinger–Feynman path summation** (§24.11) targets one output amplitude at a time, storing partition statevectors rather than the global state; its cost is governed by the gates crossing the partition cuts, not by width alone.
- **Density-matrix and trajectory simulators** (§24.4, §24.13) address *noisy* evolution — the density matrix by paying more (the dimension squares), trajectories by sampling pure states at statevector cost per sample. Noise is a modeling need, not an escape from the amplitude barrier.

The exponential barrier is also the reason a quantum computer is interesting at all. If quantum states could be losslessly compressed into polynomial-size classical descriptions *that also supported efficient gate updates, measurement sampling, and adequate numerical precision*, BQP would collapse into BPP — compression alone would not do it. The structure of the methods below — each carving out a tractable subclass — is the working theorist's evidence that the general case is genuinely hard.

## 24.3 Statevector Simulation

The **statevector simulator** is the workhorse: store the $2^n$ amplitudes in a flat array indexed by basis states, apply each gate as an in-place update, and at measurement sample from $|\alpha_x|^2$ or compute exact expectation values $\langle\psi|O|\psi\rangle$ directly. It is exact (up to floating-point round-off), supports every gate the user can write down, and gives the developer access to internals no real device exposes. Schrödinger's equation is integrated as repeated unitary multiplication — hence the name **Schrödinger simulation** sometimes used in the literature.

The per-gate algorithm is the *gate-as-stride* update. For a single-qubit gate $U$ with entries $a, b, c, d$ (rows $(a, b)$ and $(c, d)$) acting on qubit $q$, partition the amplitude array into pairs $(\alpha_x, \alpha_{x \oplus 2^q})$ where $x$ ranges over basis states with the $q$-th bit clear, and update each pair as

$$
\begin{pmatrix} \alpha_x' \\\\ \alpha_{x \oplus 2^q}' \end{pmatrix} \;=\; \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\\\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \alpha_x \\\\ \alpha_{x \oplus 2^q} \end{pmatrix}.
$$

There are $2^{n-1}$ such pairs and each costs four complex multiplies and two complex adds. Two-qubit gates use the same trick on quadruples. Diagonal gates ($Z$, $S$, $T$, $R_Z$, controlled-phase) skip the pairwise $2\times 2$ transform: each amplitude is multiplied by a phase determined by its own bit pattern — in general still a full sweep, though a controlled-phase touches only amplitudes whose control bits are set, and phase gates can be fused or tracked symbolically. Permutation gates ($X$, CNOT, SWAP) only reshuffle indices and need no floating-point arithmetic — the data movement, or a lazily tracked index remapping, remains.

Memory layout matters. Storing the amplitudes contiguously and arranging gates so the "active" qubit is the lowest-numbered keeps the strides short and is cache-friendly. Simulator preprocessors fuse gates, reorder commuting operations, and transpose qubit layouts to optimize this (hardware transpilers, §23.6, optimize for device topology instead — a different and sometimes opposing objective). High-performance simulators — Qiskit Aer, Cirq's `qsim`, NVIDIA's `cuStateVec`, Intel-QS — variously use loop tiling, SIMD lanes (AVX on x86, NEON on ARM) or GPU threads, gate fusion, and prefetching to approach memory-bandwidth peak on bandwidth-bound kernels.

The practical ceiling is on the order of $n = 33$–$35$ for a high-memory workstation or server and the mid-40s for the largest supercomputers. Reported records need careful reading because they mix representations: the widely cited $\sim$48-qubit Jülich (JUQCS) runs used adaptive 2-byte amplitude encoding (about 0.5 PiB of distributed memory) rather than dense complex128, and other supercomputer results in the 42–46-qubit range vary in precision and workload — Appendix F is where the dated, sourced list belongs, and any specific machine-record pairing quoted from memory should be distrusted. Going much beyond the mid-40s via *dense* simulation is not a question of more compute but of *exponential memory* — and while the wall stands, its practical location creeps: doubling aggregate memory buys exactly one more qubit, and compression, precision, and task-specific representations buy a few more.

What statevector simulation *cannot* do: simulate noise (Section 24.4 is the extension), exploit structure (the cost is the same for a deeply entangled state and a product state), or scale beyond the memory wall. Within those constraints it is the gold standard.

A few lines exercise exactly this access-to-internals (`examples/statevector_simulation.py`): build a GHZ circuit, read the exact amplitudes, and get outcome probabilities with no sampling at all.

```python
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit.quantum_info import Statevector

qc = QuantumCircuit(3)
qc.h(0)
qc.cx(0, 1)
qc.cx(1, 2)  # prepares the 3-qubit GHZ state

sv = Statevector(qc)
for basis, amp in sorted(sv.to_dict().items()):
    print(f"|{basis}>: {amp.real:+.3f}{amp.imag:+.3f}j")
probs = {str(k): float(round(v, 3)) for k, v in sv.probabilities_dict().items()}
print(probs)
```

Output:

```text
|000>: +0.707+0.000j
|111>: +0.707+0.000j
{'000': 0.5, '111': 0.5}
```

## 24.4 Density-Matrix Simulation

When the system is mixed — coupled to an environment, subject to gate errors, decohering over time — the pure-state description fails. The right object is the **density matrix** $\rho$, a $2^n \times 2^n$ positive semidefinite operator with trace $1$ (Chapter 11). Storing $\rho$ in dense form costs $16 \cdot 4^n$ bytes — each added qubit *quadruples* the memory, so the feasible width roughly halves relative to a statevector: $n = 15$ is 16 GiB (workstation), $n = 17$ is 256 GiB (large server), $n = 20$ is 16 TiB (top-cluster territory), and $n = 25$ (16 PiB) is beyond dense simulation on any current machine.

Gate application conjugates the density matrix: $\rho \mapsto U \rho U^{\dagger}$ for a unitary (still the Schrödinger picture — states evolve; in the Heisenberg picture observables would evolve as $O \mapsto U^{\dagger} O U$), and the general **Kraus form** $\rho \mapsto \sum_k K_k \rho K_k^{\dagger}$ with $\sum_k K_k^{\dagger} K_k = I$ for a noise channel. The amplitude-damping, depolarizing, phase-damping, and Pauli-error channels (§10.12) each have explicit Kraus decompositions and are direct to apply. Measurement on a density matrix yields outcome probabilities $\mathrm{tr}(\Pi_k \rho)$ directly, without sampling, when one wants the full distribution.

The price tag — $4^n$ versus $2^n$ — buys exactness against arbitrary noise. The alternative, when one cares about $n$ that exceeds the dense density-matrix ceiling, is **quantum trajectory simulation** (also called Monte Carlo wavefunction simulation, §24.13). It keeps a pure state $|\psi\rangle$ and a list of jump operators $L_k$ derived from the channel; at each step the simulator evolves under an effective non-Hermitian drift and, with state-dependent probability set by the jump operators, applies a normalized jump $L_k|\psi\rangle / \\|L_k|\psi\rangle\\|$ (for discrete circuit noise, the analogous move samples a Kraus operator with state-dependent probability). Averaging the per-trajectory measurement statistics over many trajectories converges to the density-matrix expectation. The per-trajectory cost is $2^n$ rather than $4^n$, at the price of running enough trajectories for the target variance — often hundreds to thousands, though the count is observable- and precision-dependent. Trajectories parallelize embarrassingly and are the right tool when $n$ is large enough that $4^n$ is out of reach.

A special case worth flagging: noise channels that are **mixtures of Pauli operators** — depolarizing, dephasing, biased Pauli noise — can be simulated efficiently when *combined* with stabilizer dynamics (§24.5). The trajectory has stabilizer representation throughout, each jump is a random Pauli, and the cost stays polynomial. This is the structural reason that fault-tolerant simulation (Chapter 19) — Clifford operations with Pauli noise — is computationally tractable on circuits with millions of qubits.

## 24.5 Stabilizer Simulation

The **Gottesman–Knill theorem** (§8.10) is the foundational efficient-classical-simulation result of quantum computing. A circuit composed only of Clifford gates ($H$, $S$, CNOT) acting on a computational-basis input state, followed by computational-basis measurement, can be simulated classically in polynomial time and polynomial space. The reason is that the **stabilizer formalism** describes such states with a list of $n$ commuting Pauli generators, each storable as $2n + 1$ bits (a sign and two bits per qubit for the $\\{I, X, Y, Z\\}$ choice).

The state is represented as the **stabilizer tableau**: a $2n \times (2n+1)$ binary matrix recording the $n$ stabilizers and the $n$ "destabilizers" needed to perform measurements efficiently. The total storage is $O(n^2)$ bits. Each Clifford gate updates the tableau in $O(n)$ time: a Hadamard on qubit $q$ swaps the $X$ and $Z$ bit-columns for that qubit across all rows; CNOT updates the control and target columns with the accompanying phase rules. A computational-basis measurement either yields a deterministic outcome — inferred from the measured Pauli's membership in the stabilizer group — or, when it anticommutes with some stabilizer, a random one, costing $O(n^2)$ for the row operations that update the generators. A full Clifford circuit on $n$ qubits with $g$ gates and $m$ measurements runs in $O(n g + n^2 m)$ time.

The **Aaronson–Gottesman 2004 paper** (often abbreviated CHP, after the simulator) gave the canonical concrete algorithm. Subsequent improvements — *graph-state* representations, *symplectic* tableau forms, and above all the Stim simulator — push the constants down by orders of magnitude. **Stim** (Craig Gidney's simulator) has handled Clifford circuits at the scale of $10^6$ qubits, with throughput on the order of $10^8$ gate operations per second on a laptop for favorable workloads — throughput varies by orders of magnitude with the instruction mix and measurement load, and its bit-packed batch sampling and detector-error-model machinery matter as much as raw tableau speed. This performance is what makes large-scale quantum-error-correction simulation (Chapter 19) practical: surface-code decoder benchmarking with millions of stabilizer measurements per shot is typically bottlenecked by the decoder rather than the simulator.

The boundary of Gottesman–Knill is the non-Clifford gate. A single $T$ gate leaves the Clifford group, and applied to a generic stabilizer state it produces a state outside the stabilizer set. The dynamics is no longer expressible as Pauli conjugation, the tableau has no exact update, and the simulator must either approximate or pay a cost that grows exponentially in the circuit's *magic* — in the worst case exponential in the $T$ count, though cancellation, placement, and structure can make particular $T$-containing circuits cheap. The next section addresses how that cost scales.

## 24.6 Near-Clifford and Magic-State Simulation

When a circuit has $k$ non-Clifford gates inserted into an otherwise-Clifford backbone, the right cost model is "exponential in $k$, polynomial in everything else". The technical mechanism is the **stabilizer decomposition**: any $n$-qubit pure state can be written as a (possibly complex-coefficient) sum of stabilizer states, and the minimum length of such a sum is the **stabilizer rank** $\chi(|\psi\rangle)$. A single magic state $|T\rangle = T|+\rangle$ has stabilizer rank $2$; $k$ copies have stabilizer rank at most $2^{\alpha k}$ with $\alpha \approx 0.4$ for the exact rank ($\alpha \leq 0.3963$, Bravyi et al. 2019; the 2016 Bravyi–Gosset paper gave the $2^{0.23k}$ approximate-rank scaling used for sampling — asymptotic bounds for specific tasks, with approximate-rank runtimes carrying an additional error dependence), beating the naive $2^k$.

A near-Clifford simulator therefore preprocesses every $T$ gate into a teleportation-style consumption of a magic-state ancilla, accumulates the stabilizer-decomposition cost factor, and simulates the underlying Clifford circuit on each term. The total runtime is roughly

$$
T_{\mathrm{sim}}(n, k) \;=\; \mathrm{poly}(n) \cdot 2^{\alpha k}.
$$

What is feasible depends on the exponent, prefactor, task, and accuracy; the published demonstrations give the calibration. Bravyi–Gosset (2016) simulated a 40-qubit hidden-shift circuit with $\sim$50 $T$ gates in hours on a workstation; the 2019 follow-up (Bravyi et al., *Quantum* 3, 181) reached 50 qubits and over 60 non-Clifford gates — scales that would have cost roughly $2^{50}$-plus amplitudes in dense statevector form.

A different family of methods replaces the stabilizer-decomposition sum by a **quasi-probability** representation. The **Pashayan–Wallman–Bartlett 2015** scheme writes every state and every operation in a "phase-space" frame chosen so that the free (stabilizer) sector carries nonnegative quasi-probability — for qubits this takes care, since contextuality obstructs a clean nonnegative picture in some frames — while non-stabilizer resources introduce negativity. The simulator importance-samples trajectories carrying signed weights; the sampling overhead scales with a quantity called the **negativity** or **magic**, which is constant for Clifford circuits and grows multiplicatively with each non-Clifford gate. The cost again is polynomial in $n$ and exponential in the magic.

The two viewpoints — sums of stabilizers and quasi-probability sampling — are closely related resource-theoretic approaches: both charge only polynomial cost for the Clifford scaffolding, and both pay exponentially in the non-Clifford content in their known upper bounds (no matching lower bound forces *every* classical simulator to pay this). Their existence sharpens the sense in which **the T-count is the magic resource of fault-tolerant quantum computing** (§8.10): compile your algorithm with few $T$ gates and these classical simulators reproduce it — so low $T$-count caps what advantage a circuit can offer against these particular methods. A high $T$-count, conversely, defeats these simulators but by itself proves nothing about all classical algorithms — an asymmetry worth keeping straight even as minimizing $T$-count remains the goal for fault-tolerant resource cost.

## 24.7 Tensor-Network Simulation

Tensor networks are the leading method for classically simulating large quantum systems whose entanglement is *limited*. The state is represented as a contracted network of small tensors, one per site, with auxiliary "bond" indices encoding the correlations between sites. The size of each bond — the **bond dimension** $\chi$ — controls the expressive power: bond dimension 1 represents product states; bond dimension $2^{n/2}$ represents an arbitrary state on $n$ qubits. The interesting regime is everything in between.

The general picture is that a tensor network is a compressed factorization of an exponentially large object. A state on $n$ qubits is, in coordinate form, a rank-$n$ tensor with $2^n$ entries; the network expresses it as a contraction of per-site tensors over shared bond indices, each tensor holding $O(\chi^2 \cdot 2)$ entries, with the contraction performed lazily on demand. Storage drops from $2^n$ to $O(n \chi^2)$ when $\chi$ stays small. Time-evolving an MPS keeps the same structure: contract local gates into the relevant site tensors, then re-truncate the bond dimensions to $\chi$ via singular-value decomposition (this contract-and-truncate cycle is the MPS/TEBD algorithm; other network classes — PEPS, general circuit contractions — have their own update and contraction schemes).

The **entanglement entropy** of a subregion is the load-bearing quantity. For a bipartition of the $n$ qubits, the Schmidt decomposition (§4.9) of the state has at most $\chi$ non-zero singular values across the cut, so the **entanglement entropy across that cut is bounded by $\log_2 \chi$**. The simulator's guiding principle runs the other way: exact representation needs $\chi$ at least the **Schmidt rank** across each cut, so if that rank stays $\le 2^S$, bond dimension $\chi = 2^S$ represents the state exactly and simulation is polynomial in $n$ and exponential only in $S$. A bound on the *entanglement entropy* alone is weaker — a concentrated Schmidt spectrum can have low entropy yet high rank — but low entropy does guarantee that truncating to a modest $\chi$ gives an accurate *approximation*, which is what makes area-law states tractable in practice.

The two dominant topologies are **matrix product states (MPS)** for one-dimensional geometries (§24.8) and **projected entangled pair states (PEPS)** for two-dimensional geometries (§24.10). Both come with a substantial software ecosystem — ITensor, TeNPy, Quimb, NVIDIA's `cuTensorNet` — and have been production tools of condensed-matter theory for decades (MPS/DMRG especially; PEPS with more caveats, §24.10). They have also powered several of the strongest classical baselines against quantum-computing benchmarks (§24.14), alongside Schrödinger–Feynman and hybrid contraction methods.

The trade-off is sharp. A circuit that builds *volume-law* entanglement — a deep random circuit is the canonical case — drives the required bond dimension toward its $2^{n/2}$ worst case across balanced cuts, and tensor networks lose their advantage. (Note the contrast with GHZ states: maximally *nonlocal*, yet Schmidt rank 2 across every cut, hence bond dimension 2 and MPS-trivial — entanglement *structure*, not entanglement per se, is what costs.) A circuit that keeps entanglement local — ground states of gapped local 1D Hamiltonians, low-depth circuits on local geometry — stays in the small-$\chi$ regime, where tensor networks excel. (Do not assume *most* near-term circuits qualify: hardware benchmarks and many variational circuits deliberately generate substantial entanglement.)

## 24.8 Matrix Product States and TEBD

A **matrix product state (MPS)** on $n$ qubits writes each amplitude as a product of matrices, one per site:

$$
|\psi\rangle \;=\; \sum_{x_1, \ldots, x_n} A^{(1)}_{x_1} A^{(2)}_{x_2} \cdots A^{(n)}_{x_n} \\, |x_1 x_2 \cdots x_n\rangle,
$$

where each $A^{(i)}_{x_i}$ is a $\chi_{i-1} \times \chi_i$ matrix — bond dimensions vary along the chain up to a maximum $\chi$, with $1 \times \chi_1$ and $\chi_{n-1} \times 1$ boundary tensors so the product collapses to a scalar coefficient. The bond dimension $\chi$ controls the entanglement across each cut between consecutive sites. Storage is $O(n \chi^2)$. Single-site gates update one $A^{(i)}$. Two-site gates acting on adjacent sites $i, i+1$ contract those two tensors, multiply by the gate, then re-split via SVD, truncating any singular values past $\chi$. Two-site gates on non-adjacent sites are handled by SWAP-routing the qubits to adjacency and back — $O(\mathrm{distance})$ SWAPs per gate, up to quadratic total overhead across a circuit, and the entanglement the SWAPs spread can inflate $\chi$, which is the real cost.

**Time-evolving block decimation (TEBD)** is the MPS-native algorithm for simulating time evolution $|\psi(t)\rangle = e^{-iHt}|\psi(0)\rangle$ of a one-dimensional Hamiltonian $H = \sum_i h_{i,i+1}$ with nearest-neighbor interactions. It Trotterizes the exponential into a product of small two-site gates (§16.2 for the Trotter machinery), applies each gate via the SVD-and-truncate step above, and walks a "brick-wall" pattern of even-bond and odd-bond gates across the chain. The cost per timestep is $O(n \chi^3)$, dominated by the SVD. Variants — second-order Trotter, higher-order Suzuki splitting, TDVP (time-dependent variational principle) — control the Trotter error or the bond-dimension truncation error in different ways.

The regime in which TEBD wins is *one-dimensional systems where the entanglement growth is bounded*. The **area law** for ground states of gapped one-dimensional Hamiltonians (Hastings 2007) says the entanglement entropy across any cut is $O(1)$, so a fixed $\chi$ — often tens to a few hundred — suffices for highly accurate ground-state simulation (the accuracy at a given $\chi$ is model- and observable-dependent; "chemical accuracy" is a molecular-energy convention that does not transfer to spin chains). **Quench dynamics** out of a gapped state commonly exhibit **linear entanglement growth** $S(t) \sim t$ (Calabrese–Cardy analyzed the CFT quench settings; integrable, localized, and constrained systems can behave differently), so the required $\chi$ grows exponentially in time. TEBD is fast and accurate at short times and becomes infeasible at long times — an exponential resource wall entered rapidly, if not at one sharp instant.

MPS has also become a credible back-end for *quantum-circuit* simulation when the circuit's entanglement is locally bounded — random circuits with low depth, variational ansätze with limited entangler layers, and adiabatic preparations near the gap. NVIDIA's `cuQuantum` and Quimb both ship MPS back-ends that handle hundreds of qubits at modest $\chi$.

## 24.9 DMRG and Ground-State Methods

The **density matrix renormalization group (DMRG)**, introduced by Steven White in 1992, predates the modern MPS reformulation but is, in modern language, an optimization over the MPS manifold. The target is a ground state of a one-dimensional or quasi-1D Hamiltonian $H$ (matrix-product-operator representations handle longer-range terms and quantum-chemistry orbital chains). The algorithm sweeps left-to-right and right-to-left across the chain; in the common two-site variant it solves a local eigenvalue problem (Lanczos or Davidson on the effective Hamiltonian of a two-site block), splits the optimized block tensor by SVD with truncation, and proceeds to the next site (one-site variants update differently).

DMRG converges rapidly — often geometrically in the sweep count — on well-conditioned gapped 1D problems; that is an algorithmic observation, not a theorem, and sweeps can stall in metastable states depending on initialization and symmetry sectors. It is the dominant method for ground states of one-dimensional quantum magnets, fermionic chains, and a wide class of quasi-1D ladders. On favorable models, energies converge to relative precision $\sim 10^{-10}$ at bond dimensions $\chi \le 1000$ for chains of hundreds of sites on a workstation — figures that vary by model and boundary conditions, and a tiny energy error does not guarantee equally accurate correlation functions. The Heisenberg, $t$-$J$, Hubbard, and SSH models — the workhorse Hamiltonians of condensed-matter theory — have all been mapped extensively by DMRG, with hard regimes remaining.

The method extends to two-dimensional systems by mapping the 2D lattice onto a 1D snake path, at the cost of exponentially-growing bond dimension in the *narrower* spatial direction. **DMRG on cylinders of width $w$** in the snake encoding requires bond dimension growing exponentially in $w$, with a model-dependent rate. In practice widths $w \approx 8$–$12$ are reachable (budget-dependent) — enough to address frustrated-magnet phase questions and, for some models and observables, to support controlled extrapolation toward 2D. Where the width wall binds, PEPS (§24.10), variational Monte Carlo, and — for sign-free models — quantum Monte Carlo are the complements.

The bridge to quantum computing is that DMRG is, today, one of the strongest classical baselines that quantum-chemistry and quantum-simulation algorithms must beat — alongside coupled cluster, selected CI, quantum Monte Carlo, DMFT, and tensor-network relatives. A VQE circuit estimating the ground state of a Hubbard chain competes against DMRG on the same Hamiltonian (mind the counting: a 50-site Hubbard model carries $\sim$100 spin-orbitals, i.e. $\sim$100 qubits under the common encodings, not 50). The *interesting* quantum-simulation regimes are those where DMRG's bond-dimension demand grows toward infeasibility — typically 2D or highly connected systems, or excited-state spectroscopy, where orthogonality constraints and root-tracking add cost.

## 24.10 PEPS and Variational Tensor Methods

A **projected entangled pair state (PEPS)** is the two-dimensional generalization of MPS. Each site of a 2D lattice carries a tensor with four bond indices in the bulk (three on edges, two at corners), one to each neighbor. The state is the contraction of the full 2D network. The bond dimension $\chi$ again controls the entanglement entropy across each cut, but now an arbitrary cut crosses $O(L)$ bonds for an $L \times L$ lattice, so the entanglement *across any straight cut* is $\le L \log_2 \chi$ — an area-law *capacity* matching the scaling of gapped 2D ground states (a bound the state need not saturate; and efficient PEPS approximability of all gapped 2D ground states is not a proven theorem).

The catch is that contracting a 2D tensor network is itself an exponentially hard problem in the worst case — it is the *partition function* of a classical statistical-mechanics problem, formally #P-hard. PEPS algorithms therefore rely on approximate contraction schemes: **boundary MPS** (compress the contraction one row at a time), **corner transfer matrix**, **tensor renormalization group**. Each scheme introduces an auxiliary bond dimension $\chi_b$ controlling the contraction accuracy. PEPS calculations are far more expensive than analogous MPS calculations — the gap depends on model, bond dimensions, and algorithm — and require careful convergence checks in two parameters $(\chi, \chi_b)$, on top of optimization and contraction bias.

Despite the cost, PEPS is a leading contender for **frustrated 2D magnets**, **finite-temperature 2D systems** (via purification and PEPO variants), and **2D fermionic systems** (Hubbard model on a square lattice) — sharing each field with DMRG cylinders, quantum Monte Carlo where sign-free, variational Monte Carlo, and tensor-renormalization methods. It has produced state-of-the-art results on high-$T_c$ pairing structure, kagome spin liquids, and topological phases — contested territory where no single method owns the answer. Quantum simulators that promise advantage on these problems are competing against PEPS calculations whose state of the art is itself a fast-moving target.

A complementary family of methods replaces the geometric ansatz with a flexible **variational neural-network state**: parameterize $\langle x|\psi\rangle$ (amplitude and phase) as the output of a neural network and minimize $\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle$ by variational Monte Carlo, typically with stochastic-reconfiguration or natural-gradient updates. Popularized by Carleo and Troyer in 2017, neural-network quantum states (NQS) have matched — and on some reported benchmarks exceeded — tensor-network accuracy on 2D Heisenberg and fermionic systems, with the caveat that such comparisons are sensitive to compute budgets and lack certified error bars. NQS sits in a different region of the design space — heavier compute, architecture and sampling bias in place of explicit entanglement geometry, no formal area-law guarantee — and which method dominates which regime is still open.

## 24.11 Schrödinger–Feynman and Hybrid Path-Sum Methods

A single output amplitude of a quantum circuit can also be computed by **summing over paths**, in direct Feynman-path-integral style. For a circuit $U = U_d \cdots U_2 U_1$ on $n$ qubits, the amplitude $\langle y | U | x\rangle$ is

$$
\langle y | U | x\rangle \;=\; \sum_{z_1, \ldots, z_{d-1}} \langle y | U_d | z_{d-1}\rangle \cdots \langle z_2 | U_2 | z_1 \rangle \langle z_1 | U_1 | x\rangle,
$$

where each intermediate $z_t$ ranges over $2^n$ basis states. Naively this is $2^{n(d-1)}$ — far worse than statevector simulation. The point is locality: when $U_t$ is a single gate on $k$ qubits (single- and two-qubit gates have $k \le 2$), the matrix element $\langle z_t | U_t | z_{t-1} \rangle$ vanishes unless $z_t$ agrees with $z_{t-1}$ outside those $k$ bits, so the sum branches only over the gated bits — though a full *layer* of parallel gates branches on every qubit it touches, and the sum decomposes into local pieces accordingly.

The **Schrödinger–Feynman hybrid simulator** (Markov, Boixo, Smelyanskiy, and others, 2017–2020) exploits this for circuits with low depth on grids of qubits. Partition the qubits into a small number of *blocks*; within each block, run a Schrödinger (statevector) simulator; between blocks, sum over the path contributions on the cutting bonds. The cost per block is its statevector — $2^{n/B}$ for $B$ equal blocks — times the number of path configurations across the cut, which is set by the operator Schmidt rank of each crossing gate (up to $4$ per two-qubit gate) compounded over the gates that cross, and shaped by slicing and reuse strategy. For a shallow circuit on a 2D grid with few entangling gates spanning the block boundaries, this can be a dramatic win.

A more recent reformulation views the entire calculation as a **tensor network contraction** with the circuit's gates as the tensors and the qubit world-lines as the bonds. The optimal contraction order — which intermediate tensors to form first — is itself an NP-hard problem (treewidth) but admits very effective heuristics. **Pan, Chen, and Zhang 2022** and follow-up work used this view, together with massive GPU parallelism, to drop the believed cost of simulating the Sycamore 2019 sampling benchmark from 10,000 years to hours-to-days on a GPU cluster (about 15 hours in the headline configuration — a comparison across different hardware, sample fidelities, and task accounting, not a like-for-like rerun). The general lesson: any quantum-advantage claim that targets a specific output statistic — a marginal, a cross-entropy score — rather than the full output distribution may be vulnerable to a contraction of just the relevant slice; it is one vulnerability among several (noise exploitation is another, §24.14).

## 24.12 GPU Acceleration and Distributed Simulation

Much of modern statevector simulation runs on GPUs. A data-center GPU of the H100 class carries tens of GiB of HBM at TB/s-scale bandwidth (exact figures vary by SKU) — of the order of, and often beyond, the RAM bandwidth of a whole multi-socket CPU node. The gate-as-stride update (§24.3) maps naturally onto GPU SIMT: each gate launches a kernel that processes pairs (or quadruples for two-qubit gates) in parallel across thousands of threads. **NVIDIA `cuStateVec`** (part of `cuQuantum`), **Google `qsim`** (the back-end behind Cirq's heavy simulations), and **Qulacs** are among the most heavily optimized statevector simulators (qsim and Qulacs also run on CPUs); on bandwidth-bound kernels they approach memory-bandwidth peak and typically beat CPU equivalents at 30+ qubits.

Multi-GPU and distributed simulation extend the reach in width. The statevector is partitioned across $P$ devices, each holding $2^n / P$ amplitudes. Gates on the "local" qubits — those whose index falls inside a single partition — run independently per device. Gates on the "global" qubits — those whose flip would cross partitions — require exchanging partner chunks between ranks, typically as pairwise peer-to-peer transfers or a qubit-layout transpose rather than literal all-to-all of half the data. Scaling thus depends on the *interconnect*, not just FLOPs: NVLink within a node and InfiniBand between nodes are the bottleneck. The large distributed records — the $\sim$48-qubit compressed-encoding runs and the 40+-qubit supercomputer simulations reported on various systems (Appendix F for the dated list) — all pay this communication tax.

Mid-sized simulations (roughly 30–32 qubits) on a single large-memory GPU are the *practical* sweet spot for the algorithm developer. A 30-qubit complex128 statevector is 16 GiB — filling a 16 GiB card with no headroom, comfortable on larger cards — and well-optimized GPU runs finish typical circuits quickly. Speedups over CPU are real but often overstated: the popular "thousandfold" figures compare against unoptimized CPU baselines, while against a well-used modern CPU node a bandwidth-bound kernel gains roughly the bandwidth ratio — an order of magnitude, give or take. The same code paths — `cuStateVec` for statevectors, `cuTensorNet` for tensor networks, the cuQuantum density-matrix component — interoperate with Qiskit, Cirq, and PennyLane via plugins and packages, with modest integration effort (device, precision, and feature constraints apply).

## 24.13 Quantum-Noise Simulators

A noise simulator's job is to predict what a *real* quantum device — finite-fidelity gates, $T_1$/$T_2$ decoherence, leakage, readout error — produces, not what an idealized circuit produces. Three architectures coexist.

**Full density-matrix simulation** (§24.4) is exact *for the supplied channel model* — non-Markovian memory needs process-tensor machinery beyond it, and leakage enlarges the local dimension past $4^n$ — and pays $4^n$ memory: practical to $n \approx 15$–16 on a workstation and to $n \approx 20$ only with top-cluster resources (§24.4). Qiskit Aer's density-matrix method and NVIDIA's density-matrix library component serve this regime.

**Trajectory simulation** keeps a pure state and applies stochastic jumps drawn from the channel's Kraus decomposition. The simulator is, per trajectory, no more expensive than statevector simulation. Averaging over $N$ trajectories converges to density-matrix expectation values with standard error $\propto 1/\sqrt{N}$ (the constant is the per-trajectory variance, which depends on the observable and the unraveling). Where the break-even against a dense density matrix falls depends on gate costs, Kraus ranks, observables, and parallelism — there is no universal $N \approx 2^n$ rule — but well before $n = 25$ the $4^n$ object is untouchable, and beyond it trajectories share the field with tensor-network, stabilizer, and Pauli-propagation noise methods. Qiskit Aer's noise back-end and Cirq's `Simulator`-with-noise path use trajectories for large $n$ (PennyLane's `default.mixed`, by contrast, is a full density-matrix simulator).

**Pauli-error / stabilizer-noise simulation** is the special case where every noise channel is a mixture of Pauli operators and every gate is Clifford. The state stays within the stabilizer formalism, jumps are random Paulis with known probabilities, and the per-trajectory cost is polynomial — $O(n)$ to $O(n^2)$ per event depending on the algorithm and workload — rather than $O(2^n)$. This is the simulator regime for **fault-tolerant error-correction studies**: surface codes, color codes, and Floquet codes on lattices of thousands to millions of qubits, where the only computationally interesting events are syndromes and decoder runs. **Stim** is the most widely used tool here; the surface-code threshold curves quoted in Chapter 19 come from circuit/noise/decoder studies built on such simulators together with decoders and analysis.

A subtler point: classical simulation of *noisy* quantum circuits can be **exponentially easier** than simulation of noiseless ones, in a precise sense. Threshold-era arguments and later sampling-specific results give this precise form: above sufficient constant-rate depolarizing noise, the output of an *uncorrected* circuit converges toward easily sampled distributions (fault tolerance below threshold is exactly the escape from this fate), and for noisy IQP and random-circuit sampling there are quantitative simulability thresholds depending on depth and noise placement (the exact attributions and statements belong to the factcheck layer — the theorems are narrower than the folklore versions). The practical consequence for hardware engineers (§24.14): driving the error rate down is not just an engineering goal — it is load-bearing for any advantage claim that must survive improving classical simulators.

## 24.14 The Quantum-Advantage Frontier

The phrase **quantum (computational) advantage** — the more cautious successor to "quantum supremacy" — names a specific genre of demonstration: a quantum device performs some sampling task in time $T_Q$ that the best known classical simulator needs time $T_C \gg T_Q$ to match. The 2019 Google **Sycamore** result reported $T_Q \approx 200$ s on a 53-qubit, depth-20 random circuit, against a then-estimated $T_C \approx 10\\,000$ years on Summit using a Schrödinger–Feynman simulator. The 2020 and 2021 USTC **Jiuzhang** and **Jiuzhang 2.0** photonic experiments made analogous claims for boson sampling. Subsequent USTC **Zuchongzhi**-series experiments — random-circuit sampling on successively larger superconducting devices — refreshed the gap (Appendix F tracks the specific devices, active qubit counts, and dates).

The *classical* side of the frontier has not been static. Three lines of advance have eroded each generation of claim.

**Tensor-network contractions on GPU clusters.** **Pan, Chen, and Zhang 2022** rephrased the Sycamore benchmark as a tensor-network contraction, found a highly effective contraction order, executed it on a few thousand GPUs, and reported a simulation in roughly 15 hours — more than six orders of magnitude below the original 10,000-year estimate, comparing across different hardware and task accounting. Follow-up work extended the technique to Zuchongzhi-class benchmarks. The general moral is that low-depth, two-dimensional circuit *geometry* keeps the contraction treewidth manageable: random-circuit sampling generates plenty of entanglement, but contraction structure, not entanglement per se, is what the tensor networks exploit.

**Massive distributed simulation.** The 2021 Gordon Bell prize-winning computation — a *tensor-network* simulation, not a dense statevector — of a 53-qubit Sycamore-class circuit on the new-generation Sunway supercomputer, using tens of millions of (lightweight, architecture-specific) cores, reported $T_C \approx 304$ s — within a small factor of the device's wall-clock time, though with different sample counts, fidelities, and energy budgets than the experiment; resource parity is part of any honest comparison. **NVIDIA `cuStateVec`** and (before its 2023 wind-down) Alibaba's simulator effort demonstrated 40+ qubit statevector simulations at competitive walltimes.

**Noise-aware classical algorithms.** When the experimental circuit's noise level is high enough — noise is always present on NISQ-era devices, though not always high enough for this mechanism to bite under the relevant accuracy metric — the output distribution approaches a low-fidelity mixture that is itself easier to sample. **Gao, Kalinowski, Chou, Lukin, Barak, and Choi** (the linear-XEB-spoofing line, published 2024) and related work have shown that some advantage benchmarks' *scores* can be matched by classical algorithms exploiting the device's noise — and matching an XEB score is not the same as reproducing the target distribution, which is exactly why benchmark design matters.

The take-away is not that quantum advantage has been disproven. The take-away is that **the advantage gap is set by the joint optimum over (a) device fidelity and qubit count, (b) classical simulator technique, and (c) the choice of benchmark**, and improvements in any one factor reshape the gap. For an honest assessment of any current advantage claim, one needs the most recent classical-simulator result on the *same* circuit family — sometimes published only months later.

Forward-looking: many classical-simulation specialists expect **sampling-based advantage** on random circuits to remain under sustained pressure from tensor-network and noise-exploiting simulators — an expectation, not a settled consensus or a theorem; scale, depth, and fidelity can outrun known methods. **Advantage on tasks with verifiable output** — Shor's factoring, precision ground-state energies — is a different game: verifiability closes the score-spoofing loophole, though it does not by itself make the classical side weak (factoring has powerful specialized classical algorithms, and ground-state verification carries its own difficulties). The field's center of gravity moves toward computations that *do something* beyond being hard to sample.

## 24.15 Choosing a Simulator

The decision tree below picks out a sensible first tool for a given problem. Read it top to bottom — but treat it as triage, not law: branches overlap, and the real first question is what *output* you need (full state, amplitudes, samples, observables) at what error and with what hardware; that often decides the method before qubit count does.

**Is the circuit Clifford-only?** Use a stabilizer simulator (Stim; CHP is the historical lineage). Cost is polynomial; QEC-style workloads with millions of qubits are feasible (enumerating a full output distribution stays exponential regardless).

**Is the circuit Clifford + a small number of $T$ gates (tens is the right order — the workable count depends on task, accuracy, and hardware)?** Use a near-Clifford approach (stabilizer-decomposition and quasi-probability methods à la Bravyi–Gosset — methods more than turnkey tools; look for maintained implementations). Cost is polynomial in $n$, exponential in the magic.

**Is the qubit count $n \le 30$ and the noise model trivial (or only Clifford-friendly)?** Use a workstation statevector simulator (Qiskit Aer, `qsim`, Qulacs). Cost $O(g \\, 2^n)$ for $g$ gates; small circuits finish in seconds, while deep or gradient-heavy workloads take proportionally longer, and $n = 30$ at complex128 already wants 16 GiB plus headroom.

**Is the qubit count just past workstation scale?** Use a GPU statevector simulator (`cuStateVec`) — the same algorithm with large constant-factor gains, bounded by card memory: a single 80 GiB device holds bare complex128 only through $n = 32$; beyond that means multi-GPU, reduced precision, or distributed CPU.

**Is the noise model rich and $n$ small enough for $4^n$ — roughly $n \le 14$–$16$ locally, more only with distributed resources?** Use a density-matrix simulator: exact for the supplied channel model.

**Is the noise model rich and $n$ beyond density-matrix reach?** Use a trajectory simulator, choosing the sample count from the target variance rather than a fixed number (hundreds to thousands is common). Cost is $N$ times the statevector cost; trajectories parallelize well.

**Is the system one-dimensional and low-entanglement (ground state of a gapped 1D Hamiltonian, or shallow 1D circuit)?** Use DMRG for ground states, TEBD for time evolution. Cost $O(n \chi^3)$ per TEBD timestep or DMRG sweep, with $\chi$ set by the entanglement.

**Is the system two-dimensional and low-entanglement?** PEPS is a leading option (alongside DMRG cylinders and variational or quantum Monte Carlo). Cost is much higher than MPS; converge in both $\chi$ and the contraction-auxiliary bond dimension.

**Is the circuit low-depth on a 2D grid, and only a few output amplitudes are wanted?** Use the Schrödinger–Feynman and tensor-contraction approaches (in software: contraction-path optimizers like `cotengra` over `cuTensorNet` or `quimb`). Cost depends on the cut/treewidth structure and slicing, not on depth alone.

**Is the circuit deep, with high-entanglement growth, no exploitable structure, and $n > 35$?** Check the "no exploitable structure" part first — Clifford dominance, low magic, symmetries, free-fermion form, heavy noise, or an observable-only task can each rescue tractability. If nothing applies, no *general-purpose* simulator handles this well: it is the candidate-advantage regime. Treat the classical baseline as an open research question — best-effort bounds, restricted checks, scaling studies — rather than declaring it infeasible; a simulator's failure is not yet a device's success.

The implicit message of the tree is that **classical simulation is plural**: there is no single best simulator, and each method's regime is governed by a physical quantity — entanglement structure, magic, noise rate, contraction treewidth, dimensionality — with empirical, gradually degrading boundaries, rather than by qubit count alone. The competent quantum-software engineer knows which tool to reach for and which lever — fidelity, structure, depth, or sheer hardware — will move the boundary in their favor.

**Sanity checks.**

1. A 35-qubit statevector simulation runs out of memory on a 256 GiB workstation. What is the minimum memory needed, and why does adding *one* more qubit roughly *quadruple* it for a density-matrix simulator (while a statevector simulator needs two more qubits for the same factor)?
2. A circuit consists of 100 Clifford gates and 12 $T$ gates on 40 qubits. Which simulator family is the natural first candidate, and what *scaling* does its cost follow? (A runtime cannot be estimated from these numbers alone — task, target error, and implementation all enter. The point: 40 qubits with little magic is cheap in the right family and hopeless in the dense one.)
3. You are simulating the dynamics of a 1D Heisenberg chain after a global quench (an abrupt change of the Hamiltonian's parameters). TEBD with bond dimension $\chi = 200$ matches the exact result to four digits at time $t = 1$, but disagrees at the second digit by $t = 10$. Why, and what does the entanglement entropy across the central cut look like?
4. A vendor claims their 60-qubit device performs random-circuit sampling 1000× faster than the best classical simulator. Three months later, a tensor-network contraction reproduces the same task in comparable wall-clock time on a GPU cluster. Which properties of the benchmark and of the comparison made this classical pushback possible — and what would you need to check (sample counts, fidelities, hardware and energy budgets) before calling the two runs equivalent?
5. You want to verify that a 25-qubit transpiled circuit implements the same unitary as its source. Statevector simulation of both circuits gives matching output distributions over 1024 shots. Is this verification trustworthy? (Distinguish matching histograms from equal unitaries: one input state and finite sampling probe a sliver of the unitary, and global phase, layout permutations, and measurement semantics complicate "same unitary" further. What stronger checks exist at 25 qubits?)
