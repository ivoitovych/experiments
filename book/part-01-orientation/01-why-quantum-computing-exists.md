# Chapter 1. Why Quantum Computing Exists

> **Status:** prereviewed · **Phase:** 6 · **Sections drafted:** 7 / 7

[← Previous: Historical Prelude](../part-00-historical-prelude/00-historical-prelude.md) · [Table of Contents](../../README.md) · [Next: Chapter 2 →](02-classical-to-quantum-contrast.md)

Quantum computing is not a faster classical computer. It is a different machine model — one whose state lives in a complex vector space of dimension exponential in the number of qubits, whose evolution is governed by unitary transformations, and whose output is sampled from a probability distribution determined by interference of amplitudes. Why does such a model exist at all, why do governments and large companies pour money into building hardware for it, and why should an experienced developer spend the time to understand it? This chapter answers those questions before any formalism is introduced. It is the only chapter in the book that is allowed to gesture; every subsequent chapter pays the gesture back with definitions, proofs, or concrete circuits.

The argument is historical in shape but engineering in motivation. Four papers between 1982 and 1996 turned quantum computing from a curiosity into a field. The first asked whether classical computers can efficiently simulate quantum systems and gave a structural reason the answer is no. The second defined a universal quantum computer and showed that a quantum machine can do things — not many things, but specific things — that no classical machine of comparable size can do efficiently. The third gave a polynomial-time quantum algorithm for the integer-factoring and discrete-logarithm problems on which today's public-key cryptography rests — undermining those schemes in principle, once a sufficiently large fault-tolerant machine exists. The fourth gave a generic but milder speedup for unstructured search. Together they define a research programme that is still running, with hardware that as of 2025–2026 is genuinely starting to demonstrate the first two regimes and is still years to decades from realising the third at scale.

> **How to read this chapter.** This is the only purely motivational chapter in the book. There is no mathematics in it that has not been seen, in some form, by anyone who has read a popular account of quantum computing — what changes here is the emphasis. §1.1 sets up the structural reason the field exists (Feynman's exponential cost of classical simulation); §1.2 demarcates what quantum computing is *not*; §1.3 and §1.4 separate the problem classes where quantum genuinely helps from those where it does not; §1.5 distinguishes provable advantage from end-to-end utility; §1.6 places us on the 2025–2026 hardware timeline. Reading this chapter before Chapter 2 lets you tell yourself, repeatedly, why the formalism we are about to develop is worth the effort.

## 1.1 Limits of Classical Computing

The cleanest origin point for quantum computing as a research field is Richard Feynman's 1982 lecture *Simulating Physics with Computers*. Feynman's question was prosaic: can a classical computer efficiently simulate the dynamics of a quantum system, say a molecule with $n$ interacting electrons or a magnetic lattice with $n$ spins? His answer was that, in general, no — and that the obstruction is not a software engineering question but a structural one.

The state of an $n$-qubit quantum system is described by a vector in $\mathbb{C}^{2^n}$. For $n = 50$ this is $2^{50} \approx 10^{15}$ complex amplitudes, already at the edge of what the largest supercomputers can hold in memory. For $n = 300$ it is more amplitudes than there are atoms in the observable universe. A classical simulator that stores the full state vector and applies a unitary as a matrix-vector product needs memory and time exponential in $n$. Approximate methods — tensor networks, quantum Monte Carlo, density-matrix renormalisation — work well for systems with limited entanglement, low temperature, or specific structure, and Chapter 24 surveys them honestly. But none of them is a polynomial-time general-purpose simulator, and for generic interacting quantum dynamics there is good evidence that none can exist under standard complexity assumptions.

Feynman's structural observation was that the natural hardware for simulating a quantum system is *another quantum system*. If the state space scales as $2^n$ for $n$ qubits of quantum simulator just as it scales as $2^n$ for the $n$-qubit target, no exponential blowup occurs. The simulator absorbs the exponential structurally rather than fighting it numerically. This is the original motivation for building a quantum computer: not to crack codes, not to speed up search, but to simulate quantum chemistry, materials, and condensed matter at the scale where classical methods fail. We will return to this in Chapter 28; it remains, in 2026, the application most likely to yield a practical near-term advantage.

David Deutsch's 1985 paper *Quantum Theory, the Church–Turing Principle and the Universal Quantum Computer* gave Feynman's idea its first formal expression. Deutsch defined a universal quantum computer — a quantum analogue of the Turing machine — and asked the now-standard question: which functions can such a machine compute efficiently that no probabilistic Turing machine can? He gave the first concrete example: a one-bit problem (the Deutsch problem of §14.1) on which a quantum algorithm uses one oracle query where any deterministic classical algorithm needs two. The speedup is tiny; the conceptual content is enormous. Deutsch had shown that the **quantum query complexity** of an explicit problem is strictly lower than its classical query complexity. The field had its first separation.

The two structural facts at the foundation of everything that follows: the state space of $n$ qubits has dimension $2^n$, and operations on it are unitary transformations in $\mathbb{C}^{2^n}$. The first sentence is why classical simulation is hard. The second is why quantum computation is not "classical computation plus randomness" — the amplitudes are complex numbers, they add and cancel, and the cancellation is what produces speedups. We will introduce the formalism in Chapter 4 and the mechanics in Chapter 10; for now, the slogan is **interference, not parallelism**, and we will earn it.

## 1.2 What Quantum Computing Is and Is Not

A great deal of misunderstanding in this field comes from a single sentence repeated in popular accounts: *"a quantum computer tries all $2^n$ possibilities at once."* Taken literally, this sentence is false in a way that obscures every interesting fact about how quantum algorithms work. A quantum computer does prepare a superposition over $2^n$ basis states, and a single application of a unitary $U$ does act on every component of that superposition. But measurement returns a single bit string sampled from a probability distribution. The exponential number of "parallel evaluations" collapses to one outcome, and an arbitrary classical query would have done as well unless something is done in between to make the amplitudes for wrong answers cancel.

That "something in between" is **interference**. A well-designed quantum algorithm arranges for amplitudes leading to incorrect outputs to add destructively, and amplitudes leading to correct outputs to add constructively, before the squared modulus is taken to give the measurement probability. The exponential state space is the medium; the structured cancellation pattern is the algorithm; the measurement is the readout. Chapter 10 develops this picture properly, and Chapter 13 uses it as the organising principle for every algorithm in Part 6. For this chapter, hold the two-word version: interference, not parallelism.

It follows that a quantum computer is not a faster classical computer for arbitrary problems. There is no known quantum algorithm that gives a generic speedup for compilation, ordinary databases, image processing, training large neural networks, video encoding, or any of the workloads that dominate modern compute spending. The speedups that *are* known are tied to specific structure: a periodicity that the quantum Fourier transform exposes, a symmetry that phase kickback exploits, a Hamiltonian that the system itself naturally evolves under. When the structure is absent, the best a quantum computer offers — provably — is the quadratic speedup of Grover (§1.3 and Chapter 15), and even that is often eaten by error-correction overhead before it pays off in practice.

Two further negatives are worth stating now to spare confusion later. A quantum computer does **not** violate the Church–Turing thesis: anything computable on a quantum machine is also computable on a classical one, just sometimes much more slowly. The thesis at stake is the *extended* Church–Turing thesis, which posits that classical and quantum computation are polynomially equivalent; the existence of efficient quantum factoring (Shor) is the standard reason to disbelieve it, although no proof of separation is known. A quantum computer also does **not** solve $\mathrm{NP}$-complete problems in polynomial time as far as anyone knows. The best general result on $\mathrm{NP}$ — Grover's search — is a quadratic speedup over brute force, not the exponential improvement an efficient $\mathrm{NP}$ solver would give. The widespread belief that $\mathrm{NP} \not\subseteq \mathrm{BQP}$ is supported by oracle results and by the absence of any candidate algorithm; Chapter 17 covers the complexity-theoretic side carefully.

What a quantum computer *is*, then: a physical device that prepares states in a complex vector space of dimension $2^n$, applies unitary transformations to them, and samples from probability distributions on the output. The states obey the postulates of quantum mechanics (Chapter 5); the gates form a universal set under composition (Chapter 8); the measurements project onto an orthonormal basis (Chapter 11). Everything else is consequence.

## 1.3 Problem Classes Where Quantum Helps

Four papers in the 1990s converted Feynman and Deutsch's structural arguments into concrete algorithmic stakes.

**Shor 1994.** Peter Shor's algorithm factors an $n$-bit integer in time polynomial in $n$, specifically $O(n^3)$ in the original analysis with later improvements. The best known classical algorithm — the general number field sieve — runs in time roughly $\exp(O(n^{1/3} \log^{2/3} n))$, super-polynomial in $n$. The same algorithmic idea solves the discrete logarithm problem in polynomial time. These two problems — factoring and discrete log — are the hardness assumptions underwriting RSA, Diffie–Hellman key exchange, and elliptic-curve cryptography. A sufficiently large fault-tolerant quantum computer breaks all of them. The cryptanalytic stakes are why the field has the funding profile it does, and why the standards bodies (NIST in particular) have been running a multi-year competition to standardise *post-quantum cryptography* — classical algorithms believed to resist quantum attack. Chapter 15 covers Shor in detail; Chapter 27 covers the cryptanalytic and post-quantum landscape.

**Grover 1996.** Lov Grover's algorithm finds a marked element in an unstructured list of $N$ items using $O(\sqrt{N})$ queries to a function that recognises the marked element. Classically, $\Theta(N)$ queries are necessary. The speedup is quadratic — not exponential — but the algorithm is generic: any search problem expressible as "find an $x$ such that $f(x) = 1$" inherits the $\sqrt{N}$ behaviour. Combined with classical reductions, this gives quadratic speedups for SAT, graph problems, and a swathe of optimisation primitives. The catch, mentioned above, is that the quadratic factor often does not survive fault-tolerant overhead: an $O(\sqrt{N})$ quantum algorithm whose every gate is wrapped in error correction may end up slower in wall-clock time than the $O(N)$ classical algorithm on real hardware for any $N$ short of astronomical. Chapter 15 covers Grover, and the realistic-speedup discussion appears in Chapter 25.

**Quantum simulation.** The Feynman programme — using a quantum machine to simulate quantum systems — has been formalised into a family of algorithms (Trotterisation, qubitisation, linear-combination-of-unitaries, quantum signal processing) with provable polynomial-time guarantees for simulating local Hamiltonians. Where classical simulation costs exponential time in system size for generic interacting systems, quantum simulation runs in time polynomial in system size, simulation time, and the inverse of the desired error. The application areas — quantum chemistry (binding energies, reaction rates), materials science (high-temperature superconductivity, magnetic phases), and nuclear and high-energy physics — are the places where a useful quantum advantage is most likely to land first, and where the requirements (logical qubit counts in the hundreds to thousands, gate fidelities sufficient for moderate-depth circuits) are most plausibly within a decade's reach. Chapters 16 and 28 develop the algorithms and applications respectively.

Beyond these three settled categories sits a fourth, more speculative one: **optimisation and machine learning**. Quantum approximate optimisation (QAOA), quantum annealing, variational quantum eigensolvers (VQE), and various quantum machine learning proposals have been pursued vigorously since around 2014. The honest 2026 status: no convincing demonstration of practical quantum advantage in this category, several formerly-advertised exponential speedups deflated to polynomial or zero after classical dequantisation results (notably Tang's 2018 work on recommendation systems and subsequent extensions to linear-system solvers), and ongoing research that may yet produce a clear advantage — or may not. Chapters 29 and 30 are honest about both the appeal and the absence of guarantees.

A useful taxonomy of provable advantages, sized by speedup:

**Exponential.** Shor (factoring, discrete log), abelian hidden subgroup, quantum simulation of generic local Hamiltonians, certain forms of linear-system solving under restrictive input-access assumptions (HHL). All are believed, none is unconditionally proven, but each rests on conjectures that complexity theorists are confident in.

**Polynomial (Grover-type and quantum-walk).** Grover and amplitude amplification (quadratic); quantum walks for element distinctness ($O(N^{2/3})$ against classical $\Theta(N)$) and collision finding ($O(N^{1/3})$ against the classical birthday bound $O(N^{1/2})$) — polynomial but *not* quadratic; and $\sqrt{n}$-style speedups for graph problems. Unconditionally provable in the query model; survival to the gate model depends on overhead.

**Speculative.** Optimisation heuristics, near-term variational algorithms, quantum machine learning. The advantage exists in specific narrowly scoped cases at most, and the boundary moves as classical dequantisation work continues. Caveat emptor.

## 1.4 Problem Classes Where Quantum Does Not Help

The complement of §1.3 is more important than newcomers expect. Quantum computers do not help with the bulk of everyday computation. They do not speed up running a web server, compiling source code, training a transformer on text, rendering a video frame, sorting a list of $n$ items below $O(n \log n)$, multiplying two $n$-bit integers below the best known classical bound, or any of the workloads dominating the world's data-centre compute spend. They do not speed up reading a file from disk, sending packets over a network, or executing operating-system kernel code. None of these tasks has the structure that quantum algorithms exploit, and there is no candidate quantum algorithm that gives a generic speedup for unstructured polynomial-time computation.

The structural reason is the same shape as the positive reason. Quantum speedups come from interference patterns engineered to exploit specific algebraic structure — periodicity, hidden subgroup structure, Hamiltonian dynamics, marked-element identification. The vast majority of practical compute workloads do not have such structure; they are I/O-bound, memory-bound, control-flow-heavy, or driven by sequential data dependencies that no quantum primitive addresses. A 2026 thought experiment: if you handed an engineer a hypothetical noise-free million-qubit machine and asked them to run their company's web stack faster on it, the answer would be that they could not, because the operations the web stack performs are not the operations the machine accelerates. The machine accelerates a specific list of mathematical primitives, not "computation in general".

A short list of areas where quantum advantage is provably or believably absent or limited:

**Generic search below $\sqrt{N}$.** Grover is optimal for unstructured search: no quantum algorithm can do better than $O(\sqrt{N})$ queries in the black-box model (Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard, and Vazirani 1997). When the structure is genuinely absent, the quadratic speedup is the ceiling.

**Sorting and comparison-based primitives.** The information-theoretic $\Omega(n \log n)$ lower bound on comparison-based sorting carries over to quantum models with at most polylogarithmic improvements.

**Most $\mathrm{NP}$-complete problems.** No known quantum algorithm gives better than a Grover-type quadratic speedup over brute force. The class $\mathrm{NP}$ is not believed to be inside $\mathrm{BQP}$.

**Arbitrary linear algebra at arbitrary scale.** HHL solves $A\\,x = b$ in polylog time, but only when $A$ is sparse, well-conditioned, the right-hand side $b$ is efficiently preparable as a quantum state, and the user wants a quantum-state output, not a classical vector $x$. Strip those assumptions and the speedup vanishes. Many advertised quantum machine-learning speedups suffer the same input-access caveat. Chapter 15 and Chapter 30 discuss these caveats explicitly.

**Polynomial-time problems with low classical exponents.** Even when a quantum algorithm gives a polynomial speedup over a classical one, the constants and the error-correction overhead can wipe out the asymptotic gain for any feasible input size. Treat asymptotic statements as starting points for a wall-clock analysis, not as guarantees of practical advantage.

The lesson for an experienced developer evaluating a quantum claim is to ask, first, what the *structure* of the problem is that the algorithm allegedly exploits, and second, what the *input-access model* assumes. Most of the time, one of those two questions reveals the speedup as either narrow or illusory.

## 1.5 Quantum Advantage vs. Practical Utility

The phrase **quantum advantage** has come to mean two different things, often conflated in popular coverage, and the distinction is worth establishing now.

**Computational quantum advantage** (sometimes "quantum supremacy", a term largely retired due to its connotations): a demonstration that a quantum device solves *some* computational task faster than the best known classical algorithm on the best available classical hardware. The 2019 Google Sycamore experiment claimed this for a random-circuit sampling task; subsequent and ongoing work by competing groups has repeatedly tightened both the quantum claim and the classical baseline. The task in question — sampling from the output distribution of a deep random circuit — has no known practical application. The point of the demonstration is to establish that the quantum machine is doing something genuinely hard for classical machines, validating the underlying physics and engineering. It is a milestone, not a product. Chapter 25 covers the current state of computational-advantage claims and the back-and-forth with improved classical simulators.

**Practical utility**: a quantum computer solves a problem that someone actually cares about — a chemistry simulation that informs a drug discovery effort, a factoring task that breaks a deployed cryptographic key, an optimisation problem whose answer saves money — faster, cheaper, or more accurately than any available classical alternative. As of 2026, no demonstration meets this bar at production scale. The gap between computational advantage and practical utility is the gap the field is currently trying to close.

Why is the gap so persistent? Three reasons. First, the algorithms with the largest provable speedups (Shor, certain quantum-simulation routines) require fault-tolerant logical qubits in the thousands to millions, and the hardware does not yet exist. Second, the algorithms feasible on current pre-fault-tolerant hardware (VQE, QAOA, small variational circuits) lack provable advantage and have so far failed to demonstrate empirical advantage on problems of practical scale. Third, classical algorithms are not standing still: every claimed quantum advantage triggers a wave of classical improvement, and several formerly-touted speedups have been substantially deflated. The interplay is healthy for the science and frustrating for marketing departments. Chapter 36 develops a checklist for evaluating quantum-advantage claims; you should reach for it whenever a press release uses the word "breakthrough".

The honest summary for an engineer: as of 2026, no quantum computer is solving a problem you have at work faster than your classical computer is. This is likely to remain true for at least several more years. It is not likely to remain true forever, and the chapters that follow develop the technical framework you will need to evaluate, deploy, and reason about quantum compute when it does become useful.

## 1.6 NISQ vs. Fault-Tolerant Quantum Computing

> **Moving-target warning — snapshot as of May 2026.** The qubit counts, error rates, and timeline forecasts in this section reflect the best sources available in 2026 and *date quickly*. If you are reading a draft, treat every specific number and date here as provisional and re-verify it against current vendor, preprint, or journal sources before relying on it.

The 2025–2026 hardware landscape divides cleanly into two regimes, and the distinction structures the rest of the book.

**NISQ — Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum.** The term was coined by John Preskill in 2018 to describe devices in the regime that has dominated the field through the early 2020s: tens to hundreds, now thousands, of physical qubits, gate fidelities good enough for short circuits but not for arbitrarily deep ones, no fault-tolerant error correction at scale, and a runtime budget determined by decoherence rather than by problem size. The headline NISQ devices in 2025–2026: superconducting processors from IBM (Heron and successors in the thousand-physical-qubit range), Google (Willow, with the first below-threshold demonstration of surface-code error correction), Quantinuum and IonQ (trapped-ion machines with tens of high-fidelity, all-to-all-connected qubits — Quantinuum's H2 at 56, IonQ's Forte at #AQ 29 — and aggressive scaling roadmaps), Atom Computing and QuEra (neutral-atom arrays scaling toward and past a thousand qubits), Rigetti and PsiQuantum (the latter pursuing a photonic fault-tolerant architecture rather than NISQ). Chapter 20 surveys hardware platforms in detail; Chapter 25 covers the NISQ era and its honest results.

**Fault-tolerant quantum computing.** The regime in which logical qubits are encoded across many physical qubits using a quantum error-correcting code (Chapter 19), and arbitrary-depth circuits become possible at the cost of a substantial physical-to-logical qubit ratio (currently around 1000:1 for surface codes at realistic error rates, with ongoing reductions). The first below-threshold demonstrations have appeared in 2024–2025; the first logical algorithms have been run on a handful of logical qubits; the cryptographically relevant scale (thousands to millions of logical qubits, depending on the algorithm and the target key size) remains years to decades away depending on whose estimate you trust. The transition from NISQ to fault-tolerant is not a single moment but a gradient, and Chapter 25 names the current phase "early fault-tolerant" — neither fully fault-tolerant nor purely NISQ, but logical-qubit experiments running alongside large physical-qubit demonstrations.

Three numbers worth carrying with you. First, **physical-to-logical ratio**: under the surface code with physical error rates around $10^{-3}$, a single logical qubit at the error rate needed for a Shor-scale factoring algorithm consumes around $10^3$ to $10^4$ physical qubits. Improvements in code design and physical error rates pull this number down; current research targets a tenfold reduction over the next several years. Second, **gate fidelities**: top platforms reach two-qubit gate errors in the range of $10^{-3}$ to $10^{-4}$ as of 2026, with single-qubit gates an order of magnitude better. The fault-tolerance threshold for the surface code is around 1% for the most permissive analyses; we are well below threshold on the best devices. Third, **logical-qubit counts demonstrated**: a small but growing number, in the single to low double digits as of late 2025, with steady increases and an open competition over which platform crosses the next milestone first.

What does this mean for the experienced developer reading this book in 2026? The cryptanalytic threat (Shor at scale) is real but not imminent — measured in years to decades, not months — and the right response is to follow the post-quantum cryptography migration that NIST and other standards bodies are driving rather than to panic. The simulation opportunity (Feynman's original motivation) is closer, with the first plausibly-useful quantum-chemistry calculations in the early fault-tolerant regime expected, on the more optimistic roadmaps, within roughly five to ten years of 2026 — a scenario to plan around, not a scheduled deliverable. The speculative opportunities (optimisation, ML) remain genuinely uncertain. And the conceptual framework — superposition, interference, entanglement, measurement, error correction, fault tolerance — is intellectually rewarding and increasingly load-bearing in adjacent fields (post-quantum cryptography, quantum sensing, the foundations of computation), regardless of when the cryptanalytic stake actually pays out.

## 1.7 Bridge to Chapter 2

This chapter argued, in informal terms, that quantum computing exists because (a) classical computers cannot efficiently simulate quantum systems, (b) a quantum machine can do specific things — factoring, hidden-subgroup problems, quantum simulation, unstructured search — exponentially or quadratically faster than the best known classical algorithms, and (c) the 2025–2026 hardware is on a trajectory that makes the simulation opportunity plausibly near-term and the cryptanalytic threat plausibly medium-term.
