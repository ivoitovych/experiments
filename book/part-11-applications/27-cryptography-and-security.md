# Chapter 27. Cryptography and Security

Cryptography is the application area where quantum computing has the sharpest, most concrete consequences. Shor's algorithm (§15.2, §15.4) breaks the traditional factoring- and discrete-log-based public-key primitives that still dominate deployment, in polynomial time on a fault-tolerant quantum computer; Grover's algorithm (§15.1) applies a quadratic query speedup to symmetric-key search — parameter pressure, not a break. The community response — post-quantum cryptography, with initial NIST standards finalized in 2024 and standardization continuing — is now shipping in TLS libraries, SSH implementations, and update toolchains, mostly in hybrid or optional modes whose adoption varies by product. Quantum key distribution exists as an independent track that offers a different security argument (quantum mechanics plus device and protocol assumptions, rather than computational hardness) at the cost of severe deployment constraints. This chapter walks the engineering picture: what is broken, what replaces it, what the threat timeline looks like, and where QKD does and does not earn its keep.

> **How to read this chapter.** §§27.1–27.4 are the cryptographic-impact summary: which primitives Shor breaks, what Grover does to the symmetric-key world, and what post-quantum cryptography offers as the replacement. §27.5 is the NIST standardization timeline and the FIPS 203/204/205 outputs you will actually deploy. §27.6 introduces the *harvest now, decrypt later* (HNDL) threat model — the reason migration starts before fault-tolerant hardware does. §§27.7–27.10 cover quantum key distribution: BB84, E91, B92, decoy states, security arguments, deployment limits, repeaters. §27.11 closes with practical recommendations and the bridge to Chapter 28.

## 27.1 Public-Key Cryptography and Shor

Nearly all conventionally deployed (pre-PQC) public-key cryptography rests on one of two hardness assumptions: integer factorization (RSA) or discrete logarithms — finite-field for Diffie–Hellman and DSA, elliptic-curve for ECDH, ECDSA, EdDSA, and Schnorr, pairing-friendly-group variants for BLS. (Already-deployed PQC hybrids and hash-based signatures are the growing exceptions.) Shor's algorithm and its discrete-log variant (§15.2, §15.4) solve both in polynomial time on a fault-tolerant quantum computer of sufficient scale.

The damage list, with the assumption broken in parentheses:

- **RSA encryption and RSA-PSS / RSA-PKCS signatures** (factoring of $N = pq$): RSA-2048 ciphertexts decrypt and signatures forge in polynomial quantum time once a relevant-scale machine exists.
- **Finite-field Diffie–Hellman (DH) and ElGamal** (DLP in $\mathbb{F}_p^*$): session keys negotiated via DH are recoverable by a passive eavesdropper holding the public transcript.
- **DSA, ECDSA, EdDSA, Schnorr signatures** (DLP in $\mathbb{F}_p^*$ or in an elliptic-curve group): private signing keys recoverable from any public key. ECDSA over `secp256r1`, `secp256k1` (Bitcoin, Ethereum), Ed25519, Schnorr/Taproot — all fall.
- **BLS signatures and pairing-based cryptography** (DLP in pairing-friendly elliptic-curve groups): signatures forge, identity-based encryption decrypts.
- **TLS 1.3 forward secrecy** rests on ephemeral (EC)DH. A passive recorder that captures handshake records today and decrypts them later with a quantum computer recovers all session traffic. This is the harvest-now-decrypt-later (HNDL) threat — §27.6.

The cost asymmetry between RSA and ECDLP is the opposite of the classical one: classically, ECC with 256-bit keys matches RSA-3072 because Pollard's rho on a 256-bit elliptic curve and the general number field sieve on a 3072-bit modulus are roughly equiweak. Quantumly, both fall to Shor, and *ECC falls cheaper*: published logical-resource estimates for 256-bit ECDLP (Roetteler et al. 2017; Häner et al. 2020) come in below those for factoring RSA-2048 — noting that this comparison crosses classical security levels (ECC-256 classically pairs with RSA-3072), and that the ratio depends on arithmetic, architecture, and optimization rather than being a universal one-half. Current resource *scenarios* (§15.3) run from Gidney–Ekerå's 2019 estimate ($\sim 20$ million physical qubits, $\sim 8$ hours, at $p = 10^{-3}$ under a specific architecture) to a reported 2025 refinement claiming under a million physical qubits over days — a claim whose changed architectural assumptions need a primary-source audit before it anchors policy ("same assumptions" it is not). These are scenarios, not bounds, and ECDLP estimates do not follow by proportional scaling — though ECDLP-256 remains the cheaper Shor target in published comparisons.

The bottom line: every *Shor-vulnerable* asymmetric primitive still deployed in TLS, SSH, IPsec, S/MIME, code-signing, OS update, secure-boot, blockchain, and electronic-passport stacks will need to be replaced or retired — stacks already running PQC hybrids are partway there. Not because Shor is here in 2026, but because (a) the migration costs years, (b) HNDL means the data leaking now is the cleartext-later, and (c) NIST has standardized the initial replacements, with more in the pipeline.

## 27.2 Symmetric-Key Cryptography and Grover

Symmetric-key cryptography fares much better. Grover's algorithm (§15.1) gives a quadratic speedup for unstructured search, which means brute-forcing a $k$-bit key takes $O(2^{k/2})$ quantum queries instead of $2^k$ classical ones. The naive reading: a 128-bit key has only 64 bits of post-quantum security; double the key size to recover the security margin.

For AES this is straightforward. AES-128 is widely deployed, AES-192 and AES-256 are broadly available, and migration is often close to a configuration change (key management, hardware acceleration, and compliance can add work). The standard recommendation: *if you need post-quantum confidentiality against Grover, use AES-256, which retains 128 bits of effective security after the square-root speedup*. AES-128 is not catastrophically broken — NIST's security Category 1 is *benchmarked* against the resources needed to break AES-128 including quantum attacks, and the concrete quantum attack (the $\sim 2^{83}$-Toffoli estimate below) costs far more than a literal $2^{64}$-operation search, so the naive "64-bit" reading overstates the damage. The concern is margin erosion — the asymptotic query exponent plus room for future cryptanalytic improvement — which makes AES-128 a poor choice for long-lived secrets. The cleanest answer is to move to AES-256.

Three details complicate the simple picture and reduce the apparent threat.

**Constant factors are real.** Grover's $\sqrt{N}$ is an asymptotic statement. A concrete fault-tolerant Grover-on-AES-128 circuit is *not* $2^{64}$ gate operations; it is more like $2^{64}$ *Grover iterations*, each of which is one AES forward pass in a fault-tolerant circuit with magic-state distillation, surface-code overhead, and reversible-arithmetic blowup. Grassl–Langenberg–Roetteler–Steinwandt (2016) and Jaques–Naehrig–Roetteler–Virdia (2020) estimated AES-128 key recovery at on the order of $2^{83}$ Toffoli operations using $\sim 6000$ logical qubits — well above the NIST category-1 threshold for *most* near-term threat models, even though the asymptotic exponent is 64.

**Grover does not parallelize efficiently.** Splitting a Grover search across $P$ machines gives only a $\sqrt{P}$ speedup, not a $P$ speedup (Zalka 1999). A passive attacker with a million quantum machines does not get to multiply their effective compute by a million; they get a thousand.

**Quantum attacks on modes of operation matter more than attacks on the block cipher.** Several modes and MACs admit superposition-query (Q2-model) attacks far below $2^{k/2}$ — Simon-style forgeries against particular CBC-MAC/PMAC/GCM-related constructions under specific nonce and structural assumptions (Kaplan–Leurent–Leverrier–Naya-Plasencia 2016; Bonnetain–Naya-Plasencia 2018); the results differ construction by construction rather than "breaking authentication" wholesale. These attacks require *quantum-superposition access to the keyed oracle*, which ordinary network adversaries do not have — though exotic delegated or embedded settings can grant it, which is why standards analysis treats the classical-query (Q1) and quantum-query (Q2) models separately. In the standard model where the adversary sees only classical ciphertexts, AES-256-GCM remains a fine post-quantum symmetric primitive under the usual nonce discipline and implementation care.

The summary: prefer AES-256 for new deployments and long-lived secrets, keep the usual symmetric hygiene (nonce discipline, tag lengths, side channels), and spend your quantum-migration anxiety on the public-key layer, where it belongs.

## 27.3 Hash Functions

Hash functions feel a similar Grover effect, with two distinct attack models to keep separate.

**Preimage and second-preimage**: finding $x$ such that $H(x) = y$ for a given $y$, on an $n$-bit hash, takes $2^n$ classical queries and $O(2^{n/2})$ quantum queries via Grover. SHA-256 has 256-bit output, so the post-quantum preimage cost is $2^{128}$ — comfortably above NIST category 1 and not a near-term concern.

**Collision finding**: finding any pair $(x_1, x_2)$ with $H(x_1) = H(x_2)$ takes $2^{n/2}$ classical queries (the birthday bound) and *not* $2^{n/4}$ as a naive reading of Grover would suggest. The relevant result is Brassard–Høyer–Tapp (BHT, 1997), which gives $O(2^{n/3})$ quantum queries using a quantum-accessible memory of size $2^{n/3}$. Without large quantum memory, the known quantum attacks improve only modestly on the classical birthday bound — down to roughly $2^{2n/5}$ time with $2^{n/5}$ classical memory and only polynomially many qubits, no QRAM (Chailloux–Naya-Plasencia–Schrottenloher 2017). So for SHA-256 collisions the picture is model-dependent rather than one number: $\sim 2^{85}$ quantum queries with large quantum-accessible memory (BHT — the memory model is the catch), $\sim 2^{102}$ time with modest classical memory and no QRAM (Chailloux et al.), against the $2^{128}$ classical birthday cost — figures in different cost metrics that resist direct comparison.

The practical recommendation tracks the symmetric one: use 256-bit hashes (SHA-256, SHA3-256, BLAKE3, BLAKE2b-256). 384-bit and 512-bit variants exist for higher-assurance settings (and higher NIST categories call for them). The hash-based-signature schemes in §27.4 lean primarily on preimage/second-preimage-style assumptions rather than collision resistance — generically *harder* to attack than collisions ($2^n$ classically versus the $2^{n/2}$ birthday bound) and less degraded by quantum collision algorithms — retaining $\sim 128$ bits of post-quantum security at $n = 256$.

## 27.4 Post-Quantum Cryptography: The Replacement Toolkit

**Post-quantum cryptography (PQC)** is the set of cryptographic primitives whose security rests on problems *believed to be hard for quantum computers*. The main families that entered the NIST standardization process — with very different fates: some produced standards, others were broken along the way — are:

**Lattice-based.** Security reduces to short-vector or learning-with-errors (LWE / Module-LWE / Ring-LWE) problems on integer lattices. The dominant family in the NIST output. Key sizes are moderate (kilobytes), operations are fast, and the schemes are versatile — KEM (key encapsulation mechanism, the post-quantum replacement for key exchange), signatures, identity-based encryption, fully-homomorphic encryption all have lattice constructions. The downside is that the security reductions tie scheme security to *worst-case* lattice problems by way of *average-case* variants, and the constants in those reductions are loose; the standardized parameter sets are calibrated against *direct* cryptanalysis rather than against the worst-case reduction. **CRYSTALS-Kyber** (KEM; standardized as ML-KEM) and **CRYSTALS-Dilithium** (signatures; ML-DSA) are the workhorses; **Falcon** (signatures) is an NTRU-lattice scheme with smaller signatures but harder constant-time implementation.

**Hash-based.** Security reduces to second-preimage resistance of a cryptographic hash function — the most conservative assumption in cryptography, with the longest track record. Hash-based schemes are *signatures only* (no KEM). **XMSS** and **LMS** (RFC 8391, RFC 8554) are stateful: each signature consumes one one-time signing key from a precomputed Merkle tree, and reusing a key catastrophically breaks security. **SPHINCS+** is stateless at the cost of larger signatures (roughly 8–50 KB across parameter sets). Hash-based signatures trade performance for conservatism — signing in particular can be slow, with the signing/verification balance set by the parameter set — and produce large signatures, but they are the *diversity fallback* if lattice schemes suffer an unexpected cryptanalytic breakthrough (where their sizes are tolerable).

**Code-based.** Security reduces to decoding a random linear code — McEliece 1978, still unbroken after almost five decades. **Classic McEliece** is a KEM with tiny ciphertexts (roughly 100–250 bytes depending on parameter set) and huge public keys (megabytes). The conservative choice for **long-lived, static** public keys — distributed or cached once, after which only the tiny ciphertexts travel per session. Its megabyte public key makes it a poor fit for ephemeral, per-handshake key exchange, where a fresh key would have to be transmitted every time.

**Multivariate.** Security reduces to solving systems of multivariate quadratic equations over a finite field. The family produced GeMSS, LUOV, Rainbow — Rainbow advanced far in the NIST process and was then broken in 2022 by Beullens with a classical algorithm. Multivariate constructions remain a research topic but are not currently considered ready for deployment.

A fifth family, **isogeny-based** cryptography (SIDH/SIKE), was an alternate/fourth-round NIST candidate until the 2022–2023 line of attacks begun by Castryck–Decru (with independent and follow-up work by Maino–Martindale, Robert, and others) broke it classically — polynomial-time for the proposed parameters. Isogeny constructions on different assumptions survive (CSIDH, SQIsign for signatures), but the family's confidence and deployment prospects changed materially, and it is not in the standardized output.

Key engineering numbers for the standardized schemes, in round figures:

- **ML-KEM-768** (Kyber-768, FIPS 203, NIST category 3): public key 1184 B, ciphertext 1088 B, shared secret 32 B. Encapsulation and decapsulation under 100 microseconds on a modern CPU.
- **ML-DSA-65** (Dilithium-3, FIPS 204, category 3): public key 1952 B, signature 3309 B. Sign and verify well under a millisecond on a modern CPU.
- **SLH-DSA-SHA2-128s** (SPHINCS+-128s, FIPS 205, category 1): public key 32 B, signature 7856 B. Signing is slow (several hundred milliseconds), verification is fast.
- **Falcon-512** (FN-DSA in NIST's draft standardization; FIPS 206 in progress as of this snapshot — its stage and dates must be checked against NIST's current announcements; category 1): public key 897 B, signature 666 B. Smallest signatures of any forthcoming PQC signature scheme, but constant-time floating-point implementation is hard.
- **Classic McEliece (mceliece6960119)** (category 5): public key 1 047 319 B (~1 MB), ciphertext 226 B. KEM only.

These are several to dozens of times larger than RSA/ECC keys and signatures, which has knock-on consequences for TLS record sizes, certificate-chain transmission, embedded-device memory, and DNSSEC packet bloat. Hybrid schemes (PQC + ECDH or PQC + ECDSA) are widely deployed and recommended during the transition, though not universal across protocols and sectors — and the even-if-one-falls-the-other-protects guarantee holds only with a robust combiner: standardized, domain-separated KDF combination of secrets (not bare concatenation), a both-must-verify signature policy, and downgrade resistance.

## 27.5 NIST Standardization Timeline

The NIST Post-Quantum Cryptography Standardization process began in 2016, produced its first final standards in 2024, and continues (HQC, FN-DSA, and the additional-signature process). It is the most consequential cryptographic standardization effort of the decade. The timeline:

- **2016**: NIST announces the call. 82 submissions arrive by November 2017; 69 accepted as round-1 candidates.
- **2019**: Round 2 narrows to 26 candidates (17 KEM/encryption, 9 signature).
- **2020**: Round 3 names 7 finalists (4 KEM, 3 signature) and 8 alternates.
- **2022**: First selection. CRYSTALS-Kyber chosen for KEM; CRYSTALS-Dilithium, Falcon, SPHINCS+ chosen for signatures. Round 4 continues evaluating McEliece, BIKE, HQC (KEM) and an "on-ramp" for additional signature schemes diverse from lattice-based.
- **2023**: Draft FIPS 203, 204, 205 published. Rainbow already broken (2022); SIKE broken (Castryck–Decru, July 2022).
- **August 2024**: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA), FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA) finalized. The standards use scheme-descriptive names: ML-KEM = Module-Lattice-Based Key-Encapsulation Mechanism (Kyber), ML-DSA = Module-Lattice-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (Dilithium), SLH-DSA = Stateless Hash-Based Digital Signature Algorithm (SPHINCS+).
- **March 2025**: HQC selected as a code-based KEM for cryptographic diversity (NIST IR 8545), slated for standardization as FIPS 207 (final timing is projected, not scheduled fact). Falcon's FN-DSA / FIPS 206 draft process continues — stages and dates per NIST's current announcements.
- **2025–2026**: The additional-signature process continues. The candidate roster — maintained authoritatively on NIST's pages, and any list frozen here would already be stale — has included multivariate designs in the oil-and-vinegar lineage (UOV, MAYO, SNOVA) alongside hash-based and other approaches.

The deployment timeline is faster than usual for cryptographic standards. OpenSSL 3.5 ships ML-KEM in 2025. Cloudflare, Google, Apple, and AWS have deployed hybrid ML-KEM + X25519 in production TLS by mid-2024. The iMessage PQ3 protocol (2024) uses ML-KEM for post-compromise security. Signal's PQXDH (late 2023) prefixes its X3DH key agreement with a Kyber-based handshake. SSH gained `mlkem768x25519-sha256` in OpenSSH 9.9 (late 2024). The browser, mobile, and infrastructure layers are already living through the migration in 2026; the long tail of embedded and legacy systems is what will take a decade.

## 27.6 Harvest Now, Decrypt Later

The earliest cryptographic threat from quantum computing is not "when will a quantum computer break my live traffic in real time" (that becomes an additional threat once such a machine exists). It is **harvest now, decrypt later (HNDL)**: a passive adversary records encrypted traffic and key exchanges today, stores them, and decrypts the archive in five, ten, or twenty years when a relevant-scale fault-tolerant quantum computer comes online. Traffic that a well-resourced adversary captures from a fiber tap or ISP mirror today *can* sit in an archive indefinitely — how much is actually collected and retained is an intelligence assumption rather than a public fact, but a defender of long-lived secrets must price it in.

HNDL changes the migration timeline in a specific, risk-based way — Mosca's inequality: if (confidentiality lifetime) + (migration time) exceeds (time until a cryptographically relevant quantum computer), you are already late. **For long-lived secrets the migration deadline is effectively the present**, not the arrival of fault-tolerant hardware; short-lived data can be prioritized lower. If a piece of data has a confidentiality lifetime of 20 years (medical records, government secrets, intellectual property, source code, journalist–source communications), and if relevant-scale fault-tolerant quantum computing materializes any time before 2046, then any transmission of that data in 2026 over non-post-quantum-protected channels is potentially compromised.

This is the underlying reason for the urgency of the NIST process and for the deployment pace of hybrid KEMs in 2024–2026. Few in the cryptographic community expect a relevant-scale fault-tolerant quantum computer imminently — published expert-survey estimates cluster around one to two decades, with wide disagreement and strategic incentives in every direction — but for HNDL the migration must complete *before* the threat, and migration itself takes on the order of a decade for large estates. The window is not "when can a quantum computer attack live traffic"; it is "when did we record the ciphertext".

Authentication is different — but less different than it first seems. A forged signature in 2040 matters for artifacts still relied upon in 2040, which covers a lot: long-lived certificates and roots of trust, firmware and archived binaries (an adversary can archive signed updates and public keys now and later forge malicious successors against legacy verifiers), notarized documents, timestamp chains. For genuinely ephemeral authentication (TLS handshake binding, short-lived tokens), the threat clock starts when the machine exists. This is why KEM-style confidentiality migration *led* signature migration in deployment — HNDL urgency plus standards readiness — not because signatures can wait indefinitely: roots of trust and update infrastructures need long lead times too.

## 27.7 Quantum Key Distribution: BB84

**Quantum key distribution (QKD)** is the other side of the cryptography-meets-quantum coin: instead of using quantum hardness to attack classical primitives, QKD uses quantum mechanics to *build* a key-establishment primitive whose security argument rests on quantum mechanics itself (measurement disturbance, no-cloning, entanglement monogamy) together with protocol and device assumptions and an authenticated classical channel — rather than on computational hardness.

The original protocol is **BB84** (Bennett–Brassard 1984). Alice prepares a long sequence of single qubits, each chosen uniformly at random from the set $\\{|0\rangle, |1\rangle, |+\rangle, |-\rangle\\}$ — four states across two mutually unbiased bases. She records, for each qubit, the *basis* (rectilinear $Z$ or diagonal $X$) and the *bit* ($0$ or $1$). Bob, for each arriving qubit, picks $Z$ or $X$ uniformly at random and measures. Bob's bit matches Alice's whenever his basis matches hers (half the time, in expectation); when the bases mismatch, his result is independent of Alice's bit.

The complete case analysis fits in one table (states in code spans; the
kept rows are exactly the basis-match rows):

| Alice's bit | Alice's basis | State sent | Bob's basis | Bob's outcome  | Kept after sifting? |
|-------------|---------------|------------|-------------|----------------|---------------------|
| 0           | Z             | `\|0⟩`     | Z           | 0, always      | yes                 |
| 0           | Z             | `\|0⟩`     | X           | 0 or 1, random | no                  |
| 1           | Z             | `\|1⟩`     | Z           | 1, always      | yes                 |
| 1           | Z             | `\|1⟩`     | X           | 0 or 1, random | no                  |
| 0           | X             | `\|+⟩`     | X           | 0, always      | yes                 |
| 0           | X             | `\|+⟩`     | Z           | 0 or 1, random | no                  |
| 1           | X             | `\|−⟩`     | X           | 1, always      | yes                 |
| 1           | X             | `\|−⟩`     | Z           | 0 or 1, random | no                  |

After the quantum transmission, Alice and Bob run a classical post-processing phase over an authenticated public channel:

1. **Basis sifting.** They announce their basis choice for each qubit. They keep the bits where the bases match and discard the rest. This leaves the **sifted key** — half the original length in expectation.
2. **Error estimation.** They reveal a random subset of the sifted key. The fraction of disagreements is the **quantum bit error rate (QBER)**. Absent an eavesdropper, QBER reflects channel and device noise (misalignment, dark counts); eavesdropping adds to it. A low QBER bounds Eve's information — it does not by itself certify her absence.
3. **Information reconciliation.** They run a classical error-correcting protocol (LDPC, cascade) over the remaining sifted key to make Alice and Bob's keys identical, leaking some information to a potential eavesdropper in the process.
4. **Privacy amplification.** They apply a universal hash function to compress the reconciled key to a shorter final key whose length comes from an entropy accounting — Eve's bounded information, the reconciliation leakage, and finite-key security margins are all subtracted — producing a final shared secret about which the eavesdropper has negligible information.

The security argument: an eavesdropper Eve cannot copy each qubit (no-cloning theorem, §5.13) and cannot measure each qubit without disturbing the ones whose basis she guessed wrong. Any such disturbance increases QBER. If QBER stays below a threshold (around 11% for the standard asymptotic one-way-postprocessing analysis of BB84), Alice and Bob can post-process to a secret key with provably negligible eavesdropper information; above the threshold, they abort. Eve's simplest individual-qubit attack — intercept-resend in a random basis — gives her full knowledge of about half the sifted bits (the positions where she guessed the basis right) and nothing useful on the rest, at the cost of a 25% QBER, well above the abort threshold. (Subtler attacks trade less information for less disturbance; the security proofs, not this one example, carry the general case.)

A simple worked example clarifies the bit accounting. Suppose Alice sends $|+\rangle$. Eve measures in the $Z$-basis: she sees $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$ with probability $1/2$ each. Conditional on her result, she forwards that basis state to Bob. Bob, if measuring in $X$, sees $|+\rangle$ or $|-\rangle$ with probability $1/2$ each — independent of Alice's bit. Eve's attack has injected a 50% error rate on these positions (Alice sent X, Bob measured X, Eve measured Z). Averaged over the sifted key, Eve's intercept-resend gives 25% QBER.

## 27.8 E91, B92, and Decoy States

**E91** (Ekert 1991) replaces prepare-and-measure with **entanglement distribution**. A central source distributes halves of Bell pairs $|\Phi^+\rangle = (|00\rangle + |11\rangle)/\sqrt{2}$ to Alice and Bob. Each measures their qubit in one of three bases chosen uniformly at random, drawn from settings that overlap on some pairs (for sifted key) and complementary on others (for a CHSH-style Bell-inequality test). The sifted-key half is identical to the BB84 sifted key; the Bell-test half lets Alice and Bob check that their correlations violate the CHSH inequality by a sufficient margin — the local-hidden-variable bound is $2$; ideal quantum correlations reach Tsirelson's bound $2\sqrt{2}$ (see §7.9, §7.12), which real devices approach but never saturate, and security requires a sufficient violation, not the maximum. Eavesdropping on the qubits in transit degrades the violation — though turning that intuition into security against arbitrary attacks, with detection loopholes closed, is exactly what the device-independent analyses formalize. E91 is conceptually clean — its security argument routes through entanglement monogamy rather than no-cloning — and is the conceptual foundation for **device-independent QKD**, where security can be proven with minimal trust in the measurement devices, given a loophole-free violation, isolated trusted labs, and good local randomness.

**B92** (Bennett 1992) is a minimalist BB84 variant — operationally, an application of the unambiguous state discrimination of §11.6 — using only *two* non-orthogonal states, e.g. $|0\rangle$ and $|+\rangle$. The encoding is: $|0\rangle$ represents bit $0$, $|+\rangle$ represents bit $1$. Bob measures in $X$ or $Z$ at random; an outcome of $|1\rangle$ (in $Z$) rules out $|0\rangle$ and so identifies $|+\rangle$ (bit 1), and an outcome of $|-\rangle$ (in $X$) rules out $|+\rangle$ and identifies $|0\rangle$ (bit 0) — possible because $\langle 1 | 0\rangle = \langle - | + \rangle = 0$ while the cross-overlaps are $1/\sqrt{2}$. Inconclusive results are discarded (operationally, unambiguous state discrimination in the spirit of §11.6, here realized with projective measurements). B92 is simpler to implement but more vulnerable to channel loss (an intermediate Eve can selectively suppress events) and is rarely deployed.

**Decoy-state QKD** (Hwang 2003, Lo–Ma–Chen 2005) is the engineering fix that made BB84 secure with realistic *coherent-state* light sources. A laser pulse attenuated to "single-photon level" is in reality a Poisson distribution over photon number: occasional pulses contain two or more photons, and Eve can split off one photon, store it, and measure once the bases are announced — a **photon-number-splitting (PNS) attack**. The decoy-state countermeasure: Alice randomly varies the mean photon number per pulse across two or three values, and the per-intensity yield and QBER let Alice and Bob bound the contribution from multi-photon pulses to the final key. With decoy states (and phase-randomized pulses), weak-coherent BB84 recovers information-theoretic security with the multi-photon contribution bounded — comparable in kind, though not identical in rate or assumptions, to ideal single-photon BB84; the rate cost depends on the optimized intensities and the channel, not on a universal factor of two. Decoy states became standard in commercial weak-coherent BB84 systems from the late 2000s onward (entanglement-based and continuous-variable systems use different countermeasures).

A practical detail worth flagging: the security of BB84 rests on proof lineages developed from the late 1990s through the 2000s — Mayers, Lo–Chau, Shor–Preskill, and Renner's composable framework — while E91-style and B92 protocols have their own proofs and device models; the lineage does not transfer by parenthesis. The asymptotic 11% QBER threshold is a textbook number; finite-key analyses tolerate less and yield shorter keys, by amounts set by block size, security parameter, and reconciliation efficiency (and at long distance, the detections — not the pulses sent — are the sample that matters). Production systems should use validated composable finite-key bounds; certification status varies across vendors.

## 27.9 QKD Security: What It Promises, What It Does Not

The selling proposition of QKD is **information-theoretic security**: no assumption on the computational power of the adversary, no assumption on future cryptanalysis, no assumption on whether large quantum computers exist. The security argument is in the structure of quantum mechanics. If quantum mechanics is correct and the implementation is faithful to the protocol's abstract description — including its authenticated classical channel, characterized devices, and trusted local randomness — QKD produces a key that is information-theoretically secret.

This is genuinely stronger than computational cryptography. RSA's security is conditional on factoring being hard. ML-KEM's security is conditional on Module-LWE being hard. Both could be undone tomorrow by a cryptanalytic breakthrough. QKD's security, taken at the level of the abstract protocol, is not conditioned on computational hardness at all — undoing it would take quantum mechanics being wrong *or* a flaw in the proof's model and assumptions (proofs have been revised before, and the authenticated channel and device model are assumptions too).

But the abstract protocol and the deployed protocol are not the same thing, and the gap is where every practical QKD attack lives.

**Side-channel attacks.** Detectors have detection-efficiency mismatches between the two bases; a bright-light blinding attack (Lydersen et al. 2010) saturates avalanche photodiode detectors into a classical photodetection regime where Eve controls Bob's measurements outright. Wavelength-dependent attacks exploit beam-splitter imperfections. Time-shift attacks exploit timing imperfections. Trojan-horse attacks shine a probe pulse into Alice's modulator and read out her basis choice from the back-reflection. The list grew steadily through the 2010s and continues; multiple commercial QKD implementations have suffered demonstrated side-channel attacks — "broken" meaning anything from partial information leakage to full key recovery under lab conditions — with countermeasures (detector redesign, monitoring, MDI protocols, certification programs) co-evolving.

**Authenticated classical channel.** The post-processing in §27.7 step 2–4 must run over an *authenticated* classical channel. QKD does not solve authentication; it solves confidentiality of a *fresh* key, given an authenticated classical channel. Authentication usually uses a pre-shared secret (the previous QKD session's key, plus a Wegman–Carter universal-hash MAC) or a public-key signature. Bootstrapping from a public-key signature makes the *authentication* computational — but not the whole system: a short-lived computational authentication can still yield *everlasting* confidentiality for keys established while it held (an adversary who breaks the signature scheme later cannot retroactively decrypt), a genuinely different trust profile from PQC-throughout. Whether that difference justifies QKD's cost is a threat-model decision, not a logical dismissal.

**Device-independent QKD** (Mayers–Yao 1998, Acín et al. 2007, Pironio et al. 2010) is the partial answer to side-channel attacks: prove security from the observed Bell-inequality violation only, without trusting Alice's and Bob's devices. The catch is that DI-QKD requires very high detection efficiencies (so the detection loophole closes) and very low channel loss, and demonstrations at the relevant key rates over the relevant distances are research-grade in 2026. **Measurement-device-independent (MDI) QKD** is the deployed compromise: trust source devices, do not trust the detector, route both Alice's and Bob's photons through a Bell-state measurement at an untrusted central node. MDI-QKD has been implemented over hundreds of kilometers and closes the most severe detector-side attacks.

## 27.10 Limits, Repeaters, and the Practical Picture

Distance and rate are the binding constraints on QKD deployment.

**Loss in optical fiber is exponential in distance.** Standard single-mode fiber attenuates at roughly 0.2 dB/km at telecom wavelengths (1550 nm), which means the photon-transmission probability falls by a factor of 100 every 100 km. The Pirandola–Laurenza–Ottaviani–Banchi (PLOB, 2017) bound is the fundamental limit on point-to-point QKD over a lossy channel: the secret-key rate is bounded by $-\log_2(1 - \eta)$ bits per channel use, where $\eta$ is the channel transmission. At $\eta \ll 1$ (long fiber runs), this is approximately $\eta / \ln 2$ — *linear* in transmission, so key rates fall exponentially with distance.

In representative reported numbers — protocol-, detector-, and year-dependent; the sourced rate–distance table belongs in Appendix F — point-to-point QKD runs from Mbps-scale at metropolitan distances down to bits per second at several hundred kilometers, hitting hardware noise floors somewhere beyond $\sim 500$ km for strictly point-to-point protocols (twin-field protocols with an untrusted midpoint stretch this — §33.4 and below). Field deployments (the Beijing–Shanghai backbone, Tokyo QKD network, Madrid Quantum Network, and the evolving EuroQCI program) cover hundreds of kilometers only via **trusted-node** architectures: a chain of QKD links in which each intermediate node terminates one link's key and relays key material to the next (typically XOR-relaying key material under local protection rather than decrypting payloads), so every node holds usable key material and must be trusted and physically secured. Relative to end-to-end information-theoretic security this is a major regression — every node is a potential point of compromise — and it erodes the argument that motivated QKD in the first place, even where the physical-site trust model is operationally acceptable.

**Free-space and satellite QKD** sidesteps the fiber limit. The Micius satellite (Pan group, 2017–2020) demonstrated entanglement-based QKD between ground stations 1200 km apart via a low-earth-orbit satellite acting as a trusted relay (and, in an entanglement-distribution demonstration, in a configuration that removes the need to trust the satellite under the protocol's assumptions — ground stations and sources still need theirs). Satellite QKD is impressive science and a plausible backbone for intercontinental key exchange, but reported rates are very low (hundreds of bits per second, pass- and weather-dependent), and the engineering and cost are substantial — comparisons to satellite-internet constellations are loose at best. It is not a turnkey alternative to fiber-based or PQC-based key exchange.

**Twin-field QKD** beats the point-to-point PLOB rate–distance *scaling* without any quantum memory (viewed end to end across an untrusted midpoint — no single-channel bound is violated): both users send phase-encoded weak pulses to an *untrusted* midpoint that performs single-photon interference, so the signal crosses only half the channel and the key rate scales as $\sqrt{\eta}$ rather than $\eta$ (Lucamarini et al. 2018). Twin-field demonstrations have pushed direct fiber links past 500, 830, and (by 2023) 1000 km at low rates; §33.4 covers the protocol and the deployment picture in detail. It is the leading near-term route to longer direct fiber links — there is no single fixed ceiling for standard BB84; detectors, clocks, and loss set the practical range — and the midpoint is untrusted in the security model, so the trusted-node compromise above does not apply (implementation side channels still need care).

**Quantum repeaters** are the long-term answer to the PLOB bound. A repeater chain distributes entanglement segment by segment, performs **entanglement swapping** at each intermediate node to extend the entangled pair, and, in many designs, **entanglement distillation** to purify the lower-fidelity pairs produced by swapping (error-corrected and all-photonic repeater generations use different machinery). Done correctly, a repeater chain produces an end-to-end entangled Bell pair without any intermediate node ever holding plaintext — no trusted-node compromise. The technological prerequisites are quantum memories with seconds-scale coherence times, deterministic entanglement generation between memory and photon, and good Bell-state measurements; small two-segment repeater demonstrations have been done in NV-center and trapped-ion platforms, but a deployable repeater that beats trusted nodes economically is research-grade in 2026 — when that changes is a milestone question, not a date this book can supply.

**Quantum digital signatures and quantum money.** Two further quantum-cryptography primitives are worth flagging without spending a section on each. **Quantum digital signatures** (Gottesman–Chuang 2001 and follow-ups) achieve signature-like functionality using non-orthogonal quantum states distributed in advance; early schemes required quantum memory, prepare-and-measure variants avoid it, and deployment remains experimental. **Wiesner's quantum money** (Wiesner 1969, published 1983) is the original idea: a banknote is a sequence of qubits in random BB84 states recorded by the bank, and the no-cloning theorem prevents counterfeiting — but verification requires sending the bill back to the bank. **Public-key quantum money** schemes (Aaronson 2009, Zhandry 2017–2024) allow anyone to verify without the issuer, at the cost of unproven cryptographic assumptions; security analyses of concrete candidates remain active research, and no broadly accepted deployment-ready construction exists.

## 27.11 Practical Recommendations and Bridge to Chapter 28

The action items that fall out of all of the above:

**For confidentiality (KEM, encryption).** Deploy hybrid post-quantum key establishment (ML-KEM combined with classical X25519 or P-256, via standardized combiner profiles) on channels carrying data whose confidentiality lifetime exceeds the threat timeline, prioritized by inventory — that covers most TLS deployments, VPNs, and messaging end-to-end-encryption protocols. (Cloud-storage encryption at rest is a different architecture — symmetric data keys wrapped by a key-management hierarchy — where the job is migrating the wrapping and agreement layer; see the archive item below.) The hybrid construction means that even if ML-KEM falls to cryptanalysis tomorrow, classical ECDH still protects until a quantum computer arrives, and vice versa — provided the combiner is robust and downgrade-resistant. The performance overhead of hybrid ML-KEM-768 + X25519 over X25519 alone is typically small — milliseconds-scale handshake cost and a few kilobytes — but benchmark it on your own stack; MTU limits, certificate chains, and constrained devices can bite.

**For authentication (signatures).** Migration is less urgent than for confidentiality (no HNDL threat), but the long tail of code-signing, OS-update signing, document signing, and certificate roots will take a decade to turn over. Start now. ML-DSA is the workhorse; Falcon (FN-DSA) when signature size is critical; SLH-DSA / SPHINCS+ when conservatism is critical and large signatures are tolerable. Hybrid signatures (ECDSA + ML-DSA) are the safe default during transition.

**For symmetric primitives.** Use AES-256 for new deployments. Use 256-bit hashes (SHA-256, SHA3-256, BLAKE3). The Grover threat to symmetric crypto is modest and asymptotic — parameter pressure, not a break — which is not license to skip the usual implementation, mode, and tag-length care.

**For QKD.** Deploy only when (a) the link geometry supports direct QKD at a key rate that meets your requirement (metropolitan-scale fiber is the comfortable regime; longer runs need twin-field midpoints, satellites, or trusted relays, each with its own trust and cost profile), (b) the threat model justifies the additional cost and operational complexity, and (c) the deployment is genuinely *layered with* PQC rather than replacing it. Reported deployment patterns include links between paired security facilities, metropolitan links between secure sites, and financial-sector links in jurisdictions or sectors that require them. QKD is *not* a substitute for PQC for general internet traffic; PQC and QKD address overlapping but different threat surfaces, with PQC the broadly deployable solution and QKD a niche augmentation.

**For HNDL-sensitive archives.** Anything you encrypt with non-post-quantum-secure key establishment is potentially in an adversary's archive for future decryption. For data at rest, the vulnerable layer is usually the *key* layer, not the bulk cipher: symmetric data keys wrapped or negotiated under classical public-key primitives are what HNDL-style attacks target. The remedy is key-management surgery — rewrap or rotate data keys under hybrid/PQC key establishment through your KMS, with bulk re-encryption only where the architecture demands it, executed under tested crypto-agility procedures (bulk operations endanger availability). And note the hard limit: rewrapping your copy does nothing for ciphertext an adversary already captured — that exposure was priced at the moment of transmission.

The next chapter moves from the cryptographic application to the **scientific-computing** application: quantum chemistry, condensed-matter simulation, lattice gauge theory, and Hamiltonian-simulation-based PDE solvers. Where cryptography is the application most threatened by quantum computing, scientific computing is the application where quantum computing was conceived (Feynman 1982) and where its first practical impact is likely to materialize.

**Sanity checks before moving on.**

1. AES-128 offers $2^{128}$ ideal brute-force key-search security classically. What is its post-quantum security against an idealized Grover attack (in queries)? Against the concrete Grassl–Roetteler resource estimate (give the order of magnitude in Toffoli operations)?
2. RSA-3072 and ECDSA over a 256-bit curve are roughly equiweak classically. Which is generally estimated to fall *cheaper* to Shor, and why (think group arithmetic, not a memorized ratio)? What would you need to fix — architecture, error rate, runtime target, matched classical security — before quoting any quantitative factor?
3. The HNDL threat sets the migration deadline for confidentiality primitives to the present. Explain why the migration deadline for signature primitives is less urgent (no need to compute, just write the one-sentence reason).
4. A BB84 deployment over 200 km of standard single-mode fiber at 0.2 dB/km has a one-way photon transmission of $\eta = 10^{-4}$. Use the PLOB bound (rate $\lesssim \eta / \ln 2$ bits per channel use) to estimate the maximum key rate the bound permits at a 1 GHz pulse rate — and say why an implementation sustains far less (the bound is a pure-loss capacity per mode; detector efficiency, sifting, reconciliation, and finite-key costs all subtract).
5. NIST FIPS 203 standardizes ML-KEM (formerly Kyber). What hardness assumption underlies it, what is the structural reason that problem is believed to resist quantum attacks, and why is "no known attack" evidence rather than proof (one-sentence answers, no derivations)?
