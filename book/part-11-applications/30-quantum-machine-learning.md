# Chapter 30. Quantum Machine Learning

> **Status:** prereviewed · **Phase:** 5 · **Sections drafted:** 9 / 9

[← Previous: Chapter 29](29-optimization-finance-and-industrial.md) · [Table of Contents](../../README.md) · [Next: Chapter 31 →](31-quantum-sensing-metrology-and-tomography.md)

Quantum machine learning (QML) is, in 2026, the most over-promised and least-delivered application area in quantum computing. The phrase covers three nearly unrelated research programs that share little except the words "quantum" and "learning": running classical machine-learning workloads on quantum hardware, using machine-learning techniques to characterize quantum systems, and training parameterized quantum circuits as if they were neural networks. The first program has been gutted by a series of dequantization results — classical algorithms discovered to match the claimed quantum speedup, removing it — since 2018; the second is the genuine bright spot; the third is an active research area with no demonstrated advantage on any natural task. A chapter that takes QML seriously has to draw those distinctions sharply, because most of the marketing literature does not.

This chapter is therefore deliberately skeptical. It does not say "QML does not work" — there are real, defensible results, especially in the quantum-data category — but it pushes back hard on the framing that any quantum circuit with a trainable parameter is a candidate for production machine learning. The honest 2026 verdict is that QML is a research field, not a deployed technology, and the most defensible use case is learning *about* quantum systems rather than learning *with* them.

> **How to read this chapter.** §30.1 is the taxonomy that everything else hangs on; if you read only one section, read that. §§30.2–30.4 walk through the three categories in order of decreasing skepticism: classical-data ML on quantum hardware (mostly dequantized), ML applied to quantum data and devices (the genuinely useful one), and parameterized quantum circuits as neural networks (research-only). §30.5 covers quantum kernel methods specifically, because they are the one corner of category 1 with rigorous separation results. §30.6 collects the named architectures (VQCs, QCNNs, QGANs) and §30.7 treats hybrid training. §30.8 states the dequantization lesson as a general rule of thumb. §30.9 is the honest verdict and the bridge to Chapter 31.

## 30.1 Three Categories of QML

The first thing to insist on is that "quantum machine learning" is not a single field. It is at least three:

- **Category 1 — Quantum algorithms for classical ML problems.** The input is classical data (a matrix of feature vectors, a sample from a distribution), the output is a classical artifact (a regression coefficient, a class label, a generative sample), and the quantum computer is invoked as a subroutine that is supposed to outperform classical linear algebra. Quantum linear regression, quantum recommendation systems, quantum principal-component analysis, and quantum support-vector machines built on HHL (§15.5) all sit here. This is the category where the "exponential speedup" headlines come from and where the dequantization results of Tang and follow-ups have been most destructive.
- **Category 2 — Machine learning of quantum data and devices.** The input originates in a quantum system — coherent states or channels in the fullest version, and more commonly *classical records generated by quantum processes*: measurement outcomes, syndrome streams from an error-corrected memory, samples from a noisy device, expectation values from a many-body Hamiltonian. The task is to learn something *about* that quantum system — its state, its Hamiltonian, its noise model, its logical-error rate. The "machine learning" can be classical, quantum, or hybrid; what matters is the data's quantum provenance (the records themselves are usually classical bits), and that producing equivalent data classically is often — not always — expensive. This is the category where QML has genuine, defensible advantages.
- **Category 3 — Parameterized quantum circuits as neural networks.** A quantum circuit with trainable gate angles is treated as a model class, trained by gradient descent on a loss function, and benchmarked against classical neural networks. Variational quantum classifiers, quantum convolutional networks, and quantum GANs all sit here. The framework is intellectually interesting — it raises real questions about expressivity, trainability, and inductive bias — but as of this snapshot no instance of it has a demonstrated advantage on a natural task.

These three categories have different data-access models, different success criteria, and different reasons to be optimistic or pessimistic. Lumping them together as "QML" makes every discussion about the field harder than it needs to be. The rest of the chapter treats them in turn.

The familiar taxonomy of "classical/quantum data $\times$ classical/quantum algorithm" gives four cells; the three categories above *overlay* it rather than replacing it — Category 2 spans the classical-learner and quantum-learner cells, and Category 3 can take either kind of data. The QQ cell (coherent quantum data processed by a quantum learner) is thinner in practice but not empty: quantum autoencoders, coherent state classification, and channel learning live there — sometimes on the same device that produced the data.

## 30.2 Category 1: Quantum Algorithms for Classical ML

The textbook examples of Category 1 are: **quantum linear regression** (solve $A\beta = y$ for $\beta$ via HHL and read out a few coefficients or summary statistics — reading many destroys the scaling), **quantum recommendation systems** (Kerenidis–Prakash 2016: sample a recommendation from a low-rank reconstruction of a preference matrix, using a quantum data structure to access it), **quantum principal-component analysis** (Lloyd–Mohseni–Rebentrost 2014: produce a quantum state encoding top eigenvectors in polylogarithmic time, under copy-access, rank, and eigenvalue-resolution assumptions), and **quantum support-vector machines** (Rebentrost–Mohseni–Lloyd 2014: a least-squares SVM formulation solved via HHL, under kernel-matrix access and conditioning assumptions).

Each of these claimed an *exponential* speedup — polylogarithmic in the data dimension where classical algorithms are polynomial. Each of these speedups depended on the assumption that classical data could be loaded into a quantum register in time polylogarithmic in the data size. That assumption was usually packaged as a hypothetical device called **QRAM** (quantum random-access memory): a black box implementing the query $\sum_i \alpha_i |i\rangle|0\rangle \mapsto \sum_i \alpha_i |i\rangle|x_i\rangle$ for a classical dataset $\\{x_i\\}$ in time $\mathrm{polylog}(N)$ — the amplitudes come from the caller, and building and maintaining the memory itself can cost $O(N)$. No large-scale QRAM has been built. Proposed architectures (bucket-brigade and variants) face demanding fault-tolerance requirements under noise — an active technical literature — and the question of whether QRAM is achievable at scale is genuinely open, with much of the field's assessment since $\sim 2020$ leaning pessimistic.

Worse, even if QRAM existed, the speedups disappear under a fair comparison. Ewin Tang's 2018 work on the **recommendation-systems problem** showed that the Kerenidis–Prakash quantum algorithm could be *dequantized*: a classical algorithm, given an analogous classical access model (sample-and-query access: norm queries, entry queries, and length-squared sampling, typically via a preprocessed data structure), runs in time polylogarithmic in $N$ — matching the quantum algorithm's dimension dependence up to polynomial factors in the rank, condition number, and error tolerance. The claimed exponential separation reflected the mismatch of access models — a quantum algorithm with QRAM compared against a classical algorithm without an analogous access model — rather than quantum computation itself.

The follow-up program was prolific. By 2021, the Tang-style dequantization had been extended to quantum PCA, quantum SVMs, quantum linear regression in the low-rank regime, and other named Category-1 results *in their classical-data regimes* (density-matrix algorithms fed genuinely quantum input states are a different story — no classical sampling analog automatically exists). The general lesson — stated in §30.8 — is that when a quantum algorithm claims an exponential speedup over classical linear algebra and relies on a strong quantum access model, the corresponding classical algorithm under an analogous *classical* sampling-access model is very often polylogarithmic in dimension too — a heuristic with counterexamples, and the surviving polynomial dependencies can be substantially worse. The exponential gap was about the access model, not about quantum computation.

A few problems survive dequantization: those whose access model genuinely requires quantum oracle calls (an oracle theorem is not yet an application), and those whose input is a bona fide quantum state — which, by this chapter's own taxonomy, migrate into Category 2. For purely classical data the working *heuristic* in 2026 — a skeptic's default, not a theorem — is that a claimed HHL-style exponential speedup is not established until the end-to-end access models have been compared; polynomial improvements and favorable parameter regimes can survive even where the exponential story dies.

## 30.3 Category 2: Learning About Quantum Systems

Category 2 is where QML earns its keep. The defining feature is that the data originates in a quantum experiment, an error-corrected memory, or a many-body simulation — and producing equivalent data classically is often costly, though not always: stabilizer circuits, low-entanglement dynamics, and calibrated surrogate models can be efficient, so quantum provenance alone does not prove classical hardness. Learning *from* such data is a legitimate machine-learning problem, with rigorous advantages in specified access models (advantage over a naive baseline is weaker evidence).

The clearest example is **quantum state tomography**. Reconstructing a general $n$-qubit density matrix to fixed trace-distance error requires a number of measurements growing exponentially in $n$ ($\Omega(4^n)$-type lower bounds, with details depending on the metric and measurement model) — not because every Pauli observable must literally be estimated, but because a general density matrix carries $4^n - 1$ real parameters. For many tasks, however, the full density matrix is overkill: one only needs to predict a small set of properties, such as fidelities with target states or expectation values of local observables. The **classical shadows** framework of Huang–Kueng–Preskill (2020) shows that a number of shadow samples scaling as $O(\max_i \\|O_i\\|_{\mathrm{shadow}}^2 \log(M/\delta) / \varepsilon^2)$ — logarithmic in the number of target observables $M$, but carrying the accuracy $\varepsilon$, confidence $\delta$, and the observables' *shadow norm*, small for local observables and potentially exponentially large for arbitrary ones — suffices to predict $M$ linear properties of the state. Each sample consumes one fresh copy, rotated by a randomized (single-qubit or global) Clifford and measured in the computational basis. The shadow protocol is a *learning algorithm*: it consumes copies of $\rho$, processes them classically, and outputs predictions about $\rho$ that generalize to observables not seen during training.

Beyond shadows, Category 2 includes:

- **Hamiltonian learning.** Given access to a time-evolved state $e^{-iHt}|\psi_0\rangle$ for known $|\psi_0\rangle$ and chosen $t$, recover the Hamiltonian $H$ within a parameterized family (local, geometrically constrained, $k$-body). Sample-complexity bounds depend on the structure of $H$; for learning from dynamics, Huang–Tong–Fang–Su (2023) achieve Heisenberg-limited scaling in total evolution time (error falling as $1/t$ rather than the standard quantum limit's $1/\sqrt{t}$ — a metrology resource comparison, §31.2, not a quantum-versus-classical-computation one), and Haah–Kothari–Tang (2024) give optimal learning of low-intersection local Hamiltonians from Gibbs states and real-time evolutions (the companion Gibbs-state-only result is Anshu–Arunachalam–Kuwahara–Soleimanifar 2021), with the dependence on system size set by the locality structure rather than by Hilbert-space dimension.
- **Decoding quantum error correction.** Surface-code syndromes are streams of binary measurements with spatial and temporal correlations induced by the underlying noise model; classical decoders (minimum-weight perfect matching, union-find, belief-propagation) and learned decoders (recurrent or transformer models trained on simulated syndrome streams) both perform well in their regimes; learned decoders have reported improvements on biased or correlated noise where analytic decoders' assumptions break — though optimized analytic decoders can incorporate bias too, and learned decoders bring training-distribution drift and latency risks. This is a Category-2 application: the *data* is the syndrome stream from a quantum device, the *learner* is typically classical — so its value, however real, is not a quantum computational speedup.
- **Learning noise models.** Characterizing the noise channel on a device is itself a learning problem. Randomized benchmarking and gate-set tomography are the textbook tools; more recently, machine-learning-based noise characterization (e.g., learning Pauli noise channels from cycle-benchmarking data, learning crosstalk graphs) has filled in finer-grained pictures of device behavior, particularly for the crosstalk and leakage modes that simpler protocols miss.
- **Learning quantum states from few copies.** Whether a state has a particular property (low entanglement entropy, low magic, membership in a stabilizer class, low energy under some Hamiltonian) is a decision problem with sample-complexity bounds. Recent work on "shadow learning" and on PAC-style models of quantum learning establishes that various property classes are learnable from polynomial copies — under stated measurement models and distance promises, and usually answering a much weaker question than tomography — in regimes where full reconstruction would require exponentially many.

The pattern: when the *data* is genuinely quantum, the learning problem is real — and the wins are heterogeneous rather than one thing: shadows beat full tomography for specified observables, learned decoders help under selected noise, Hamiltonian-learning theorems improve selected scalings, and most of the learners involved are classical. This is the corner of QML most likely to see production deployment over the next decade — some decoders and calibration learners are already in experimental use — not as a replacement for classical ML, but as the instrumentation layer of a working quantum computer.

## 30.4 Category 3: Parameterized Quantum Circuits as Neural Networks

The third category treats a quantum circuit with trainable gate parameters as a *model class*. A parameterized circuit $U(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ applied to a fixed input state, optionally with classical pre-encoding of a data point $x$ into the circuit (a **feature map**), produces an output state from which expectation values are read out as the model's prediction. Training is by gradient descent on a loss function evaluated over a dataset, with gradients computed via the **parameter-shift rule** (§8.13) or finite differences.

The framework is broad enough to subsume variational quantum classifiers, quantum convolutional networks, quantum GANs, quantum Boltzmann machines, and others. Its appeal is expressivity: *if* $\mathsf{BQP} \ne \mathsf{BPP}$ (conjectured, not proven), polynomial-size quantum circuit families compute functions outside efficient classical computation — though turning that into a claim about what polynomial-size classical *neural networks* can represent takes additional model assumptions the folklore skips, and "wide enough" must mean polynomial-size for the claim to mean anything. And a second caveat bites regardless: the mere *existence* of a circuit representing a function is not the same as being able to *train* one to learn it from data. In practice, the picture is much less rosy.

Three obstructions dominate.

**Barren plateaus.** McClean–Boixo–Smelyanskiy–Babbush–Neven (2018) showed that for parameterized circuits random enough to approximate 2-designs, the gradient of a global cost with respect to any parameter has (near-)zero mean and variance decaying exponentially in $n$ — so typical gradients concentrate exponentially close to zero and gradient descent stalls. Subsequent work mapped the boundaries: local cost functions escape the exponential suppression for shallow circuits (with the protection eroding as depth grows), noise induces its own concentration (model-dependent, often exponential in depth), and structured architectures fare variously — quantum convolutional networks, for instance, carry trainability guarantees stemming from their locality and pooling structure, not merely from encoding the answer. The evidence-based reading: *generic* deep parameterized models have no demonstration of scalable trainability (many train fine at small sizes), while *structured* and problem-informed ones can be trainable — with the burden on each architecture to exhibit its guarantee.

**Noise-induced flatness.** On NISQ hardware (Chapter 25), the gates applied during training are themselves noisy. The composition of noisy channels drives the output state toward a fixed point — the maximally mixed state for unital noise; amplitude damping and other nonunital channels have different fixed points — at a model-dependent rate, often exponential in depth; the expectation values used as outputs concentrate near their mean and the loss surface becomes flat for reasons unrelated to the gradient-statistics argument. Empirically, training accuracy on real hardware degrades with circuit depth in ways consistent with this picture even when the noiseless circuit would have been trainable.

**No demonstrated advantage.** On tasks with meaningful classical baselines (image classification, structured-data regression — nothing at language-model scale has been seriously attempted), no Category-3 architecture has, as of this snapshot, produced a result that classical baselines do not match or beat on the same dataset under fair compute comparison. The best published results are typically on tiny, hand-crafted datasets (the canonical "moons" or "circles" toy problems, or small extracts of MNIST) where the comparison is more illustrative than competitive. The field has produced striking *negative* results — barren plateaus, noise-induced flatness, and (next door in Category 1) the dequantization of kernel speedups — but no robust positive result that has survived independent replication.

Category 3 is therefore best understood as a *research framework* for studying the expressivity and trainability of parameterized quantum models, not as a candidate production technology. The framework has pedagogical value (see §30.9), is connected to genuine questions in complexity theory, and may eventually produce an advantage on some quantum-native task. It has not done so yet.

## 30.5 Quantum Kernel Methods

Quantum kernel methods are the one corner of Category 1 with a rigorous separation result, so they deserve a section to themselves. The setup is a **feature map** $x \mapsto |\phi(x)\rangle$ that encodes a classical data point $x$ into a quantum state. The induced **quantum kernel** is the inner product of feature states:

$$
k(x, x') \;=\; |\langle\phi(x)|\phi(x')\rangle|^2.
$$

A kernel-based classifier (support-vector machine, kernel ridge regression) trained on classical data using $k$ as its kernel is a *quantum* classifier in the sense that the kernel is evaluated by a quantum circuit — typically a swap-test or Hadamard-test estimator running the feature-map circuit twice and computing an inner product — but its model class and training are otherwise classical.

The reason kernel methods are interesting in the QML setting is twofold. First, the kernel-evaluation step has no trainable circuit parameters, so the barren-plateau obstruction of §30.4 does not apply — though noise still corrupts the kernel estimates themselves: the feature-map circuit's depth and the shot budget set the estimation error, and sampling noise can break positive-semidefiniteness, requiring repair before training. Second, the kernel embeds data into an exponentially large Hilbert space — dimension alone guarantees nothing (classical RBF kernels are infinite-dimensional), but under suitable feature-map design the embedding can be hard to *evaluate* classically, which is where any advantage must come from.

The rigorous result is Liu–Arunachalam–Temme (2021): there is a *learning problem* — built around discrete logarithms in a carefully constructed group — for which a quantum kernel method learns efficiently while every efficient classical learner fails, assuming the classical hardness of the discrete logarithm. (The separation is *computational*: the classical learner is limited in computation, not necessarily starved of samples — check the theorem's exact statement before quoting a sample-complexity version.) This is the cleanest known separation for a quantum machine-learning problem. It is also, candidly, an artificial problem: the feature map is engineered around the discrete-log structure, and there is no natural dataset for which the construction applies.

For generic, naturally occurring data — image patches, protein sequences, financial time series — quantum kernels do not appear to beat well-tuned classical kernels (Gaussian RBF, learned deep kernels) on benchmarks where both have been measured. The provable advantage is real but narrow; the generic advantage is modest at best. Quantum kernel methods are still the most defensible Category-1 entry, because the kernel evaluation is shallow enough to run on near-term hardware and the framework does not depend on QRAM. They remain a research target with one provable success and many open empirical questions.

## 30.6 Variational Quantum Classifiers, QCNNs, and Quantum GANs

The named Category-3 architectures are worth knowing about, if only to understand what one is *not* getting from them.

**Variational quantum classifiers (VQCs).** Encode a data point $x$ via a feature map $U_\phi(x)$, apply a parameterized circuit $U(\boldsymbol{\theta})$, measure an observable $O$, and use the expectation $\langle\psi(x, \boldsymbol{\theta})|O|\psi(x, \boldsymbol{\theta})\rangle$ as the classifier score. Train $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ on a classical loss. The VQC is the prototype of a Category-3 model and inherits all the Category-3 obstructions: it is subject to barren plateaus when the parameterized block is deep or expressive enough to matter, to noise-induced flatness on real hardware, and to the absence of a demonstrated advantage on natural data.

**Quantum convolutional neural networks (QCNNs).** Cong–Choi–Lukin (2019) proposed a structured circuit ansatz inspired by classical CNNs: alternating layers of local "convolution" gates (acting on $k$-qubit patches) and "pooling" operations that discard half the qubits (via entangling gates and discarding, or measurement with conditioning, depending on the variant). The structure is restrictive enough to carry trainability guarantees — absence-of-plateau results under stated architectures and cost functions and has been shown to detect topological-phase order in 1D and 2D condensed-matter models — which is, notably, a Category-2 problem (learning *about* quantum systems) rather than a Category-3 problem in disguise. QCNNs may turn out to be useful, but their useful applications are in Category 2.

**Quantum generative adversarial networks (QGANs).** Lloyd–Weedbrook (2018) and Dallaire-Demers–Killoran (2018) proposed quantum analogs of GANs in which the generator, the discriminator, or both are parameterized quantum circuits. The motivating intuition was that quantum generators could sample from distributions classical generators cannot match efficiently. In practice, QGANs share the trainability problems of VQCs and add the instability problems of classical GAN training; no QGAN has produced a benchmarked advantage on a real generative task. They are interesting as a stress test of variational training (and as a probe of distributions that are quantum-easy and classical-hard, which connects them to the boson-sampling literature) but not as a production technology.

**Quantum Boltzmann machines, quantum reinforcement learning, quantum autoencoders.** A long list of further architectures has been proposed, each translating a classical ML construct into a parameterized quantum circuit. The general pattern repeats: an academically interesting model, a small-scale demonstration on toy data, no benchmarked advantage over a classical baseline, no production deployment. The reader who wants the full taxonomy can consult Cerezo–Verdon–Huang–Cincio–Coles (2022) or the *Variational Quantum Algorithms* review by Cerezo et al. (2021); for purposes of this chapter the headline is that the named architectures are research objects, not products.

## 30.7 Hybrid Quantum-Classical Training

All the Category-3 training discussed above is *hybrid*: a classical optimizer proposes circuit parameters, a quantum device evaluates the loss (and possibly the gradient via parameter-shift), and the classical optimizer updates the parameters based on the result. The hybrid loop is the same one used in VQE (§15.8) and QAOA (§15.9), and inherits the same practical issues: shot-noise on every expectation-value query, sensitivity to hardware drift between shots, and the need for a classical optimizer robust to stochastic and noisy gradient estimates.

In practice, the classical-side optimizer choices in 2026 QML experiments cluster around three families: gradient-descent variants (SGD with momentum, Adam) using parameter-shift gradients; black-box optimizers that side-step full parameter-shift sweeps — SPSA (a stochastic gradient *approximation* from two evaluations) alongside genuinely gradient-free COBYLA and Nelder–Mead — at the cost of noisier steps; and natural-gradient methods (the quantum Fisher information metric of Stokes–Izaac–Killoran–Carleo, 2020) that use the geometry of the parameterized-state manifold to precondition the updates. None of these has emerged as a universal default; the choice is problem- and hardware-dependent.

The shot budget dominates the wall-clock cost of training. A parameter-shift gradient for a model with $p$ parameters requires $2p$ expectation-value evaluations per training step; each evaluation needs enough shots to reduce the shot-noise variance below the signal scale. With $p = 100$ trainable parameters, $S = 10^4$ shots per evaluation, $K = 10^3$ training steps, and a per-shot device time of $1\\,\mathrm{ms}$, the total wall-clock is $2 p S K \cdot 10^{-3}\\,\mathrm{s} = 2 \times 10^6\\,\mathrm{s} \approx 23\\,\mathrm{days}$ — under serial shots, a single observable, and no batching, each of which is a lever in practice. This is for a toy-scale problem. Production scaling is not on the table without orders-of-magnitude improvements in either shot rate or per-shot information yield (the latter being the motivation for shadow-based estimators and classical-shadow training proposals).

One subtler point: the hybrid loop's classical step is *much* faster than its quantum step, so the optimizer can afford to do significant classical computation between quantum queries — for instance, fitting a surrogate model of the loss surface and proposing the next query point via Bayesian optimization. Several QML pipelines exploit this asymmetry; it does not solve the trainability problem, but it does shift some of the cost from the expensive quantum side to the cheap classical side.

## 30.8 The Dequantization Lesson

The dequantization program initiated by Tang's recommendation-systems result generalizes into a working rule of thumb. The rule is best stated as a question the reader should ask whenever an "exponential quantum speedup" is claimed for a classical-data problem:

**What is the quantum algorithm's data-access model, and what is the corresponding classical sampling-access model?** If the quantum algorithm assumes QRAM, or block-encoded access to a classical matrix, or any other cheap-superposition access to a classical dataset (proposals typically claim polylogarithmic, not constant, query time — and QRAM queries, amplitude preparation, and block encodings are distinct oracles with different construction costs), then the fair classical comparison is to a classical algorithm with $\ell_2$-sampling access — the ability to sample rows of the data with probability proportional to their squared norm, and entries within a row with probability proportional to their squared magnitude.

Under that *matched* classical access model, the Tang program has produced classical algorithms with running times that are polylogarithmic in the data dimension for: low-rank linear regression, low-rank matrix recovery, quantum recommendation systems, quantum PCA, quantum SVD, certain classes of quantum kernel methods, and the Lloyd–Mohseni–Rebentrost family. The polynomial dependence on rank, condition number, and accuracy tolerance is typically worse than the quantum algorithm's by a few powers, but the *exponential* gap in problem size has vanished. The quantum speedup claimed in the original paper was an artifact of comparing the quantum algorithm under a strong quantum access model to a classical algorithm under no analogous access model.

The lesson, restated as a checklist:

1. Identify the quantum algorithm's input access model precisely. ("QRAM-loaded state $\sum_i \sqrt{p_i}|i\rangle|x_i\rangle$" is a much stronger assumption than "classical matrix in RAM.")
2. Construct the matched classical access model. ($\ell_2$-sampling, or query access to entries plus row norms, depending on the structure.)
3. Look for a classical algorithm under that matched access model. If one exists at polylogarithmic dimension dependence, the quantum exponential speedup is dequantized.
4. The honest comparison is then between the (dequantized) classical polylog algorithm and the quantum polylog algorithm — typically the gap is polynomial in rank or condition number, sometimes in favor of the quantum side, often not.

The exceptions to the dequantization pattern are instructive. **Shor's algorithm** for factoring (§15.2) and the **discrete-log algorithm** (§15.4) survive for a different reason: their input is a short classical description (the integer $N$, a group element), not a large dataset — the modular exponentiation is *computed*, not looked up, so there is no data-access asymmetry to dequantize; the speedup is genuinely computational. **HHL** for genuinely quantum input states (states produced by another quantum subroutine, not loaded from classical memory) also survives — for its own reason: no classical sampling access to a coherent quantum input exists even in principle. The dequantization lesson is specifically about *classical-data* problems with hypothetical quantum data-access oracles, and it should be the first lens applied whenever such a problem comes up.

## 30.9 Honest Verdict, Pedagogical Value, and Bridge to Chapter 31

The QML field in 2026 is not where the QML field of 2014 promised it would be. The exponential speedups have mostly evaporated under dequantization; the variational quantum networks have run into barren plateaus and noise; the quantum kernels have a single artificial separation result and no compelling natural-data advantage; the QRAM assumption that underwrote half the field is still a hypothetical device. The hype cycle has peaked and is on its way down.

What is left, on a sober reading:

- Category 2 — learning *about* quantum systems — is genuinely useful and is on the path to production. Classical shadows, learned decoders, and calibration/noise-model learners are real tools — some already in experimental device-team use — while Hamiltonian-learning theory is still crossing from theorem to routine practice; the trend line is positive.
- Quantum kernel methods are the best Category-1 entry: a real provable separation on an artificial problem, a clean trainability story (no plateau because there is nothing to train), and a near-term implementation cost. They are not yet beating classical kernels on natural data, but they are not embarrassing either.
- Category 3 is research. It is interesting research with real connections to complexity theory and to the expressivity of quantum circuits, but it is not a production technology, and the empirical record on natural data is thin.

The pedagogical value of QML is worth flagging even when the production value is low. Parameterized quantum circuits are an excellent framework for thinking about circuit expressivity, about the geometry of parameterized-state manifolds, about the interplay between noise and trainability, and about the limits of variational methods. A reader who works through a QML construction — defining a feature map, computing parameter-shift gradients, hitting a barren plateau, redesigning the ansatz — has internalized much of what one needs to know about variational quantum algorithms in general, including the ones (VQE, QAOA) that have more legitimate near-term applications. QML is a good *teaching* topic even when it is a poor *deployment* topic.

The most defensible single sentence about QML in 2026 is: **the most reliable thing a quantum computer can learn is something about itself, or about another quantum system.** Production machine learning for classical data is, with the partial exception of artificial-problem quantum kernels, not on the table this decade.

Chapter 31 turns to the closely related but cleaner subject of **quantum sensing, metrology, and tomography.** Where QML asked "can a quantum computer learn a classical pattern?" and got an ambiguous answer, sensing and metrology ask "can a quantum system measure a physical quantity better than a classical system?" — and the answer there is yes, with rigorous quantum-Cramér–Rao bounds, with deployed atomic clocks and gravimeters, and with a much firmer foundation. The Category-2 spirit of this chapter — learning *about* quantum and physical systems with quantum tools — is the spirit of all of Chapter 31.

**Sanity checks before moving on.**

1. State, in your own words, the difference between Category 1 and Category 2 QML, and give one example of a problem in each. Then explain why "training a parameterized quantum circuit on MNIST" sits in Category 3 and not Category 1.
2. Tang's dequantization of quantum recommendation systems shows that the classical algorithm runs in time polylogarithmic in $N$ *under what access model?* Why is the original "exponential quantum speedup" claim still technically correct under its original access model, and why is it nevertheless considered overturned?
3. Estimate the wall-clock time for a hybrid VQC training run with $p = 50$ parameters, $S = 5 \times 10^3$ shots per expectation value, $K = 500$ optimizer steps, and a $1\\,\mathrm{ms}$ per-shot device time. Use parameter-shift gradients (so $2p$ evaluations per step). Identify the dominant cost driver and one practical lever to reduce it.
4. The Liu–Arunachalam–Temme (2021) separation for quantum kernel methods is based on a problem encoded around discrete logarithms. Explain why the discrete-log hardness assumption is doing the work, whether the separation is computational or sample-theoretic (check the theorem's statement), and why the construction supplies no guarantee for a natural dataset like image classification — which is different from proving no advantage exists there.
5. A vendor claims a "quantum exponential speedup" for fraud detection on a tabular bank-transaction dataset, citing a quantum kernel SVM. Identify at least three questions you would ask to assess the claim — at least one of which should be about data-access model and at least one of which should be about whether the comparison is to a *dequantized* classical baseline rather than a naive one.
